Chapter 2

Temperature, Heat, and the Restless Atmosphere

A weather report may say it is 22°C (72°F). That number tells you how warm the air is right now. It does not tell you where the warmth came from, where it is going, or what it will do next.

Those are the questions that make weather.

Chapter 1 laid out the weather machine: the Sun heats Earth unevenly, warm air rises, and air flows to even things out. This chapter opens the machine and looks at its moving parts. Why does the beach cool off on a summer afternoon while the parking lot behind it bakes? Why is the coldest moment of the night often just after sunrise? And why does one bubble of warm air stop a few hundred meters up, while another climbs into a thunderstorm?

The answers all come back to heat on the move. It crosses space as light, passes from ground to air by touch, and rides along with the wind. Land and water take it in at very different speeds. And air that rises cools, even when no heat leaves it at all.

The atmosphere is restless because heat never stays put.

2.1 Temperature Is Not Heat

Temperature and heat sound like the same thing. They are not.

As Chapter 1 showed, temperature measures how fast molecules are moving. It tells you how hot something is, not how much energy it holds. Heat is energy on the move. It always flows from something warmer to something cooler.

A cup of boiling water is far hotter than a warm lake. But the lake holds vastly more energy, because it holds vastly more water. Pour the cup into the lake, and the lake hardly notices.

Materials also differ in how much heat it takes to warm them. Water is especially stubborn. It needs several times more energy than dry soil or rock to warm by the same amount. This is its heat capacity, and it will matter a great deal when we reach the coast.

For weather, the lesson is simple. Oceans, soil, pavement, forests, and the air itself all take in and give up heat at different rates. Those differences, more than any single temperature, keep the atmosphere moving.

2.2 Sunlight In, Heat Out

The Sun warms Earth from 150 million kilometers (93 million miles) away, across empty space.

It does this by radiation: energy that travels as waves, like light, and needs nothing to travel through. Most sunlight passes straight through clear air, the way light passes through a shallow pond to brighten its floor. What stops it is the bottom of the ocean of air: the ground and the sea. They absorb the sunlight and warm up, and, as Chapter 1 explained, they warm the air from below.

Not every surface takes the same share. The fraction of sunlight a surface reflects is its albedo. Fresh snow reflects most of the light that strikes it, which is why a snowfield can stay frozen under a blazing sky. Dark pavement and open ocean absorb most of it.

Anything warm also gives off radiation of its own. The ground, the sea, clouds, and you are all glowing right now, in light the eye cannot see. This is infrared radiation, the warmth you feel on your face from a campfire.

So the ground plays a constant game of in and out. By day, sunlight pours in faster than infrared leaks out, and the ground warms. At night, the sunlight stops but the infrared keeps going, and the ground cools. Water vapor, carbon dioxide, and clouds catch some of that outgoing infrared and send part of it back down. Without them, the bottom of the ocean of air would be far colder.

Weather starts with this balance between energy in and energy out.

2.3 Heat by Touch, Heat by Travel

Once the ground is warm, it hands its heat to the air in three ways.

The first is conduction, heat passed by direct contact. Fast-jiggling molecules in a sunny sidewalk bump into the air molecules touching it and speed them up. Air is a poor conductor, so conduction warms only a thin layer right at the surface.

At night, conduction runs in reverse. On a calm, clear night, the cooling ground can chill the air touching it below freezing while the air at head height stays a few degrees above. That is how frost forms on the lawn when the porch thermometer says it shouldn't.

The second is convection, from Chapter 1. Conduction warms the bottom layer, and convection lifts it away. On a sunny day, rising columns of warm air called thermals carry heat hundreds or thousands of meters up. Hawks and glider pilots ride them for free.

The third is advection, heat carried sideways by the wind. A wind from the tropics brings warmth. A north wind behind a cold front can drop the temperature by 10°C (18°F) in an hour. None of this needs the Sun to change. The air simply arrives already warm or already cold.

Convection moves heat up and down. Advection moves it across, like a current in the ocean of air.

2.4 Land Heats Fast, Water Heats Slowly

Spend a summer day at the beach and you can feel it with your feet. By noon the sand is too hot to walk on, but the water is still cool. After dark the sand turns cold, and the water feels almost warm.

Water resists changing temperature for four reasons.

Water mixes. Waves and currents stir the warm surface down into cooler water below. Solid ground can't stir itself.

Sunlight soaks into water. It reaches several meters down, warming a thick layer a little instead of a thin layer a lot. On land, sunlight stops in the top few centimeters, and that thin skin gets very hot.

Water is stubborn. Its high heat capacity means it takes far more energy to warm.

And water spends energy on evaporation. Some of the sunlight falling on the sea goes into making vapor instead of warming the water, and it is carried off as latent heat, the hidden fuel from Chapter 1.

So land swings fast and far, and water holds steady. Coastal towns have milder summers and milder winters than inland towns at the same latitude. Chapter 14 follows that story through the seasons.

2.5 Sea Breezes and Land Breezes

Put fast-warming land next to slow-warming water, and you get a wind.

On a sunny morning, the land heats up faster than the sea. Air over the land warms and rises, and cooler, heavier air from over the water slides in underneath to take its place. That inflow is a sea breeze. High above, the risen air drifts back out to sea and sinks, closing the loop. It is Chapter 1's convection loop, lying on its side across the shoreline.

A sea breeze often arrives in late morning or early afternoon. The air turns cooler and damper, and the temperature can drop several degrees in a few minutes.

Where the sea air pushes inland, it shoves the warm land air upward, and a line of clouds often grows along its leading edge. On the Florida peninsula, sea breezes from both coasts meet in the middle on summer afternoons, and thunderstorms erupt where they collide.

At night the loop reverses. The land cools faster than the water, so now the air over the water is warmer, and it rises. Cool air flows off the land and out to sea. This land breeze is usually gentler, because the difference in temperature is smaller at night.

Large lakes make the same winds, on a smaller scale.

2.6 The Daily Temperature Cycle

The hottest time of day is not noon, and the coldest is not midnight.

Temperature rises as long as more energy comes in than goes out. Think of a bathtub with the tap running and the drain open. The water keeps rising as long as the tap pours faster than the drain empties, even after you start turning the tap down.

The Sun is highest at midday, but the ground keeps gaining more than it loses for hours afterward. So the warmest part of the day usually comes in mid- to late afternoon. After sunset the ground cools all night, and it is still losing a little more than it gains when the Sun first rises. So the coldest moment usually comes around sunrise, or just after.

Local conditions bend that curve. Clouds flatten it, as Chapter 1 noted, shading the day and holding in warmth at night. Wind flattens it too, by stirring the air so that no cold layer settles at night. Humid nights stay warm, because water vapor catches outgoing infrared.

Deserts have the opposite: dry air, bare ground, and few clouds. A desert can bake in the afternoon and drop 20°C (36°F) by dawn. Fresh snow sharpens the cold even more. It reflects sunlight by day and shields the surface from the warmer ground beneath, so a clear, calm night over new snow brings some of the coldest temperatures of the year.

And at any hour, the wind can override everything. A cold front arriving at 2 p.m. can make that the warmest moment of the day.

The same lag happens over a year: the longest day comes in June in the Northern Hemisphere, but the hottest weeks come later. Chapter 14 explains why.

2.7 Rising Air Cools

Air that rises cools down, even if nothing takes its heat away.

Chapter 1 showed that air pressure falls as you climb. Imagine a bubble of air, what meteorologists call a parcel, rising from the ground. As it climbs into thinner air, less pressure squeezes it, so it expands. Expanding takes energy, and the only energy the parcel has is the motion of its own molecules. They slow down, and the parcel cools.

Sinking air does the opposite. It is squeezed as it descends, and it warms. You can feel both: a bicycle pump warms as you push, and a spray can grows cold as the gas rushes out. Scientists call this an adiabatic change, meaning no heat is added or taken away.

The numbers are steady enough to plan by. A parcel of clear air cools by about 10°C for every kilometer it climbs (about 5.5°F per 1,000 feet). This is the dry rate. "Dry" just means no cloud has formed yet.

Once the parcel cools enough, its vapor condenses into a cloud, as Chapter 3 will show. Condensing vapor releases latent heat, so from then on the parcel cools only about half as fast. This is the moist rate.

This one idea explains a surprising amount of weather. Nearly every cloud forms in air that is rising and cooling. Sinking air warms and dries, which is why high pressure brings clear skies. And air that pours down a mountainside arrives warmer and drier than when it started up the other side. Chapter 5 tells the story of those winds.

2.8 Stable and Unstable Air

Give a bubble of air a push upward. Does it keep going, or fall back?

It depends on how its temperature compares with the air around it at its new height. If the parcel is warmer than its neighbors, it is lighter, and it keeps rising. The air is unstable. If it is colder, it sinks back. The air is stable. Think of a boat tipped by a wave. A stable boat rocks back upright. An unstable one keeps rolling.

What decides the answer is how fast the surrounding air cools with height. On average it cools about 6.5°C per kilometer (3.6°F per 1,000 feet), but that rate changes from day to day and hour to hour. When the surrounding air cools slowly with height, a rising parcel soon finds itself colder than its neighbors and sinks back. When the surrounding air cools quickly, because the ground is hot or the air high above is unusually cold, the parcel stays warmer and keeps climbing.

Much of the time the air sits in between. It is stable for clear air, but once a cloud forms and the parcel switches to the slower moist rate, it turns warmer than its surroundings and takes off. On many summer days, the air resists rising until something lifts it to the cloud base. Then the clouds suddenly tower. Chapter 7 shows how forecasters measure this hidden switch.

You can read stability in the sky. Stable air makes flat, layered clouds, fog, haze, and steady drizzle. Unstable air makes heaped-up clouds, gusty winds, bumpy flights, and showers.

That is the answer Chapter 1 promised. Whether a rising bubble stops early or keeps climbing is the difference between a quiet afternoon and a thunderstorm.

2.9 Inversions: A Lid Near the Ground

Sometimes the air gets warmer as you climb.

This is a temperature inversion, the most stable air there is. A rising bubble quickly finds itself colder than the warm layer above and sinks back, so an inversion works like a lid. Chapter 1 described the biggest one, the stratosphere, which caps all our weather. Inversions near the ground are smaller lids, and they shape our mornings.

The most common forms on clear, calm nights. The ground radiates its heat to space and chills the air touching it, and by dawn a cold layer lies along the bottom. The morning sun usually breaks it up within a few hours.

In hilly country, the cold air is heavy, so it drains downhill and pools in valleys and hollows, the way water collects in the lowest places. Valley floors get frost and fog while the slopes above stay milder. Fruit growers plant their orchards partway up the hillside for exactly this reason.

Inversions also form higher up, where air is sinking inside a high-pressure system. The sinking air is squeezed and warmed, and it settles like a lid over cooler air near the ground, sometimes for days.

Whatever forms it, a lid traps cold air, fog, smoke, and pollution. Chimney smoke rises a little, hits the lid, and spreads out flat. In basins such as Los Angeles and Salt Lake City, a stubborn inversion can hold smog in place until a new weather system sweeps it away.

Under the lid, the bottom of the ocean of air goes stagnant.

2.10 Cities Run Hot

A city makes its own climate, and it runs warm.

On a calm, clear night, the heart of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the fields outside it, sometimes as much as 10°C (18°F). This is the urban heat island.

Almost every part of a city adds to it. Dark roofs and pavement soak up sunlight. Concrete and brick store that heat by day and release it slowly at night. Tall buildings trap outgoing infrared and block the breezes that might carry heat away. There is little soil or greenery to cool the air by evaporation. And engines and air conditioners add heat of their own.

The difference is strongest at night, when the countryside cools quickly and the city cannot. That matters most in a heat wave. When nights stay hot, the body never gets a chance to recover, which is why heat waves are deadliest in cities. Chapter 11 returns to that danger.

Trees, parks, and light-colored roofs and pavements all help a city cool itself.

2.11 How Temperature Feels

Your body is not a thermometer.

You make your own heat, and you keep a thin layer of warm air against your skin. Wind strips that layer away, and you lose heat faster. That is wind chill. On a day of −10°C (14°F), a stiff breeze can make it feel like −20°C (−4°F). But wind chill does not make the air colder. A car parked in the wind cools to the air temperature faster, but never below it.

In hot weather, the trouble runs the other way. Your body cools itself by sweating, and evaporation carries heat away, as the shivering swimmer in Chapter 1 discovered. In humid air, sweat evaporates slowly, so you cool less. The heat index combines temperature and humidity into a "feels like" number. At 32°C (90°F) in very humid air, it can feel like 41°C (105°F). Chapter 3 explains humidity itself.

Both numbers describe stress on the body, not the air, and both can be dangerous. Chapters 10 and 11 cover how to stay safe.

Field Observation

Track a Day's Temperature

Pick a day with settled weather. Read the temperature every hour from early morning to evening, and note the clouds and wind each time.

Keep the thermometer in the shade, about head height, over grass and away from walls. A thermometer in the sun measures the thermometer, not the air. Chapter 18 explains how to set one up properly.

Plot your readings with time along the bottom and temperature up the side. Look for the morning low, the afternoon high, and any dips when a cloud passes or the wind shifts.

You are watching the balance between energy in and energy out.

Try It

Race Land Against Water

You will need: two identical containers, dry soil or sand, water, two thermometers, and a sunny spot or a desk lamp.

Fill one container with soil and the other with water. Set a thermometer just below the surface of each. Put both in the sun and read them every five minutes for half an hour. Then move both into the shade and keep reading.

The soil warms faster, gets hotter, and cools faster. The water barely moves.

You have built a sea breeze's engine on a tabletop.

Try It

Find a Lid

On a clear, calm morning, compare the temperature in a low spot, such as a valley floor or a hollow, with a nearby slope.

The low spot may be colder, even though it sits lower. Look for frost in the hollows, fog lying in the valley, and smoke spreading flat instead of rising.

Stay on public paths and off the radio towers. The inversion will still be there tomorrow.

Chapter Summary

Temperature measures how hot something is. Heat is energy flowing from warmer to cooler.

Sunlight warms the ground and sea, which warm the air from below. Everything warm gives off infrared, and the balance of energy in and out decides whether a place warms or cools.

Heat reaches the air by conduction, rises with convection, and travels sideways on the wind.

Land warms and cools quickly, and water slowly. That difference drives sea and land breezes.

The day's warmest hour comes in the afternoon and its coldest around sunrise, because temperature follows the energy balance, not the height of the Sun.

Rising air expands and cools. Sinking air is squeezed and warms. Stability decides whether a rising bubble keeps going.

Inversions put a lid on the air near the ground. Cities run warmer than the countryside, especially at night.

The atmosphere is restless because heat never stays put.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Heat: Energy flowing from something warmer to something cooler. Heat capacity: How much heat it takes to warm a material. Radiation: Energy that travels as waves, like sunlight. Infrared: Invisible radiation given off by anything warm. Albedo: The share of sunlight a surface reflects. Conduction: Heat passed by direct contact. Advection: Heat or cold carried sideways by the wind. Sea breeze: A daytime wind from the water onto the land. Land breeze: A nighttime wind from the land out over the water. Parcel: A bubble of air imagined moving on its own. Adiabatic: Temperature change caused only by air expanding or being squeezed. Stable and unstable air: Air that pushes a rising parcel back down, or lets it keep climbing. Temperature inversion: A layer where the air gets warmer with height. Urban heat island: A city that stays warmer than the countryside around it. Wind chill and heat index: How cold or hot it feels, once wind or humidity is counted.
