Chapter 16

Weather Records, Extremes, and How Meteorologists Know a Record Is Real

For ninety years, the hottest place ever measured on Earth was a hilltop fort at El Azizia, in Libya. On September 13, 1922, a thermometer there read 58.0°C (136.4°F). The number went into every almanac and every textbook.

In 2010, a panel of the World Meteorological Organization, the WMO, decided to look again. One of its members, the Libyan meteorologist Khalid El Fadli, tracked down the original logbook. Then, in 2011, revolution broke out in Libya, and for about six months the rest of the panel heard nothing from him. His first email came in August, as fighting closed in on Tripoli. He had kept working.

The logbook told the story. Two days before the famous reading, a new and untrained observer had taken over. The handwriting changed, and highs and lows began landing in the wrong columns. The station's usual thermometer had been replaced with a cheaper kind, and the panel concluded that the new observer had most likely read it wrong, by about 7°C. The fort itself sat on dark, sun-baked ground that ran hot. Nearby stations showed nothing like it.

On September 13, 2012, ninety years to the day after the reading, the WMO threw the record out.

A weather record is not just an impressive number. It is a number that has survived questions. This chapter is about those questions, and about the strange and wonderful extremes that passed them.

16.1 A Record Needs a Label

On July 4, 1956, a thunderstorm over Unionville, Maryland, dropped 31.2 millimeters (1.23 inches) of rain in a single minute. On June 22, 1947, a storm over Holt, Missouri, dropped 305 millimeters (12 inches) in less than an hour. And on January 7 and 8, 1966, as a tropical cyclone crossed the island of Réunion in the Indian Ocean, a mountain spot called Foc-Foc caught 1,825 millimeters (about 72 inches) in a single day.

Which is the world's heaviest rain? All three are. Each holds a different record, because each was measured over a different stretch of time.

Every record needs a label with three parts. What was measured: rain, snow, a gust, a temperature, a hailstone. Where: one station, one city, one country, or the whole planet. And how long: one minute, one hour, a day, a season, all time. Change any part of the label, and you are in a different drawer of the record book.

Even "a day" needs care. A storm that rains from noon to noon may be split across two calendar days, and neither day may look remarkable, while the heaviest 24 hours inside the storm sets a record. The rain didn't divide itself. The calendar did.

Place needs the same care. A city may have a downtown station, an airport station, and a university station, each with its own history. A record at one is not automatically a record for the city.

16.2 Record or Extreme?

The hurricane that struck Galveston, Texas, in September 1900 was not the strongest storm on record. Its winds were nowhere near the top of the list. But its surge of about 4.6 meters (15 feet) swept over a city with no seawall and little warning, and at least 8,000 people died (Chapter 9). It is still the deadliest natural disaster in American history.

That is the difference between a record and an extreme. A record is the highest or lowest verified value in a defined set of measurements. An extreme is weather severe enough to do harm, whether or not it breaks a record. Harm depends on who is in the way, how prepared they are, and how long the weather lasts. Rank is only one part of it.

Some of the most important records aren't the flashy ones. When heat settled over Chicago in July 1995, the afternoons were brutal, but it was the nights that killed. The city never cooled off, and more than 700 people died (Chapter 11). Meteorologists keep track of the record high minimum, the warmest low temperature, because a night that gives no relief is often more dangerous than a hotter afternoon followed by a cool evening. Its opposite, the record low maximum, the coldest high, marks a day that stayed bitter from morning to dusk.

Length of record matters too. A station that has run for twelve years and one that has run for 140 years can both report an "all-time high." Both are true. They answer different questions.

16.3 The Thermometer in the Shade

With El Azizia gone, the world record for heat passed to a ranch in Death Valley, California. On July 10, 1913, a thermometer at Greenland Ranch, now called Furnace Creek, read 56.7°C (134°F). The thermometer's scale stopped at 135°F. It had nearly run out of numbers.

That record still stands, but not without argument. Some climatologists doubt it, and the WMO's own notes suggest a sandstorm may have blown superheated sand against the shelter. A record can be official and still debated.

Most arguments about temperature records come down to one word: exposure, meaning what surrounds the thermometer. The goal is to measure the air, not the sunshine. So an official thermometer sits in shade, inside a ventilated white shelter, about a meter and a half (5 feet) above open grass, well away from walls, pavement, and machinery. Chapter 18 explains how those shelters work.

Put a thermometer in sunlight, or beside a brick wall, or over a parking lot, and it will happily report a heat wave that the air never had. The car dashboard reading 50°C in a sunny lot is telling the truth about the car. It is not telling the truth about the weather.

Even a well-placed station can drift. Trees grow and throw shade. A parking lot or a warehouse goes in next door. A town grows up around an airport and warms its nights (Chapter 2, 2.10). A station that was excellent when it was built can be compromised fifty years later without anyone moving the thermometer at all.

16.4 The Coldest Air and the Coldest Snow

On July 21, 1983, in the middle of the Antarctic winter, the thermometer at Russia's Vostok Station, more than 3,400 meters (11,000 feet) up on the ice sheet, fell to −89.2°C (−128.6°F). It is the lowest air temperature ever measured by a weather station.

In 2018, scientists announced something colder. Satellites looking down at the high plateau of East Antarctica, in winters from 2004 to 2016, had found shallow hollows in the snow reaching about −98°C (−144°F).

So why is Vostok still the record? Because the two numbers measure different things. The satellites measured the surface of the snow, which radiates its heat to the clear night sky and can grow colder than the air above it. Vostok measured the air, in a shelter, at standard height. Both are real. They sit in different drawers, and the label decides which drawer.

The coldest records also make a point about speed. On January 23 and 24, 1916, the temperature at Browning, Montana, fell from 7°C (44°F) to −49°C (−56°F) in a single day, as an Arctic air mass slid down the face of the Rockies (Chapter 6). That drop of 56°C (100°F) in 24 hours is the U.S. record. A number like that is believable when the rest of the weather agrees with it, here a sharp front and a deep, dense pool of Arctic air. A record that fits the weather map is far easier to trust than one that stands alone.

16.5 Snow, the Hardest Thing to Measure

On January 11 and 12, 1997, a volunteer weather observer in Montague, New York, on the snowy Tug Hill Plateau east of Lake Ontario, measured 196 centimeters (77 inches) of new snow in 24 hours. It looked like a new national record, beating the 192 centimeters (75.8 inches) that fell at Silver Lake, Colorado, on April 14 and 15, 1921.

A committee of the National Weather Service looked into it. The problem was the measuring board. The standard is to measure new snow on a flat board and clear it no more often than every six hours, four times a day. The Montague observer had measured and cleared it five times. Fresh snow is fluffy, and it settles under its own weight. Clear the board more often, and you catch more of the fluff before it settles, so the total comes out higher. The committee would not accept the number. Silver Lake kept the record.

That may seem harsh. But it shows how snow records work. Snow drifts, settles, melts, and blows away. One yard can hold bare grass, ankle-deep snow, and a drift taller than a person. So the rules about where, how often, and how to measure are the only way to compare one storm with another.

The rules also separate two things the news often mixes up. Snowfall is how much falls. Snow depth is how much is lying on the ground at one moment. A storm can drop 28 centimeters, settle and melt to 18, and leave you with less on the ground than fell from the sky. A record report has to say which it means.

Lake-effect bands make all of this harder. In Buffalo in November 2014, one suburb got 165 centimeters while the airport, a few kilometers away, got 16 (Chapter 10). Both were right.

16.6 The Gust Nobody Noticed

On April 12, 1934, observers on the summit of Mount Washington, New Hampshire, recorded a gust of 372 km/h (231 mph). For 62 years it was the fastest wind ever measured on Earth.

Then, on April 10, 1996, Tropical Cyclone Olivia crossed Barrow Island, off the coast of Western Australia. An anemometer on the island, 10 meters (33 feet) above the ground, caught a gust of 408 km/h (253 mph). Almost no one noticed. The instrument belonged to a private company, not the national weather service, so no one followed up. In 2009, a WMO committee member remembered the reading, the committee reviewed the instrument and its data, and in January 2010 the WMO named it the new world record.

Wind records need their own labels. A gust is a burst lasting a few seconds. A sustained wind is an average over a longer stretch, one minute in the United States and ten minutes in most other countries. The Barrow Island number was a three-second gust. Hurricane Patricia's 345 km/h (215 mph) in 2015 was a one-minute sustained wind (Chapter 9). Comparing them is like comparing a sprinter's top speed with a marathon pace.

Height and surroundings matter too. The standard is 10 meters above open ground. A gust on a ridge, a rooftop, or between two tall buildings can be real and still not count.

And extreme wind often breaks the very instruments trying to measure it. A station that reports 225 km/h and then goes silent has not proven the wind topped out at 225. It has proven the wind reached at least that much while the instrument was still there to say so. The fastest winds of all are inside tornadoes, measured not by anemometers but by radar, a little above the ground (Chapter 8).

16.7 The Highest and Lowest Air Pressure

On October 12, 1979, a U.S. Air Force reconnaissance plane flew into the eye of Typhoon Tip, over the open western Pacific. It dropped an instrument on a parachute down through the eye, and on the way to the sea it measured 870 hectopascals. That is still the lowest air pressure ever measured at sea level.

The opposite record comes from the cold heart of Siberia. On December 31, 1968, at Agata, a station in a deep pool of frigid, heavy air, the pressure reached 1,083.8 hectopascals.

Pressure records come with a catch, because pressure falls as you climb (Chapter 5). A mountain station always reads lower than a beach. To compare them, meteorologists calculate what the pressure would be if the station sat at sea level. This is the sea-level pressure printed on weather maps. The actual reading at the station is the station pressure.

For a station near the coast, the calculation is small and reliable. For a station high in the mountains, it means imagining a column of air hundreds of meters deep that isn't really there, and its temperature has to be guessed. In cold weather, the guess can push the result far too high. That is why the WMO now keeps two records for high pressure. Agata holds the one for stations below 750 meters. Tosontsengel, Mongolia, at about 1,725 meters, holds the one above, with 1,089.1 hectopascals on December 30, 2004, and the record book marks it with its elevation for a reason.

Tip's number has its own fine print. A hurricane's lowest pressure may fall between two passes of the plane. The reported value is the best estimate from the measurements, not a number engraved in the eye.

16.8 The Hailstone in the Freezer

On the evening of July 23, 2010, a storm over Vivian, South Dakota, dropped enormous hail. Les Scott, a local resident, gathered the biggest stone he could find and put it in his freezer. Then the storm knocked out the power, and the stone began to melt.

When the National Weather Service came to measure it the next day, the stone was still 20 centimeters (8.0 inches) across and 47.3 centimeters (18.6 inches) around. A few days later, sealed in a plastic bag, it was weighed on a post office scale: 0.88 kilograms (1 pound 15 ounces). A national committee voted that it was the largest hailstone ever found in the United States, both by diameter and by weight. It had been bigger still before the freezer failed.

But it did not take every record. A stone that fell in Aurora, Nebraska, on June 22, 2003, was flatter and more lopsided, and its circumference, 47.6 centimeters (18.75 inches), was larger. So the Vivian stone is the widest and heaviest, and the Aurora stone is the longest around. Hail grows in lumpy, irregular layers (Chapter 7), so one stone rarely wins every category.

The world's heaviest hailstone fell in the Gopalganj district of Bangladesh on April 14, 1986: 1.02 kilograms (2.25 pounds).

Hail is hard to verify because it vanishes. It melts, breaks on impact, and gets compared to fruit and sports balls in excited reports. A photo without a ruler is a story, not a measurement. The best evidence is the stone itself, measured with calipers, weighed on a tested scale, and kept frozen in a sealed bag.

16.9 Lightning Seen From Space

On April 29, 2020, a single bolt of lightning stretched 768 kilometers (477 miles) across the sky, from Mississippi through Louisiana into Texas. Weeks later, on June 18, 2020, a flash over Uruguay and northern Argentina flickered on for 17.1 seconds.

Nobody on the ground could have measured either one. From the ground, you see only a piece of a flash. These were found by satellites in orbit carrying lightning mappers, cameras that watch for flashes across a large part of the globe at once. The WMO certified both records in 2022 and gave these giant flashes a name: megaflashes.

Then, in 2025, the WMO certified an even longer one, 829 kilometers (515 miles), in a storm complex that stretched from eastern Texas to near Kansas City. It had happened years earlier. It was found only when scientists went back and re-examined the stored satellite data. Some records are set the day they happen. Others wait in the archive for someone to look.

Megaflashes grow in huge, long-lived thunderstorm systems over flat land, such as the Great Plains and the plains of South America, where electric charge spreads across enormous layers of cloud (Chapter 7, 7.10).

Lightning also holds a grimmer record. On November 2, 1994, lightning struck fuel tanks near the town of Dronka, Egypt, during a storm that was also flooding the area. Burning fuel rode the floodwater into the town, and 469 people died, the deadliest lightning disaster on record.

16.10 Accepted and Rejected

In February 2020, two Antarctic stations reported record warmth within three days of each other. On February 6, Argentina's Esperanza base, at the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula, reached 18.3°C (64.9°F). On February 9, an automatic station on nearby Seymour Island reached 20.75°C (69.4°F).

The WMO checked both. Esperanza passed: the station and its instruments met the standards, and the weather that day could explain the warmth. Seymour Island failed. Its sensor sat under an improvised sun shield that let it heat up, so its reading could not count. One record went into the book, and one did not.

Two years later, on July 19, 2022, the thermometer at Coningsby, in Lincolnshire, reached 40.3°C (104.5°F), the first time Britain had ever topped 40°C. The UK's Met Office inspected the sites, tested the equipment, and compared station against station before confirming it nine days later. In the same review, it rejected a provisional Scottish record from Floors Castle, because that station's setup did not meet the standards.

Every review asks the same handful of questions. Was the instrument working and tested? Was it placed properly? Do neighboring stations, radar, satellites, and the weather map support it? What does the station's paperwork say? That paperwork is called metadata, information about the measurement itself: where the station stood, at what height, with which instrument, who read it, and when anything changed. A number says what was recorded. Metadata says what the number means.

Automated checks also raise a flag on any reading that looks impossible, jumps suddenly, or disagrees with its neighbors. A flag doesn't mean the reading is wrong. Extremes trigger flags exactly because they look like errors. That is why the next step is people, and why a new record may stay provisional for weeks or months before it is certified.

16.11 Rescuing Old Weather

In October 2010, a website called Old Weather invited anyone with a computer to read the logbooks of Royal Navy ships from the First World War. Every few hours, those crews had written down the ship's position, the wind, the weather, the air pressure, and the temperature. By mid-2012, more than 16,000 volunteers had typed in more than a million pages, and the project later moved on to old American ships in the Arctic.

Those numbers now help rebuild the weather of a century ago. A reanalysis takes a modern weather model and feeds it every old observation that can be found, from ships, stations, and balloons, to reconstruct the whole atmosphere, day by day, for decades. It can map a storm that no one saw whole. But it is a model held in place by measurements, not a measurement itself, and where old observations are scarce, especially over the oceans before satellites, its smooth lines hide a lot of guessing.

Old station records need repair too. Over a century, a station may move across town, switch from glass thermometers to electronic sensors, swap shelters, or change the hour of its daily reading. Each change can shift its numbers a little. Even the clock matters: an observer who resets the thermometer late in the afternoon can count one hot afternoon on two days in a row. Many American volunteer observers switched from afternoon to morning readings during the twentieth century, and that one change made later years look slightly cooler than they really were.

Climate scientists find these jumps by comparing a station with its neighbors. If one station suddenly runs half a degree warmer and none of the others do, something changed at the station, not in the climate. Correcting for that is called homogenization. The original raw data are always kept. The adjusted data are documented so anyone can check them. Correcting a known station move in the open is ordinary measurement science.

16.12 The Hundred-Year Flood That Came Twice

In late July 2016, a storm dropped about 15 centimeters (6 inches) of rain in a couple of hours on Ellicott City, Maryland, and a torrent tore through its old main street. It was called a 1,000-year flood. On May 27, 2018, it happened again. About 20 centimeters (8 inches) fell in a few hours, and the river beside the town rose more than 5 meters (16.5 feet) in under three hours.

How can a 1,000-year flood come twice in two years? Because the name is misleading. A "1,000-year" event has about a 0.1 percent chance of happening in any given year. A 100-year flood has a 1 percent chance each year. That average spacing is called the return period. It is a measure of odds, not a schedule. The weather keeps no waiting list.

The odds also add up over time. A 1 percent chance each year sounds small, but over a 30-year mortgage it grows to about a 26 percent chance of at least one such flood. More than one in four.

Those odds come with an assumption: that the weather of the future behaves like the weather of the past. Scientists call this stationarity. It is breaking down. Towns pave over fields, so rain runs off faster. Seas are rising. And a warmer atmosphere holds more water to drop (Chapter 3, 3.2). A flood level that once had a 1 percent chance may now have more.

The record books show the shift. In a steady climate, new record highs and new record lows would turn up at about the same rate. In the United States, record highs have been outnumbering record lows, by about two to one in the first decade of this century. One cold record doesn't cancel that out, any more than one cold day cancels summer. And sometimes the shift arrives all at once: when Lytton, British Columbia, reached 49.6°C (121.3°F) in June 2021, it broke Canada's old record by more than 4°C (Chapter 5, 5.12, and Chapter 14, 14.12).

Weather Safety

Don't Chase the Record

No measurement is worth getting hurt for.

  • Lightning. Don't go out to collect hail while thunder is still rumbling. Wait until 30 minutes after the last thunder (Chapter 7).
  • Floods. Never wade or drive into floodwater to read a gauge or take a photo (Chapter 11).
  • Snow and ice. Don't climb onto an icy roof or deck to measure snow. A snow board on the ground is the standard anyway.
  • Wind. Don't stay on the beach or the ridge for a hurricane gust reading (Chapter 9).
  • After the storm. Stay away from downed power lines and damaged buildings, however interesting the damage looks (Chapter 8).

A missing observation is far better than a missing observer. The atmosphere will make another number.

Field Observation

Investigate a Record Claim

Next time you see a "record" in the news or on social media, run it through the five gates of Visual Spread 16.10. Write down the exact value, the date, the station, the time period, and the instrument. Find the original source, such as a national weather service or the WMO, not a screenshot. Check whether it is preliminary or certified, and whether it was measured or only forecast. Then ask if the comparison is fair: gust against gust, sea-level pressure against sea-level pressure, snowfall against snowfall.

Finish with one sentence: why the claim is believable, or why it is still uncertain.

Try It

Good Spot, Bad Spot

On a sunny afternoon, set two identical thermometers outside: one in shade over grass, with air moving freely around it, and one near pavement or a sunny wall. Read both every 15 minutes for an hour or two. The badly placed thermometer will usually run warmer, sometimes by several degrees. That gap is why exposure rules exist. (Bring the thermometers in before they bake.)

Try It

Work Out the Odds

Pick an event with a 2 percent chance each year, a "50-year flood." The chance it doesn't happen in one year is 0.98. Over 20 years, multiply 0.98 by itself 20 times (most phone calculators have an x^y key): about 0.67. So the chance it happens at least once in 20 years is 1 minus 0.67, about 0.33. One in three. Try it for your own town's flood map and the years you plan to live there.

Chapter Summary

A weather record is a number that has survived questions. Every record needs a label: what was measured, where, and over how long. Change any part, and it is a different record.

A record is not the same as an extreme. The deadliest storms are often not the strongest, and warm nights can be more dangerous than hot afternoons.

Most record disputes come down to how the measurement was made. Temperature must be measured in the shade, over open ground. Snow must be measured on a board cleared no more than every six hours. Wind records must say whether they are gusts or sustained winds. Pressure records must say whether they are station or sea-level values. Hail must be measured, not guessed.

Records are checked by testing the instrument, inspecting the site, comparing the neighbors and the weather map, and reading the metadata. Some, like El Azizia and Seymour Island, fail. Others, like Esperanza and Coningsby, pass.

Old records are rescued from logbooks, repaired for station moves and changes, and rebuilt into reanalyses. A 100-year flood has a 1 percent chance every year, and in a warming climate, those odds are shifting. Record highs now outnumber record lows.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, the extremes are real. Knowing which numbers to trust is how we learn from them.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Weather record: The highest, lowest, greatest, or longest verified value in a defined set of measurements. Extreme: Weather severe enough to do harm, whether or not it breaks a record. Period of record: The span of years a station or dataset covers. Record high minimum: The warmest daily low temperature on record. Record low maximum: The coldest daily high temperature on record. Exposure: What surrounds an instrument, and how it affects the reading. Snowfall: How much snow falls in a storm or a period. Snow depth: How much snow is on the ground at one moment. Snow board: A flat board for measuring new snow, cleared at set times. Gust: A burst of wind lasting a few seconds. Sustained wind: Wind averaged over a set time, one minute in the US and ten minutes in most other countries. Station pressure: The air pressure actually measured at a station's height. Sea-level pressure: The pressure a station would read if it sat at sea level. Megaflash: A single lightning flash hundreds of kilometers long, seen by satellites. Metadata: Information about how, where, when, and with what a measurement was made. Quality-control flag: An automatic warning that a reading looks suspicious and needs review. Provisional record: A possible record still being checked. Certified record: A record accepted after official review. Raw data: Measurements exactly as first recorded. Adjusted data: Measurements corrected, in a documented way, for station moves and other changes. Homogenization: Finding and correcting jumps in a long record that come from changes at the station, not the climate. Reanalysis: A reconstruction of past weather made by feeding old observations into a modern weather model. Return period: The average spacing between events of a given size, such as 100 years for a 1-percent-a-year flood. Stationarity: The assumption that future weather will behave statistically like past weather.
