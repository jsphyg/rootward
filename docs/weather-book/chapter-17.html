Chapter 17

Weather Folklore, Animal Signs, and Which Old Sayings Actually Work

On January 6, 1839, a blizzard roared up the coast of New England. It lasted about twelve hours and wrecked ship after ship. One of them, the schooner Favorite, was smashed on a reef called Norman's Woe, off Gloucester, Massachusetts.

That December, the poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow turned the wreck into a poem, "The Wreck of the Hesperus." In it, an old sailor begs the skipper to put into port, because he fears a hurricane. He has seen a sign:

Last night, the moon had a golden ring,
And to-night no moon we see!

The skipper blows a whiff from his pipe and laughs. Soon the gale has the ship.

The poem is invented, but the old sailor's reasoning is not. A ring around the Moon is made by ice crystals in a high, thin veil of cloud, and that veil often runs a day ahead of a storm (Chapter 15, 15.8). The sailor was reading the sky. The skipper was ignoring it.

Long before radar, people forecast weather with sayings like that one. Some are sharp observations packed into a rhyme. Others are wishful thinking that rhymes too. This chapter sorts them out and shows you how to test any saying yourself.

17.1 The Oldest Weather Book

In the 300s BC, in Athens, Theophrastus took over Aristotle's school, the Lyceum. Among the many works linked to his name is a short book called On Weather Signs. It lists clues that rain, wind, storms, or fair weather are on the way: the look of the Sun, the Moon, the clouds, and much more. Scholars still debate how much of it he wrote himself. Either way, it shows that people were collecting weather sayings more than two thousand years ago.

A good saying is really a compressed observation, noticed by someone and passed down.

But a saying belongs to a landscape. "Wind from the east, weather not fit for beast" makes sense on coasts where an east wind blows in off a cold sea ahead of a storm. Move it inland, or to the tropics, where east winds are the everyday trade winds (Chapter 14, 14.6), and the reason it worked disappears. A compass direction has no personality of its own.

Sayings also survive for reasons that have nothing to do with being right. They rhyme, so they stick. And memory keeps score unfairly. Suppose your neighbor says ants march before rain. The three times it worked, you remember. The times it didn't quietly vanish. This habit of remembering the hits and forgetting the misses is called confirmation bias. It is the main reason bad sayings live as long as good ones.

So for every saying in this chapter, the question is the same. Is there something real in the air behind it? And does it actually work better than a guess?

17.2 Red Sky at Night

Nearly two thousand years ago, according to the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus remarked on a forecast everyone already knew: "When it is evening, ye say, It will be fair weather: for the sky is red. And in the morning, It will be foul weather to day: for the sky is red and lowring." Sailors later gave it the version we know: "Red sky at night, sailor's delight. Red sky in morning, sailor's warning."

Chapter 15 showed why sunset is red. Low sunlight travels a long road through the air, and the blue is scattered away (15.3). The saying adds one more piece: where the clouds are.

Here is the test. In the middle latitudes, weather usually travels from west to east, carried by the westerlies (Chapter 14, 14.7). At sunset, the Sun is in the west. For its red light to reach the clouds overhead and paint them, the western sky must be fairly clear for a long way. Clear air to the west means clear air coming. That is the sailor's delight.

At sunrise, the Sun is in the east. If it lights up clouds overhead and to the west, then the clear sky is behind you, moving away, and clouds are what's coming. That is the warning. Britain's Met Office explains it the same way and adds that the saying works best where weather comes mostly from the west, as it does in the UK.

So the saying passes, with conditions. It fails in the tropics, where weather often moves from east to west. It fails when the weather pattern stalls or swings around a low (Chapter 5, 5.12). It fails in summer, when storms bubble up locally in the afternoon rather than marching in from the west. And it fails when the red comes from wildfire smoke or dust rather than lit-up clouds (Chapter 11, 11.11).

There is no famous scorecard for red sky, which is surprising for a saying so old. You can keep one yourself. The Field Observation at the end of this chapter shows how.

Verdict: one of the better sayings, in the middle latitudes. It tells you where the clear sky is, which often tells you what is coming.

17.3 Rings, Ripples, and Towers

Longfellow's old sailor had good company. "Mackerel sky and mares' tails make tall ships carry low sails" is another sailor's saying, and it describes the same storm from farther away.

Mares' tails are wisps of cirrus, ice crystals trailing in fast winds high up. A mackerel sky is rows of tiny cloudlets like the scales on a fish, usually cirrocumulus (Chapter 4, 4.3). Both mean moisture and wind are increasing in the upper air. When they spread across the sky from one direction, thicken into a milky veil, and a halo appears around the Sun or Moon, you are watching the warm-front ladder from Chapter 4 (4.9). Rain or snow may be a day away.

The key word is may. Plenty of halos fade with nothing following. The ring says there is ice overhead. It does not set a deadline.

Some versions add a countdown: count the stars inside the Moon's ring, and that is how many days until rain. There is no physics behind it. How many stars you see depends on how thick the cloud is, how bright the Moon is, the haze, the city lights, and your eyes. The stars are not counting anything.

"The higher the clouds, the finer the weather" is half right. Thin, scattered cirrus on a bright day usually means nothing. But a high veil that keeps thickening is often the first sign of a storm. The trend matters more than the height.

Two cloud sayings are strong. "When clouds look like towers, expect showers" is simply watching rising air at work. Cumulus with crisp, cauliflower tops that keep climbing through the afternoon can become showers and thunderstorms within an hour or two (Chapter 7, 7.3). And smooth, lens-shaped clouds parked over mountains really do mean strong wind, though up at ridge level rather than necessarily in your valley (Chapter 4, 4.8).

Verdict: halos, mares' tails, and growing towers are among the most useful signs in this chapter. Star counting is not.

17.4 When Smoke Hangs Low

On Wednesday, October 27, 1948, a fog began to settle over Donora, Pennsylvania, a mill town on the Monongahela River southeast of Pittsburgh. It did not lift. Warm air had slid in above the valley, forming a lid over the cold air at the bottom (Chapter 2, 2.9). Smoke and fumes from the town's zinc works and steel-wire plant rose a little, hit the lid, and spread out flat. Day after day they built up.

More than 6,000 of the town's 14,000 people fell ill. By the time rain finally came on Sunday, October 31, and washed the air clean, 20 people had died. About 50 more died of breathing problems within a month. Donora helped start the clean-air movement in the United States.

The old saying goes, "Smoke hangs low, rain or snow." At Donora, the smoke hung low for days, and the rain came last of all, to end it.

That is the real lesson of smoke. It reads the stability of the air, not the rain. Smoke that rises and scatters means the air near the ground is unstable and mixing well, which often happens on fair, breezy days, but also before afternoon showers. Smoke that flattens and drifts along the ground means a lid. A lid can come with fog, with a calm high-pressure night, or ahead of a warm front. Low smoke is a strong sign of trapped air and poor air quality. It is a weak sign of rain.

Dew sayings work the same way. "When the dew is on the grass, rain will never come to pass" is partly true, because heavy dew means the night was clear and calm (Chapter 3, 3.5). But that describes last night. A front can still arrive by afternoon. Dew is a weather report, not a forecast. The same goes for heavy frost.

So does "sound travels far before rain." Under a lid of warm air, sound bends back toward the ground, so a distant train can seem close. But the lid can just as easily belong to a clear, quiet night.

Verdict: smoke, dew, frost, and far-carrying sound are good signs of stable air, and weak signs of rain.

17.5 The Smell of Rain

In March 1964, two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, published a paper in the journal Nature about a smell everyone knew and no one had named. They found that dry rocks and soil collect oily compounds that are released when rain wets them. They needed a word for the scent, so they made one: petrichor, from the Greek petra, stone, and ichor, the fluid said to flow in the veins of the gods.

Part of the smell comes from geosmin, a compound made by soil bacteria. The human nose is extraordinarily sensitive to it. In 2015, researchers at MIT used high-speed cameras to show how the smell gets into the air: a raindrop landing on dry, porous ground traps tiny air bubbles, which burst upward as a fine spray, carrying the scent with them. Before a thunderstorm, there can also be a sharp, clean smell, partly from ozone made by lightning.

So "you can smell rain coming" is real. But notice what it means. The smell is coming from rain that is already falling somewhere upwind. It is not a forecast. It is a detection.

Several of the best old signs work this way. On a hot afternoon, a sudden cool gust often means a thunderstorm's outflow has reached you, sometimes several minutes before the rain (Chapter 6, 6.11). Leaves flipping over to show their pale undersides mean the wind has picked up and shifted. A dog that hides under the bed may be hearing thunder you can't hear yet.

These signs are useful, but their warning time is minutes, not days. And "the calm before the storm" is not one of them. Sometimes the wind dies before a storm. Often it doesn't. Never wait for a lull before taking shelter.

Verdict: the smell of rain, a sudden cool gust, and flipping leaves are reliable signs that a storm is close. Thunder is the signal to go inside (Chapter 7).

17.6 The Birds That Left Early

In the spring of 2014, Henry Streby and his team were testing whether tiny tracking devices, weighing less than two nickels, could be carried by golden-winged warblers, small songbirds nesting in the Cumberland Mountains of eastern Tennessee. Then, in late April, a huge storm system crossed the region. It would spawn 84 confirmed tornadoes and kill at least 35 people.

When the researchers got the trackers back, they found something no one had planned to study. More than a day before the storms arrived, the warblers had left. They flew about 1,500 kilometers (930 miles) in five days, out of the storms' way, and then came back.

How did they know? The researchers suspect the birds heard the storm. Severe storms give off infrasound, sound too deep for human ears, that can travel hundreds of kilometers. The study was published in Current Biology in December 2014.

Animals clearly sense weather we can't, through sound, pressure, humidity, and light. The hard question is whether a particular behavior reliably comes before a particular kind of weather.

Most bird sayings are weaker than the warblers' story. "Swallows fly low before rain" has a real basis, but an indirect one. Swallows eat flying insects, and insects often stay lower in humid, unsettled air. The birds are following their dinner. Birds falling quiet, or seabirds coming inland, can mean wind and weather are changing, but both happen for many other reasons too.

Verdict: animals sense more than we do. A single bird is weak evidence. A whole flock doing something unusual is worth noticing, but not worth more than a forecast.

17.7 Sharks, Fish, and Falling Pressure

In September 2001, Tropical Storm Gabrielle approached the Gulf Coast of Florida. In a shallow bay, scientists were already tracking young blacktip sharks with acoustic tags. As the storm drew near, the sharks left the bay, in numbers the team had never seen, for deeper water outside. A week or two after the storm, they came back.

It wasn't the wind or the rain that sent them, the researchers concluded. It was the drop in air pressure, which the sharks may sense through their inner ear. A falling barometer means less weight of air pressing on the sea, and so less pressure underwater too. The study was published in 2003 by Michelle Heupel, Colin Simpfendorfer, and Robert Hueter.

That supports the angler's saying that fish behave differently before a storm. It doesn't say how. Anglers report fish biting more before a front, and fish biting less. Species differ, and so do light, water temperature, and food. There is no rule that falling pressure means good fishing.

Some farm sayings hold up worse. "Cows lie down before rain" is the most famous, and Britain's Met Office says there is no scientific evidence for it. Cows lie down to rest and to chew their cud, rain or shine. A field of cows lying down tells you the cows are lying down.

And "cats wash behind their ears before rain" has no evidence at all. The cat is doing cat business.

Verdict: sharks are better barometers than cows. Neither is better than a barometer.

17.8 The Cricket Thermometer

In 1897, the physicist Amos Dolbear published a short paper in The American Naturalist titled "The Cricket as a Thermometer." On warm nights, he had noticed, crickets chirp faster. On cool nights, they slow down. He worked out a formula for turning chirps into degrees.

He wasn't quite the first. In 1881, Margarette W. Brooks of Salem, Massachusetts, had described the same link in a letter to Popular Science Monthly. Dolbear didn't learn of it until after his paper came out, and the rule is still called Dolbear's law.

It works because a cricket is cold-blooded. Its body runs at the temperature of the air, and the chemistry that powers its chirping speeds up as it warms. The snowy tree cricket is so regular that it is nicknamed the thermometer cricket.

You can try it tonight. In Celsius, count the chirps of one cricket in 8 seconds and add 5. In Fahrenheit, as NOAA suggests, count the chirps in 15 seconds and add 40. The Celsius rule is fairly accurate between about 5°C and 30°C (40°F and 85°F). In colder air, crickets slow down and fall silent.

Notice what the cricket is doing, though. It is telling you the temperature now. It is a thermometer, not a forecaster.

The same is true of many other creatures. Frogs call more in humid, rainy weather because damp air protects their skin. Fireflies flash most on warm, still, humid evenings. Mosquitoes swarm when the air is humid and the wind is light. Bees head home when the light dims and the wind rises. All of them are reporting the weather around them. None is announcing next week.

Verdict: crickets are one of the best animal weather signs, because they measure something real. They measure the present.

17.9 Pinecones, Hair, and the Storm Glass

In the 1860s, a curious glass tube became popular in Britain. It was sealed and filled with a clear liquid in which crystals formed, feathery on some days, cloudy or settled on others. It was called a storm glass, and it had a famous champion: Admiral Robert FitzRoy, the founder of Britain's storm warnings (Chapter 12, 12.1). FitzRoy believed its crystals changed with the direction of the wind.

Others were less sure. In 1863, the scientist Charles Tomlinson concluded that the storm glass was only "a crude kind of thermoscope," a device that shows temperature, and a worse one than a thermometer. In 2008, a study in the Journal of Crystal Growth found the same thing: temperature change alone makes the crystals grow and shrink. The storm glass is a lovely piece of chemistry with an ambitious job title.

Some natural signs do much better, because they respond to something real: moisture. A pinecone opens its scales in dry air, which lets the seeds fly free, and closes them in damp air. Hair stretches and frizzes as it soaks up water vapor. Since the 1700s, hair has been the working part of instruments that measure humidity, called hygrometers (Chapter 18). Seaweed hung by the door goes limp and damp. A rope of natural fiber tightens.

These are honest humidity meters. But humidity is not rain. Air turns damp before many storms, and it also turns damp every evening as it cools, near the sea, and in the bathroom after a shower. A closing pinecone says "more moisture." It doesn't say why.

Verdict: pinecones, hair, and seaweed really do sense humidity. The storm glass mostly senses the temperature of the room.

17.10 Feeling It in Your Bones

In December 2017, a team led by Anupam Jena at Harvard Medical School looked for rain in medical records. They studied more than 11 million doctor's visits by about 1.5 million older Americans on Medicare between 2008 and 2012, and they compared visits on rainy days with visits on dry ones. If rain makes joints ache, more people should have come in with joint and back pain on rainy days. They didn't. The team found no link.

Two years later, in October 2019, a team at the University of Manchester, in England, led by Will Dixon, tried a different approach. In a project called "Cloudy with a Chance of Pain," people with arthritis and other long-term pain recorded their pain every day on their phones during 2016, while the phones noted the local weather. The main analysis used 2,658 people with about six months of daily data.

This time there was a link, but not to rain. The strongest connection was to high humidity, with low pressure and stronger wind also playing a part. Rain itself made no difference, and neither did temperature on average. On a damp, windy, low-pressure day, the chance of a painful day was about 20 percent higher than on an average day. For a person with a 5-in-100 chance of a bad day, that means about 6 in 100.

So the two studies don't really disagree. Rain doesn't make joints hurt. Damp, windy, low-pressure weather might, a little, for some people. The effect is real enough to measure, and too small to forecast with.

If you think your joints are a barometer, keep a diary of pain next to pressure, humidity, and wind. After a few months, you'll know whether you have a pattern or a story.

Verdict: possibly real for some people, but weak, and not about rain.

17.11 Groundhogs, Woolly Bears, and Almanacs

In the fall of 1948, Charles Curran, the curator of insects at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, went to Bear Mountain State Park, north of the city, to collect woolly bear caterpillars. These fuzzy caterpillars, which grow up to be Isabella tiger moths, have a rusty-brown band across the middle and black at each end. Folklore says the wider the brown band, the milder the winter.

Curran counted the bands, and a reporter shared his forecast in the New York Herald Tribune. He kept it up for about eight years. His counts mostly pointed to mild winters, and the winters that followed were milder than average. But Curran was having fun with an old tradition, not running an experiment. Scientists now know that the bands depend mostly on how old the caterpillar is and how damp the season was while it grew. They describe the caterpillar's past, not the coming winter.

Every year on February 2, a groundhog in Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, makes a more famous forecast. By legend, Phil has done it since 1887: see his shadow, and winter lasts six more weeks. NOAA has scored him against U.S. temperatures in February and March. Over the past 20 years, Phil has been right about 35 percent of the time. A coin would do better.

And then there are the almanacs. The Old Farmer's Almanac, founded by Robert B. Thomas in 1792, still publishes long-range forecasts from a secret formula, said to be kept in a black tin box at its offices in Dublin, New Hampshire. In 1981, John Walsh of the University of Illinois and a colleague checked five years of its monthly forecasts. The temperature forecasts got the direction right 50.7 percent of the time, and the precipitation forecasts 51.9 percent. Guessing would get about 50.

Thick onion skins, big acorn crops, and early winter coats reflect the season just past or the length of the days, not the months ahead. Real seasonal outlooks come from the oceans and climate patterns of Chapter 14 (14.11).

Verdict: fun, traditional, and no better than chance.

17.12 Keep a Scorecard

Chapter 13 told the story of John Finley, whose tornado forecasts of 1884 were 96.6 percent correct, until someone pointed out that always forecasting "no tornado" would have scored 98.2 (13.12). The same trap catches weather sayings. A sign that is "right" most of the time may just be riding on how common, or how rare, the weather already is.

So test a saying the way a scientist would. First, make it exact. "A ring around the Moon means rain soon" becomes "After a clear 22-degree halo around the Moon, at least a trace of rain or snow will fall here within 24 hours." Now it can be checked.

Then record every case, not just the memorable ones. Each night you look, the result falls into one of four boxes:

Rain followsNo rain follows
Sign seenHitFalse alarm
No signMissCorrect "no"

A good sign needs plenty of hits, but also few false alarms and few misses. A saying that only remembers its hits is a scrapbook, not a scorecard.

Next, compare it with the base rate, how often the weather happens anyway. If it rains on 40 days in every 100 in your season, a sign that is followed by rain 45 times in 100 is barely better than nothing. A useful sign has to beat the simple guesses: rain on the usual share of days, tomorrow like today, or the official forecast.

And be patient. Three hits in a row prove nothing. A real test takes dozens of cases.

The best use of old sayings is alongside modern ones. A halo by itself is a hint. A halo, a falling barometer, a wind turning south, and a forecast showing a warm front on the way is a strong case. The saying supplies the clue. Meteorology supplies the reason.

Weather Safety

Never Let a Saying Overrule a Warning

  • Act on official warnings at once, even if the animals seem calm, the sky isn't green, there was no red sunrise, or the storm is coming from an unusual direction (Chapter 20).
  • Thunder means go inside now. Don't wait for the cool gust, the smell of rain, or a lull. Stay in for 30 minutes after the last thunder (Chapter 7).
  • Don't wait for a calm before the storm. Many dangerous storms arrive with no lull at all.
  • Don't plan the season by caterpillars or groundhogs. Prepare for winter, hurricanes, and fire every year (Chapters 9, 10, and 11).
  • If smoke is hanging low and the air smells of it, check the air-quality report and limit time outdoors (Chapter 11, 11.11).

Dangerous weather does not have to send a sign first. A tornado is not required to consult the livestock.

Field Observation

Test the Red Sky

For one season, look west at every sunset and east at every sunrise. Each time, write down whether the sky is red, where the colored clouds are, whether the red seems to come from smoke or haze, and the wind direction. Then write down the weather over the next 24 hours.

At the end, sort your cases into the four boxes of 17.12: hits, misses, false alarms, and correct "no"s. Count how often it rained after a red evening, and compare that with how often it rained after any evening. Is the saying better than the base rate where you live? Try the same with halos around the Moon.

Try It

A Pinecone Hygrometer

Find a dry, open pinecone. Set it somewhere dry indoors and note how wide its scales are. Then put it in a damp, closed place, such as a sealed plastic bag with a damp paper towel (not touching water). Over a few hours, the scales close. Bring it back to dry air, and they open again. The cone is sensing moisture. It can't tell an approaching storm from a steamy kitchen.

Try It

Count a Cricket

On a warm evening, find one cricket chirping steadily. Count its chirps for 8 seconds and add 5 to get degrees Celsius, or count for 15 seconds and add 40 to get degrees Fahrenheit. Check your answer against a thermometer. Try it on a few nights and see how close the cricket gets.

Chapter Summary

Weather sayings began as observations, packed into rhymes and passed down. Some contain real physics. Others survive because they rhyme and because we remember the hits and forget the misses.

Red sky at night works fairly well in the middle latitudes, where weather moves west to east, because a red evening means clear sky to the west. It fails in the tropics, in stalled patterns, and when the red comes from smoke.

Halos, mares' tails, mackerel skies, and growing towers are among the best signs. Smoke, dew, and far-carrying sound reveal stable air but say little about rain. The smell of rain and a sudden cool gust detect weather that is already close.

Warblers and sharks show that animals can sense storms, but most animal sayings are unreliable. Crickets tell the temperature now. Pinecones and hair sense humidity. Groundhogs, woolly bears, almanacs, and storm glasses do no better than chance.

To test a saying, make it exact, record every case, count the hits, misses, and false alarms, and compare it with the base rate.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, generations of people watched the sky closely. The best of what they saw is still worth knowing. Science just asks us to keep score.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Weather lore (folklore): Traditional sayings and beliefs used to foretell the weather. Confirmation bias: Remembering the evidence that fits a belief and forgetting the evidence that doesn't. Mares' tails: Wispy cirrus clouds shaped like streaming horse tails. Mackerel sky: Rows of small, high cloudlets like fish scales, usually cirrocumulus. Petrichor: The earthy smell of rain falling on dry ground. Geosmin: A strong-smelling compound made by soil bacteria, part of the smell of rain. Detection: Noticing weather that is already happening nearby, as opposed to forecasting it. Infrasound: Sound too low for human ears, which can travel hundreds of kilometers. Dolbear's law: The rule linking a cricket's chirping rate to the air temperature. Hygrometer: An instrument that measures humidity. Storm glass: A sealed glass of chemicals whose crystals change mainly with temperature. Hit: The sign appeared and the weather followed. Miss: The weather came without the sign. False alarm: The sign appeared but the weather didn't follow. Base rate: How often the weather happens anyway, without any sign.
