Chapter 9

Tropical Cyclones: Hurricanes, Typhoons, Storm Surge, and Inland Flooding

A hurricane is the place where the ocean of air drinks most deeply from the ocean of water.

Over the warmest tropical seas, the bottom of the atmosphere sits on a second, wetter ocean. Every day, the Sun heats that water, and the water hands its heat and moisture to the air above it. Most days, the air carries that heat away in ordinary showers. But now and then, a cluster of thunderstorms organizes itself into a spinning machine that keeps drawing on the sea and feeding the heat back into itself. It can grow hundreds of kilometers wide and run for two weeks.

This is the basic weather machine of Chapter 1 at its most powerful. The Sun supplies the energy. The tropical sea stores it. The storm carries it upward and away, toward the poles. It is the atmosphere trying to even things out, on a grand and violent scale.

Chapter 6 followed the great lows of the middle latitudes, which feed on the clash of warm and cold air. A tropical cyclone is a different kind of storm. It has no fronts. Its fuel is warm water, and the heat released when water vapor turns back into cloud.

This chapter follows one of these storms from a cluster of clouds off Africa to a hurricane with a calm eye. Then it follows the storm to shore, where the wind gets the headlines but the water does most of the killing.

9.1 One Storm, Many Names

A tropical cyclone is a large, rotating storm that forms over warm tropical water, with thunderstorms wrapped around a center of low pressure.

The name changes with the ocean. Over the North Atlantic and the eastern North Pacific, a strong one is a hurricane. Over the western North Pacific, it is a typhoon. Over the Indian Ocean and the South Pacific, it is simply a cyclone. The storm itself is the same in every basin.

About 85 of them reach tropical-storm strength somewhere on Earth in a typical year. The western North Pacific is the busiest basin by far, with about a third of the world's total. The Atlantic, despite its fame, is usually well behind.

Each basin has a season, when its water is warmest. The Atlantic season officially runs from June 1 to November 30, and it peaks in early to mid-September, when the sea has had all summer to warm. In the Southern Hemisphere, the season runs the other way, from about November to April.

The Coriolis effect of Chapter 5 sets the direction of spin. Tropical cyclones turn counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern. They almost never cross the equator, and the cool South Atlantic almost never makes one at all.

9.2 A Storm That Drinks the Sea

A tropical cyclone runs on the heat hidden inside water vapor.

It works like this. Wind blowing across warm water evaporates huge amounts of it. That evaporation stores energy in the vapor, the latent heat of Chapter 1. The moist air spirals inward along the sea toward the low pressure at the center. Near the center, it rises in towering thunderstorms. As it rises, it cools, its vapor condenses into cloud and rain, and the stored heat is released into the air around it.

That heat warms the middle of the storm. Warm air is lighter, so the column of air over the center weighs less, and the pressure at the sea surface drops further. Lower pressure pulls in more air from outside, faster. Faster wind evaporates more water. More water means more heat.

The storm feeds itself. As long as the sea stays warm and nothing disturbs the machine, each turn of the cycle makes the next one stronger.

At the top of the storm, 12 to 15 kilometers (8 to 10 miles) up, the risen air has to go somewhere. It spreads outward in a broad, slow pinwheel of high cloud called the outflow. A storm with good outflow is like a chimney with a good draft. It can keep pulling air up from below.

Because its center is warmer than its surroundings, a tropical cyclone is called a warm-core storm. The lows of Chapter 6 are the opposite. Their energy comes from warm and cold air sliding past each other along fronts, and their cores are cold aloft. When a hurricane leaves the tropics, it can turn into one of those lows, as Section 9.12 explains.

9.3 The Recipe

Every year, the tropics fill with clusters of thunderstorms. Only a few become tropical cyclones. They need several things at once.

Warm water, and plenty of it. The sea surface usually has to be at least 26.5°C (80°F). But the warmth has to run deep, through roughly the top 50 meters (about 160 feet). A strong storm churns the sea and pulls up cooler water from below. If the warm layer is thin, the storm soon cools its own fuel supply.

Moist air, high up. Thunderstorms need humid air through much of the lower atmosphere. A layer of dry air in the middle, such as dust-laden air blowing off the Sahara, can choke a young storm.

Something to start from. Tropical cyclones almost never begin in quiet air. In the Atlantic, the usual seed is a tropical wave, a ripple of low pressure that drifts westward off Africa every few days from late spring to fall. Each one brings a burst of showers and a shift in the wind. Most fade away. But most of the Atlantic's major hurricanes start as one of them.

Weak wind shear. Chapter 7 showed how wind changing with height helps a thunderstorm organize. A tropical cyclone needs the opposite. Its rising column has to stand straight up, with the warm core stacked over the center at the surface. Strong winds aloft tilt the storm, blow its thunderstorms off to one side, and push in dry air. Shear has killed more would-be hurricanes than anything else.

Some distance from the equator. The storm needs the Coriolis effect to start spinning, and the Coriolis effect fades to nothing at the equator. Almost all tropical cyclones form at least 500 kilometers (300 miles) away from it.

Even with every ingredient in place, most clusters never organize. Forecasters still can't say exactly why one does and its neighbor doesn't.

9.4 From Cluster to Hurricane

As a young storm grows, it climbs a ladder of names. Each rung is set by its maximum sustained wind, the strongest wind averaged over one minute (the US standard; most other countries use ten minutes).

It starts as a tropical disturbance, a cluster of thunderstorms that holds together for a day or more. Once the wind around it closes into a full circle around a center, it becomes a tropical depression, with winds up to 62 km/h (38 mph). A depression gets a number, not a name.

At 63 km/h (39 mph), it becomes a tropical storm, and it gets a name. Names make it easy to talk about several storms at once without confusing them. In the Atlantic, six lists of names are used in rotation, so a list comes back every six years. When a storm is so deadly or destructive that reusing its name would be painful, the name is retired and replaced. Katrina, Harvey, and Ian will never be used again.

At 119 km/h (74 mph), the storm becomes a hurricane or typhoon. Most strong ones now have an eye. At 178 km/h (111 mph), it is a major hurricane, Category 3 or higher on the scale in Section 9.10.

There is one more label, used when there isn't time to wait. A potential tropical cyclone is a disturbance that hasn't yet organized but is expected to bring storm conditions to land within two days. The label lets forecasters issue warnings before the storm has a proper name.

Each label describes only the wind. A depression can still drop enough rain to flood a city. The ladder tells you how hard the wind blows, not how much harm the storm will do.

9.5 Inside the Storm

From a satellite, a mature hurricane looks like a pinwheel of cloud with a hole in the middle. A typical one is about 500 kilometers (300 miles) across.

The hole is the eye. Most eyes are 30 to 65 kilometers (20 to 40 miles) wide, though they can be as small as 8 kilometers (5 miles) or more than 200 kilometers (120 miles). Inside, the air sinks gently from the top of the storm. Sinking air warms and dries, as Chapter 2 explained, so the clouds thin out. The wind drops to a breeze. Sometimes the Sun or stars shine through. The lowest pressure in the whole storm is here.

Around the eye stands the eyewall, a ring of towering thunderstorms. This is where the air spiraling inward along the sea finally rises. It can't reach the exact center, because it is spinning too fast, so it turns upward at the edge of the eye. The eyewall has the strongest wind, the heaviest rain, and the fastest updrafts in the storm. Over the middle of it, the high clouds merge into a solid shield that satellite forecasters call the central dense overcast.

Beyond the eyewall, curved rainbands spiral outward for hundreds of kilometers. Each band is a line of showers and thunderstorms, with calmer gaps between them. The outer bands can reach the coast a day before the center does.

The eye fools people. When it passes over, the wind falls, the rain stops, and the storm seems to be over. People step outside to look at the damage. Then, perhaps an hour later, the far side of the eyewall arrives, as fierce as the first, and now the wind blows from the opposite direction. Anything loosened in the first half becomes a missile in the second.

9.6 Growing Fast

Some hurricanes strengthen slowly over days. A few explode.

Forecasters call it rapid intensification when a storm's maximum wind rises by at least 55 km/h (35 mph) in 24 hours. It happens when everything lines up at once: very warm water running deep, moist air, weak shear, and good outflow. Then the heat engine of Section 9.2 runs faster with every turn.

The worst case is a storm that does this just before it reaches land. In October 2023, forecasters expected Otis to reach the Mexican coast near Acapulco as a tropical storm. Instead, it grew from a tropical storm into a Category 5 hurricane in about a day. It struck the city as the strongest hurricane ever to hit Mexico's Pacific coast, and many people had almost no time to prepare.

The ocean's depth matters as much as its surface. A hurricane's wind churns the sea and stirs up cooler water from below, leaving a trail of cooler water behind it, called a cold wake. Over a thin warm layer, the storm cools its own fuel and weakens. Over deep warm water, such as a warm eddy that has broken off a current like the Gulf Stream, there is no cool water near the surface to stir up, and the storm can keep growing.

Strong hurricanes also go through a strange cycle of rebuilding. A second ring of thunderstorms forms outside the eyewall. It steals the inflowing air, and the inner eyewall starves and collapses. For a while, the storm's peak wind drops. Then the outer ring shrinks inward to become the new eyewall, and the storm often strengthens again. This eyewall replacement cycle usually leaves a bigger storm behind it, with damaging wind spread over a wider area. A small drop in the peak wind doesn't mean the danger has shrunk.

9.7 Where It Goes

A tropical cyclone is not driven by its own wind. It is carried along by the larger currents of air around it, like a leaf spinning on a river. Forecasters call them steering currents.

In the Atlantic, the main current is set by the great high-pressure area that sits over the ocean in summer, often called the Bermuda High. Air flows clockwise around it. On its southern side, the flow runs east to west, so young storms drift westward across the tropics, toward the Caribbean and North America.

Then, as a storm reaches the western edge of the high, the steering current turns north. If a trough in the jet stream, like those in Chapter 5, is sweeping across the continent, its winds can catch the storm and fling it northeast, out to sea. This curving track, west, then north, then northeast, is called recurvature.

The whole question for the coast is timing. If the trough arrives early, the storm turns away. If the high is strong and stretches far to the west, the storm keeps going west into land. If the steering currents are weak, the storm can drift, loop, or stall, and a stalled storm brings the worst floods of all, as Section 9.12 explains.

Along the way, the center wobbles. Changes inside the eyewall can nudge it a few kilometers one way or another for an hour or two. A wobble on radar can make people think the storm has turned. Usually it hasn't. The steering currents decide the track, and the wobbles average out.

9.8 Flying Into the Storm

On July 27, 1943, an Army Air Forces instructor named Joseph Duckworth was training British pilots to fly by instruments in Texas. A hurricane was coming ashore near Galveston, and the British pilots teased him about the flimsy American training planes they had been given. Duckworth bet them he could fly one straight into the hurricane and back.

He took off in a small, single-engine trainer with a navigator, Ralph O'Hair, flew through the rain and turbulence of the eyewall, and came out in the calm of the eye. Then he flew home, picked up the base's weather officer, and did it again. It was the first deliberate flight into a hurricane.

Planes still fly into hurricanes today. The Hurricane Hunters, crews from the US Air Force Reserve and NOAA, fly big four-engine planes through the eyewall again and again, crisscrossing the storm. They find the exact center and measure its pressure and wind. They drop dropsondes, small tubes of instruments on parachutes that fall through the storm to the sea, radioing back pressure, temperature, humidity, and wind all the way down.

Out at sea, where no plane is flying, satellites keep watch. Forecasters can estimate a storm's strength from the pattern of its clouds alone. Other satellites bounce radar off the sea to measure the wind from how rough the water is. As a storm comes near land, coastal radars take over. Chapter 12 explains how satellites, radars, and computer forecasts work.

All of this has made forecasts far better. Track errors have shrunk by more than half since 1990. Strength is still harder. It depends on small changes deep inside the eyewall and on the hidden warmth of the sea below, and a rapid intensification like that of Otis can still catch everyone by surprise.

9.9 Reading the Forecast Map

The best-known picture in all of weather may be the hurricane forecast cone. It is also one of the most misread.

The cone shows where the storm's center will probably go. It does not show how big the storm is. It is drawn so that, based on the forecast errors of the past five years, the center stays inside it about two-thirds of the time. That means the center ends up outside it about one time in three. The cone widens with time because forecasts get less certain the further ahead they reach.

Three mistakes come from it. People outside the cone think they are safe, but wind, rain, surge, and tornadoes regularly reach far beyond it. People near the line down the middle think the storm will hit them, and people just off that line think it will miss. But the line is just one possible track. And people look at the cone and nothing else, when the most important threats, the water ones, are shown on other maps.

The better question is not "Where is the line?" It is "What could happen where I am, and when?"

The National Hurricane Center answers that with watches and warnings for each hazard. A hurricane watch means hurricane conditions are possible, usually within 48 hours. A hurricane warning means they are expected, usually within 36 hours. Separate storm surge watches and warnings cover the threat from rising water, and for anyone near the coast, they are the most important alerts of all. Chapter 20 explains how all of these work and how to receive them.

9.10 The Category Is Only the Wind

In the early 1970s, an engineer named Herbert Saffir and the director of the National Hurricane Center, Robert Simpson, built a five-step scale for hurricanes. Saffir had studied what different winds did to buildings. Simpson added what he knew about the storms. The Saffir–Simpson scale gave every hurricane a category from 1 to 5, and it soon became the number everyone knew.

At first, each category also listed a storm surge and a central pressure. That turned out to be misleading. In 2005, Hurricane Katrina came ashore as a Category 3, yet it pushed up the highest surge ever measured in the United States, about 8.5 meters (28 feet) on the Mississippi coast. In 2008, Hurricane Ike was only a Category 2 when it struck near Galveston, Texas, but it was so large that it drove a surge of about 4.5 to 6 meters (15 to 20 feet) across the coast. Category had not predicted the water.

So in 2010, the National Hurricane Center stripped the surge and pressure out of the scale. It is now the Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale. It rates a hurricane by its maximum sustained wind and the damage that wind can do, and nothing else. It says nothing about surge, rain, tornadoes, or size.

The category also changes. A storm can climb or fall two categories in a day. When it weakens near the coast, its surge, already built up by days of wind over the sea, may still arrive in full, and its rain may get worse.

A Category 1 hurricane is not a mild one. Its winds can still tear off roofs, drop trees onto houses, and cut power for days. And a weaker storm that is huge, slow, or aimed at a shallow bay can be far deadlier than a stronger one that is small and fast.

9.11 When the Sea Comes Ashore

On September 8, 1900, Galveston, Texas, was a booming port city on a low sandy island. Its highest ground stood less than 3 meters (9 feet) above the sea. That night, a hurricane pushed the Gulf of Mexico up over the whole island. The water rose about 4.6 meters (15 feet). Houses broke apart, and their wreckage became a battering ram that smashed the houses behind them. At least 8,000 people died, and perhaps many more. It is still the deadliest natural disaster in American history.

The killer was the storm surge, a rise of seawater pushed ashore by a storm, above the normal tide.

The main cause is wind. For days, a hurricane's wind drags the sea surface along with it, and where it blows toward land, the water piles up against the coast. The storm's low pressure lifts the sea a little too, but wind does most of the work.

The shape of the sea floor matters most of all. Over a broad, shallow shelf, like that off the coasts of the Gulf of Mexico, the water pushed shoreward has nowhere to go but up. Bays and river mouths funnel it higher still. Off a coast where the sea floor drops away steeply, the same storm makes a much smaller surge.

In the Northern Hemisphere, the highest surge usually comes just to the right of where the eye crosses the coast, looking in the direction the storm is moving. There, the storm's counterclockwise wind blows straight onshore, and its forward motion adds to it. But bays and inlets can shift the worst flooding elsewhere.

The surge adds to whatever the tide is doing. The combined height is called the storm tide. A surge arriving at high tide reaches higher than the same surge at low tide. Then the waves ride on top, pounding whatever the water reaches. Water can arrive hours before the strongest wind, flooding the roads people need to escape, and it can come in from behind, up rivers, creeks, and canals. A house doesn't have to face the beach to flood.

Even a storm far out at sea sends long swells ahead of it, more than a thousand kilometers. On a sunny beach, they bring heavy surf and rip currents, narrow streams of water racing away from shore. A rip pulls you out, not under. Swim parallel to the beach to escape it.

After 1900, Galveston built a seawall and raised the whole city behind it. Today, about nine in ten American deaths from tropical cyclones come from water, not wind.

9.12 Rain, Tornadoes, and the Long Goodbye

The center crossing the coast is called landfall. It is not the end of the storm. For many places, it is the start of the worst part.

Over land, a tropical cyclone loses its fuel, and rough ground slows the wind. The wind usually weakens within a day. But the storm still carries an enormous amount of water.

How much rain falls in one place depends far more on the storm's speed than on its category. In August 2017, Hurricane Harvey came ashore in Texas as a Category 4 and then stalled, with weak steering currents all around it. For four days, it drifted near Houston, dragging rainbands in from the Gulf over the same places. Near Nederland, Texas, more than 1.5 meters (60 inches) of rain fell, the most from any tropical cyclone in American history. Mountains add to it. When moist tropical air is forced up a mountainside, it can wring out deadly floods and landslides even from a storm that is only a depression. Chapter 11 explains how flooding works and how to stay safe in it.

Hurricanes also make tornadoes. Over land, friction slows the wind near the ground while the wind just above keeps racing, and that shear sets small storms in the rainbands spinning. Most of these tornadoes form in the front-right part of the storm, often in the outer bands, far from the center. They are usually weak and brief, but they come fast, wrapped in rain, often with little warning. Hurricane Ivan, in 2004, spawned more than a hundred. Chapter 8 explains tornadoes.

Rarely, over very warm, waterlogged ground, the land acts a little like the sea and keeps the storm going. Forecasters call this the brown ocean effect.

More often, a storm that moves north into cooler air changes into a different kind of storm. It meets the jet stream and the fronts of Chapter 6. Its warm core fades, it grows fronts of its own, and its wind spreads over a much wider area. This is extratropical transition. In October 2012, Hurricane Sandy became one of these lows just before it reached New Jersey. It was no longer officially a hurricane, but its huge wind field pushed a surge into New York Harbor that flooded the city's subway tunnels.

A storm that loses its tropical label has not lost its danger. It has only changed how it works.

Weather Safety

Hurricanes

  • Run from water, hide from wind. If you live where storm surge or flooding can reach, leave when officials tell you to. If you live outside those zones in a sturdy home, you can usually shelter in place from the wind.
  • Know your evacuation zone now, before any storm. Zones are drawn mainly by surge risk, not by distance from the beach.
  • Leave early. Surge can flood roads hours before the wind arrives. Don't plan a route over low bridges or causeways.
  • In a mobile home, or if you depend on power for medical equipment, plan to leave whatever your zone.
  • Before the wind starts, bring in or tie down anything outside, cover windows with shutters or plywood (tape does nothing), and fill your car's tank or charge it.
  • During the storm, stay in a small interior room on the lowest floor that will stay dry, away from windows. Never shelter below the level the water could reach.
  • In a high-rise, stay below the top floors. Wind is stronger higher up.
  • If the eye passes over, stay inside. The other half of the storm is coming.
  • Never drive or walk into floodwater. Chapter 11 explains why.
  • Run generators outdoors only, far from doors, windows, and vents. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills quickly. A garage, even with the door open, is not outdoors.
  • Afterward, wait for officials before returning. Treat every downed wire as live. Expect days without power in hot, humid weather, and check on older neighbors.

For tornadoes in a hurricane, see Chapter 8. For flooding and heat, see Chapter 11. For watches and warnings and the one-page quick reference, see Chapter 20.

Field Observation

Read a Hurricane Advisory

Find a current or past advisory from the National Hurricane Center. Note the center's position, its maximum sustained wind, its central pressure, and how fast it is moving. Then look past the cone. How far out does tropical-storm-force wind reach? Where is surge expected, and how high? Where is the heaviest rain forecast?

Then compare two hurricanes of the same category, such as Katrina and another Category 3. You'll see that the same number can mean very different disasters. The category is the wind. The story is everything else.

Try It

Make a Hurricane Decision Chart

Before the season starts, write three headings: Leave, Stay, and Decide early.

Under Leave, list what would send your household away: an evacuation order, a home in a surge zone, a mobile home, medical equipment that needs power. Under Stay, list what lets you shelter in place: outside flood zones, a sturdy building, an interior room. Under Decide early, list what slows you down: pets, a long drive, an older relative, a route over bridges.

Then pack a kit for a week without power: water, food that keeps, medicines, a flashlight, a weather radio, phone power banks, cash, and copies of important papers in a waterproof bag. Photograph your home before the storm.

The point is to make the hard decision on a calm day, not while the wind is rising.

Chapter Summary

A tropical cyclone is a warm-core storm that runs on warm ocean water and the heat released when its water vapor condenses in thunderstorms. Hurricane, typhoon, and cyclone are names for the same storm in different oceans.

It needs warm water running deep, moist air, a starting disturbance, weak wind shear, and enough distance from the equator to spin.

As it strengthens, it climbs from depression to tropical storm to hurricane. A calm eye sits inside a ring of violent eyewall thunderstorms, with rainbands spiraling outward.

Some storms intensify rapidly over deep warm water. Strong ones rebuild their eyewalls and grow larger as they do.

Steering currents carry the storm. The forecast cone shows where its center will probably go, not how big it is or where its dangers will reach.

The Saffir–Simpson scale rates only the wind. Storm surge, flooding rain, tornadoes, and rip currents depend on the storm's size, speed, and path, and on the shape of the coast and the land.

Water causes most of the deaths. Run from water, hide from wind. Where the ocean of air meets the ocean of water, the most dangerous thing a hurricane carries is the sea itself.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Tropical cyclone: A rotating, warm-core storm that forms over warm tropical water. Hurricane, typhoon, cyclone: Regional names for a strong tropical cyclone. Latent heat: Heat stored in water vapor and released when it condenses. Outflow: Air spreading out from the top of the storm. Warm core: A storm center that is warmer than the air around it. Tropical wave: A ripple of low pressure that drifts west across the tropics. The seed of many Atlantic hurricanes. Wind shear: Wind changing speed or direction with height. It tilts and weakens tropical cyclones. Maximum sustained wind: The strongest wind averaged over one minute (in the US). Tropical depression: A tropical cyclone with winds up to 62 km/h (38 mph). Tropical storm: A named tropical cyclone with winds of 63–118 km/h (39–73 mph). Major hurricane: A hurricane of Category 3 or higher. Potential tropical cyclone: A disturbance close enough to land that warnings are issued before it organizes. Eye: The calm center of a strong tropical cyclone. Eyewall: The ring of thunderstorms around the eye, with the strongest wind. Rainband: A curved band of showers and thunderstorms spiraling around the center. Central dense overcast: The thick cloud shield over the center of the storm. Rapid intensification: A rise in maximum wind of at least 55 km/h (35 mph) in 24 hours. Cold wake: The trail of cooler water a hurricane leaves behind. Eyewall replacement cycle: An outer eyewall forming and replacing the inner one. Steering currents: The larger winds that carry a storm along. Recurvature: A track that turns from west to north to northeast. Hurricane Hunters: Aircraft crews who fly into tropical cyclones to measure them. Dropsonde: A tube of instruments dropped by parachute through a storm. Forecast cone: The probable path of the storm's center, not its size. Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale: Categories 1 to 5, based on wind alone. Storm surge: Seawater pushed ashore by a storm, above the normal tide. Storm tide: Storm surge plus the normal tide. Rip current: A narrow stream of water flowing away from the shore. Landfall: When the storm's center crosses the coast. Brown ocean effect: A storm holding its strength over very wet, warm land. Extratropical transition: A tropical cyclone changing into a midlatitude low with fronts. Hurricane watch: Hurricane conditions are possible, usually within 48 hours. Hurricane warning: Hurricane conditions are expected, usually within 36 hours. Storm surge warning: Life-threatening rising water is expected.
