Chapter 5

Air Pressure, Wind, and the Forces That Move the Atmosphere

Every ocean has currents, and so does the ocean of air.

Chapter 1 showed where they come from. Air has weight, and that weight is not spread evenly. The air flows from where it presses hardest toward where it presses least, trying to even things out, and we feel that flow as wind.

If that were the whole story, every wind would blow straight from high pressure to low, and the weather would sort itself out in an afternoon. It never does. Earth spins beneath the moving air and bends its path. The ground drags at it. Mountains send it tumbling down their far sides. And far overhead, rivers of fast wind carry whole storms across continents.

A breeze through the trees and a jet stream racing at the height of an airliner follow the same few rules. The difference is scale, and a frankly indecent amount of speed.

This chapter weighs the air, maps its highs and lows, and follows the forces that turn them into the wind on your face. By the end, a weather map should look like what it is: a picture of the currents at the bottom of the ocean.

5.1 Weighing the Ocean of Air

In 1643, an Italian scientist named Evangelista Torricelli weighed the sky.

He filled a long glass tube with mercury and turned it upside down into a bowl of mercury. The column fell a little, then stopped, about 76 centimeters (30 inches) tall. Nothing held it up except the air pressing down on the bowl. The weight of the column balanced the weight of the atmosphere above it.

Torricelli had built the first barometer, an instrument that measures air pressure. He also noticed that the column was not always the same height. It rose and fell a little from day to day. In a letter the next year, he put it this way: "We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of the element air." This book's favorite picture is nearly four centuries old.

The units come from that history. In the United States, pressure is often given in inches of mercury, the height of Torricelli's column. Weather maps around the world use hectopascals (hPa), a metric unit. Older books and some broadcasts use millibars (mb), which are exactly the same size.

Average sea-level pressure is about 1,013 hPa, or 29.92 inches. Most days fall between about 980 and 1,040 hPa. That is a change of only a few percent, but it is enough to drive every wind on Earth.

Few barometers use mercury today. Most use a small sealed metal can that flexes with the pressure, and your phone uses an electronic chip. Chapter 18 explains how they work.

5.2 Pressure at Sea Level

A barometer in Denver reads low every day of the year.

Denver isn't permanently stormy. It sits about 1.6 kilometers (1 mile) up, so there is less air above it. As Chapter 1 showed, pressure falls as you climb, about 1 hPa for every 8 meters (26 feet) near sea level. A typical Denver reading is around 840 hPa.

The pressure measured at a station's own height is its station pressure. Pilots use it, but it makes a useless weather map. Every mountain town would look like a deep low, and the real highs and lows would hide behind the shape of the ground.

So meteorologists calculate what each station's pressure would be at sea level, with the missing air added back in. This sea-level pressure is the number on weather maps and in forecasts. Take out the mountains, and what is left is the weather.

5.3 Highs, Lows, and Isobars

A weather map is a map of hills and hollows in the ocean of air.

A high is a place where the pressure is higher than all around it. A low is a place where it is lower. Neither has a fixed number. A reading of 1,010 hPa is a high if everything nearby reads 1,004, and a low if everything nearby reads 1,020.

To show the pattern, meteorologists join places of equal pressure with lines called isobars, from the Greek for "equal weight." They work like the contour lines on a hiking map. Closed rings mark the highs and lows. Most surface maps draw a line every 4 hPa.

The spacing is the most useful thing on the map. Where isobars crowd together, pressure changes sharply over a short distance, and the wind is strong. Where they spread apart, the wind is light. On a hiking map, crowded lines mean a steep slope. On a weather map, they mean a windy one.

As Chapter 1 described, highs usually bring sinking air and fair weather, and lows bring rising air, clouds, and rain. These are tendencies, not promises. A high can trap fog, smog, or a week of heat under the lid from Chapter 2. A low can pass with barely a sprinkle if the air is dry.

5.4 The Push and the Turn

Two forces decide where the wind goes. The first one starts it moving. The second one bends it.

The first is the pressure-gradient force, the push from higher pressure toward lower. "Gradient" just means how steeply something changes over a distance. A big difference over a short distance is a strong push. This is the force Chapter 1 described, and the one crowded isobars reveal. If it were the only force, the wind would blow straight across the isobars, from every high into every low.

The second is the Coriolis effect, named for the French scientist Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who worked out the mathematics in the 1830s. Earth spins, carrying the ground around beneath the moving air. Air that sets off in a straight line ends up curving across the turning ground.

In the Northern Hemisphere, moving air turns to the right. In the Southern Hemisphere, it turns to the left. The effect is zero at the equator and strongest near the poles. It grows with wind speed, and it matters only for motion that lasts hours and covers long distances.

So it does not decide which way water swirls down a sink. At that scale, the shape of the basin matters far more. The atmosphere has enough mysteries without recruiting bathroom fixtures.

The Coriolis effect never starts a wind. The push gets the air moving, and the turn sends it curving away.

5.5 Wind Aloft, Wind Below

A few kilometers up, the wind settles into a balance.

High above the ground, little slows the air down. As it starts toward low pressure, the Coriolis effect turns it, and the faster it goes, the more it turns. Soon the turn exactly balances the push. The wind stops crossing the isobars and runs alongside them. This is the geostrophic wind, from Greek words meaning "earth-turning."

So aloft, the wind doesn't blow from high to low. It blows between them, following the lines.

In 1857, the Dutch scientist Christophorus Buys Ballot turned this into a rule anyone can use. In the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind, and low pressure is on your left. In the Southern Hemisphere, it is on your right. With nothing but the breeze on your neck, you can point toward the nearest storm.

Near the ground, the balance breaks. Trees, buildings, hills, and waves drag on the moving air. Friction slows the wind, and slower wind turns less. The push wins a little, and the surface wind drifts across the isobars toward the low, at about 10 to 20 degrees over the sea and 30 degrees or more over rough land.

That small leak toward the low matters enormously.

5.6 Spirals Around Highs and Lows

Put the push, the turn, and the drag together, and the wind spirals.

In the Northern Hemisphere, air near the ground spirals into a low counterclockwise, and out of a high clockwise. In the Southern Hemisphere, both reverse. A satellite picture of a big storm shows it plainly: a great comma of cloud wound around its center.

Follow the air into a low and you see why lows bring bad weather. Air pours in from every side. It can't pile up forever, so it rises. This flowing together is convergence, and as Chapter 3 showed, rising air builds clouds. High above, the air spreads apart again, which is divergence. If it spreads out aloft faster than it flows in below, the low deepens and the storm strengthens. That is why forecasters watch the upper winds so closely.

A high runs the same machine backward. Air spreads out at the ground, and air from above sinks to replace it, warming and drying as it comes.

A cyclone is any large circulation around a low. The word does not mean hurricane. The ordinary storms that cross North America and Europe every few days are cyclones too, and hurricanes are one special kind, covered in Chapter 9. An anticyclone is the circulation around a high.

Inward and rising, outward and sinking. That is the basic pattern of nearly every weather map.

5.7 What the Barometer's Trend Tells You

The direction a barometer is moving says more than the number it shows.

A single reading tells you where you are on the map. The trend tells you what is coming. Falling pressure usually means a low or a front is approaching, with thickening cloud, rising wind, and perhaps rain or snow. Rising pressure usually means the low is leaving or a high is building, and skies often clear. Steady pressure means little change for now.

Speed matters too. A gentle fall over a day can mean a weak system passing. A drop of several hectopascals in three hours means a strong low is close, and the wind is likely to rise sharply.

The barometer is not a forecast by itself. It works best read together with the wind, clouds, and temperature, the method Chapter 13 teaches.

There is one small trap. Even on a calm day, pressure rises and falls a little twice a day, peaking around 10 a.m. and 10 p.m. This is the atmospheric tide, a slow ripple through the whole ocean of air driven mainly by the Sun's heating. In the tropics, it is often the biggest change a barometer sees all day. Farther from the equator, it is about 1 hPa. An afternoon dip is not always a storm. Sometimes the atmosphere is merely keeping its appointment.

5.8 Naming and Measuring the Wind

A north wind comes from the north.

Winds are named for where they come from, not where they are going. A north wind blows from north to south. A sea breeze, as Chapter 2 showed, blows from the sea. The rule makes sense, because the name tells you where the air has been. In Canada, a north wind often brings Arctic cold and a south wind brings warmth. In Chile, it is the other way around.

Speeds come in kilometers per hour, miles per hour, meters per second, and the knot, one nautical mile per hour, used at sea and in the air.

The wind is never perfectly steady. Reports give a sustained wind, the average over a minute or more, and gusts, the brief bursts above it. Gusts do much of the damage, because a branch or a roof has to survive the strongest push, not the average one.

You don't need an instrument to judge the wind. In 1805, a British naval officer named Francis Beaufort wrote a scale of wind force from 0 to 12, based on a warship's sails. It was later rewritten for things anyone can see: smoke, leaves, branches, waves, and foam. With a little practice, the Beaufort scale is surprisingly accurate. Wind instruments are described in Chapter 18.

5.9 Wind Near the Ground

The bottom few hundred meters of the ocean of air are its roughest water.

This layer, the part that feels the ground, is the boundary layer. Above it, the wind flows smoothly. Within it, every tree, hill, and building trips the air and sends it tumbling. That tumbling is turbulence, the swirling motion you see in blowing leaves and feel as bumps on a plane. It also comes from rising warm air, and from layers of air sliding past each other at different speeds. That change of wind with height is wind shear, which Chapter 4 showed in the clouds and Chapter 7 shows inside storms.

The boundary layer breathes with the day. As the Sun heats the ground, rising air mixes the layer, often a kilometer or two deep. The mixing drags fast wind down from above, so afternoons are often the gustiest time of day. At night, a stable layer forms near the ground, as Chapter 2 described, and the mixing stops. The ground goes calm, while a few hundred meters up the wind blows on and can even speed up. Over the Great Plains, this low-level jet can top 80 km/h (50 mph), feeding nighttime thunderstorms.

Close to the ground, the details take over. Buildings squeeze the wind into fast streams around corners and down the faces of towers, which is why a city street can feel far windier than the airport report. Forests slow the wind near the floor while it blows on above the treetops. But in high wind, a forest is no shelter. Trees and branches are the first things to fail.

5.10 Mountain Winds

Mountains make their own winds, and some of the fiercest anywhere.

The gentlest follow the Sun. On a sunny morning, the slopes warm, and a valley breeze flows up them, often building cumulus over the peaks by afternoon. At night, the slopes cool, and chilled air drains downhill as a mountain breeze, the same cold air that Chapter 2 showed pooling in valleys. It is the sea breeze's cousin, driven by slopes instead of shorelines.

Over a great ice sheet, that drain becomes a gale. Air chilled by the ice pours downhill under its own weight, gathering speed. These katabatic winds, from the Greek for "going down," are strongest in Antarctica. At Commonwealth Bay, where the explorer Douglas Mawson camped in 1912, the wind averages about 80 km/h (50 mph) all year. He called it the home of the blizzard.

The most dramatic mountain winds are warm. Chapter 2 followed a bubble of air over a mountain: it cooled as it climbed, dropped its rain, and warmed as it sank down the far side, arriving warmer and drier than it started. A strong wind doing the same thing becomes a warm, dry, gusty downslope wind. In the Alps it is the foehn. East of the Rockies it is the chinook, nicknamed the snow eater, which can melt a snowpack in hours and raise the temperature by 20°C (36°F) or more in a few minutes. Southern California's Santa Ana blows from the high deserts to the coast, warming as it sinks. All of them dry out the land, and they are among the most dangerous winds for wildfire, as Chapter 11 explains.

The same journey marks the land. The windward side of a range gets the rain, and the far side lies in a rain shadow. Washington's Olympic Mountains get more than 3.5 meters (140 inches) of rain a year on their western slopes. The town of Sequim, in their shadow, gets about 40 centimeters (16 inches).

5.11 Rivers of Air Aloft

Near the top of the weather layer, the wind runs in rivers.

About 9 to 12 kilometers (30,000 to 40,000 feet) up, where airliners cruise, run narrow bands of very fast wind called jet streams. They blow mostly west to east, often at 150 to 250 km/h (90 to 155 mph), and in winter they can top 300 km/h (185 mph). That is why a flight from New York to London is often about an hour faster than the flight back.

Jet streams form where warm and cold air meet. Warm air expands, so a column of warm air is taller than a column of cold air. High up, that makes the pressure higher on the warm side. Air is pushed toward the cold side, and the Coriolis effect turns it until it runs along the boundary. The sharper the contrast, the faster the jet, so jet streams are strongest in winter. Each hemisphere has a polar jet, along the edge of the cold air, and a subtropical jet nearer the tropics. Chapter 14 shows how they fit into the planet's great belts of wind.

A jet stream doesn't run straight. It meanders north and south in great waves, usually four to six around the globe. These are Rossby waves, named for the meteorologist Carl-Gustaf Rossby, who explained them in 1939. Where the flow bulges toward the pole, it makes a ridge, with warm, sinking air and usually fair weather. Where it dips toward the equator, it makes a trough, with cold, rising air and storms.

Smaller ripples called shortwaves travel through the big waves like eddies in a current. Each brings a burst of rising air, and it is often a shortwave that decides exactly where the rain or snow falls.

Storms at the surface ride along beneath the jet. Where the jet stream goes, the weather follows.

5.12 When the Weather Gets Stuck

Usually the waves in the jet stream keep moving. Sometimes they stop.

This is blocking. A big ridge or a deep low parks in place, and the systems that would normally march past must go around it or stall. Whatever weather sits under the block stays for days, sometimes weeks. Sunshine becomes a heat wave. A few wet days become a flood.

An omega block looks like the Greek letter Ω: a tall ridge with a low on each side. In late June 2021, one parked a dome of hot, sinking air over the Pacific Northwest, and Lytton, British Columbia, set Canada's national record of 49.6°C (121.3°F). A cutoff low is a pocket of cold air aloft that pinches off from the jet stream and drifts on its own, bringing days of cloud and rain while forecasters struggle to say when it will leave.

Then there is the polar vortex, a phrase that turns up in the news every cold winter. It is a huge, permanent swirl of cold air around the pole, mostly high above the weather layer. It is not a storm, and it does not escape. But some winters it is knocked off balance, the jet stream below buckles, and lobes of Arctic air spill far south. That is what brought record cold to the American Midwest in January 2019.

Chapter 11 covers the heat waves, droughts, and floods that blocks can cause. Here the lesson is simple. Most weather comes and goes because the currents aloft keep flowing. When they stop, the weather stops with them.

Field Observation

Read the Wind Without Instruments

Go somewhere open. Look at smoke, flags, leaves, branches, and water if there is any nearby.

Using the Beaufort scale in Visual Spread 5.8, estimate the wind's force. Note where it is coming from, and whether it is steady or gusty. Then check a nearby official report.

Repeat on several days. Your estimates will soon be close.

Try It

Build a Simple Wind Vane

You will need a drinking straw, an index card, a pin, a pencil with an eraser, modeling clay, a paper plate, and a compass.

Tape a card arrowhead and a larger card tail to opposite ends of the straw. Pin the middle of the straw to the eraser. Stand the pencil in clay on the plate, and mark north, south, east, and west using the compass.

Set it outside. The arrow points into the wind, toward where the wind comes from.

Try It

Keep a Pressure Log

Most phones have a barometer, and free apps can read it. Record the pressure at the same times each day for a week, along with the clouds, the wind, and any rain.

Look for patterns. Did the pressure fall before the rain? Did the sky clear after the lowest reading?

For a bonus, find each day's surface weather map online. Spot the nearest high and low, and use section 5.6 to guess the wind direction where you are. Then check.

A week won't show every rule, but it will turn a number on a screen into a storm you saw coming.

Weather Safety

High Winds

Stay indoors and away from windows. Secure or bring in loose outdoor objects before the wind arrives. Stay out of woods, where branches and trees can fall. Avoid driving tall vehicles. Expect power outages.

Never go near a downed power line, or anything touching one. Assume it is live.

In high wind, most of the danger comes from what the wind throws and what it knocks down.

Chapter Summary

Air pressure is the weight of the ocean of air. A barometer measures it, in hectopascals, millibars, or inches of mercury. Average sea-level pressure is about 1,013 hPa, or 29.92 inches.

Weather maps adjust every station to sea level, so that mountains don't look like permanent lows.

Isobars connect places with equal pressure. Crowded isobars mean strong wind. Highs usually bring sinking air and fair weather. Lows bring rising air, clouds, and rain.

The pressure-gradient force pushes air from high to low. The Coriolis effect turns it, to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern.

Aloft, the push and the turn balance, and the wind runs along the isobars. Near the ground, friction lets it cross toward the low.

So surface winds spiral into lows and out of highs. Air flowing into a low rises and builds clouds.

The barometer's trend matters more than its number. Falling pressure often means a storm is coming. Rising pressure often means it is clearing.

Wind is named for where it comes from. The Beaufort scale lets you judge its strength by eye.

Mountains make their own winds: valley and mountain breezes, cold katabatic winds, and warm, dry downslope winds such as the chinook and foehn. They also cast rain shadows.

Jet streams are rivers of fast wind near the top of the weather layer. Their ridges and troughs steer storms. When the pattern stalls, the weather does too.

Pressure and wind are the currents at the bottom of the ocean of air, and every storm rides them.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Barometer: An instrument that measures air pressure. Hectopascal (hPa) and millibar (mb): Equal units of air pressure used on weather maps. Inches of mercury (inHg): A unit of pressure based on the height of a mercury column. Station pressure: The pressure measured at a station's own height. Sea-level pressure: Pressure adjusted to sea level so stations can be compared. High and low: Areas of higher or lower pressure than their surroundings. Isobar: A line on a map joining places with equal pressure. Pressure-gradient force: The push of air from higher toward lower pressure. Coriolis effect: The turning of moving air caused by Earth's spin. Geostrophic wind: Wind aloft that runs along the isobars. Friction: The drag of the ground that slows the wind. Convergence: Air flowing together, which forces it to rise. Divergence: Air spreading apart. Cyclone: A large circulation around a low. Not only a hurricane. Anticyclone: A large circulation around a high. Atmospheric tide: The small twice-daily rise and fall of pressure. Sustained wind and gust: The average wind speed and its brief bursts. Knot: One nautical mile per hour, about 1.85 km/h. Beaufort scale: A scale of wind force from 0 to 12, judged by its effects. Boundary layer: The lowest part of the atmosphere, stirred by the ground. Turbulence: Irregular, swirling air. Low-level jet: A band of fast wind a few hundred meters up, usually at night. Valley breeze and mountain breeze: Daytime upslope and nighttime downslope winds. Katabatic wind: Cold, heavy air pouring downhill, especially off ice sheets. Foehn and chinook: Warm, dry winds blowing down the far side of mountains. Rain shadow: The dry side of a mountain range. Jet stream: A narrow river of fast wind high in the atmosphere. Rossby waves: The great north-south waves in the jet stream. Ridge and trough: A poleward bulge and an equatorward dip in the jet stream. Shortwave: A small ripple in the upper winds that can set off rain or snow. Blocking: A stalled upper-level pattern that holds weather in place. Polar vortex: The large swirl of cold air high above each pole.
