Chapter 14

Climate Patterns: El Niño, La Niña, the Jet Stream, and Why Seasons Differ

In 1892, a Peruvian naval captain named Camilo Carrillo stood before a geography congress in Lima and described something the fishermen of the northern coast had known for generations. Every so often, around Christmas, a warm current flowed south along the shore. The sailors of the port of Paita called it El Niño, the Christ Child, for the season it arrived.

Some years the warm water stayed for months. The fish vanished, and rain poured onto desert towns that hardly ever saw it.

No one in Lima that day could have guessed how far the story reached. The warm water off Peru turned out to be one corner of a slow sloshing of the whole Pacific Ocean, and that sloshing bends the jet stream, shifts the world's rain, and changes the odds of a snowy winter in Ohio or a dry one in Australia.

This chapter steps back from tomorrow's weather to the weather's long habits: why there are seasons, how heat spreads across the planet in belts of wind and rivers in the sea, what tilts a whole season one way or another, and how the ocean of air itself is warming.

Weather is what the atmosphere does today. Climate is what it tends to do. The difference matters more than ever.

14.1 What You Expect, What You Get

In April 1815, Mount Tambora in Indonesia exploded in the largest volcanic eruption in recorded history. It threw a veil of fine sulfur droplets into the stratosphere, above the reach of rain, and the veil spread around the world, reflecting a little sunlight back to space.

The summer of 1816 never quite arrived. Snow fell in New England in June. Frost killed crops in July and August. In Europe, cold rain ruined harvests and brought famine. At a villa on Lake Geneva, a gloomy, stormy June kept a group of young writers indoors, and Mary Shelley began writing Frankenstein.

People called it the Year Without a Summer. They knew it was wrong because they knew what a summer should be like. That knowledge is climate: the long-term habits of the weather in a place.

Climate is more than an average. It includes the usual range, how often extremes come, and when the wet and dry seasons fall. Two cities can have the same average temperature for the year, one mild all year and the other hot in summer and bitter in winter. The average hides the difference.

Meteorologists measure "usual" with climate normals, averages taken over 30 years and updated every ten. The current normals cover 1991 to 2020. When a forecast says today will be "10 degrees above normal," that is the yardstick.

A normal is a reference, not a promise. The normal high might be 27°C (80°F), but few days land on it exactly. And the climate itself wanders a little from year to year, a slow restlessness called climate variability. Tambora was one cause. The ocean, as we'll see, is a bigger one.

14.2 The Tilted Planet

More than 2,200 years ago, the Greek scholar Eratosthenes heard a curious report. In the city of Syene, far up the Nile, the noon Sun on the longest day of the year shone straight down a deep well and lit the water at the bottom. In Alexandria, to the north, a stick cast a shadow at the same moment. The Sun there was about 7 degrees from straight overhead, about one-fiftieth of a circle.

Eratosthenes reasoned that the ground must curve between the two cities. Knowing roughly how far apart they were, he worked out the size of the whole Earth, and he came remarkably close.

His measurement rests on a simple fact. The Sun's angle in the sky depends on where you stand and when. Chapter 1 showed why that matters: high sunlight packs its energy into a small patch of ground, and low sunlight spreads it thin (Chapter 1, 1.5).

The seasons come from that same idea, applied across a year. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, and it keeps pointing the same direction in space as the planet circles the Sun. In June, the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun. The noon Sun climbs high, the days are long, and the ground soaks up energy. In December, the north leans away. The Sun stays low, the days are short, and the energy runs thin. The Southern Hemisphere has the opposite season at the same time.

It isn't distance. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, about 3 percent closer than in July. The tilt simply matters far more. The Northern Hemisphere has its winter a little nearer the fire, facing the wrong way.

14.3 Long Days and Late Summers

In the town of Utqiaġvik, on the northern tip of Alaska, the Sun sets around November 18 and doesn't rise again until about January 22. For some 64 days, the town lives through polar night, with only a dim twilight at noon. In summer, the reverse comes: the Sun stays up for weeks on end.

The tilt doesn't just change the Sun's angle. It changes the length of the day. Near the equator, day and night stay close to 12 hours each all year, and the seasons are mostly wet and dry, not hot and cold. Moving toward the poles, the summer days stretch and the winter days shrink, until at the Arctic and Antarctic Circles the Sun stays up for a whole day in summer and stays down for a whole day in winter.

Sun angle and day length work together, rising and shrinking at once. That is why the seasons grow stronger the farther you go from the equator.

But the hottest weeks don't come on the longest day. In much of the Northern Hemisphere, the longest day is around June 21, yet the warmest stretch usually comes in July or early August. The shortest day is around December 21, and the coldest weeks come in January.

This delay is seasonal lag, and it is the bathtub from Chapter 2 again, filled over months instead of hours (Chapter 2, 2.6). After the June solstice, the days start to shorten, but the ground and the ocean still take in more energy than they lose, so they keep warming. After the December solstice, the days lengthen, but the land keeps losing more than it gains for weeks.

Water fills its bathtub slowest of all. So the oceans, and the towns beside them, lag furthest behind.

14.4 Land, Sea, and the Shape of a Year

"The coldest winter I ever spent was a summer in San Francisco." The line is credited to Mark Twain on postcards and mugs all over the city. There is no evidence he ever said it. But whoever did knew their weather.

San Francisco sits beside a cold ocean. In summer, the land inland bakes, and the cool, foggy sea air pours in through the Golden Gate to replace the rising hot air. July afternoons in the city can be cool and gray, while the valleys an hour inland swelter. The city's warmest month is usually September, when the sea breeze finally weakens.

Chapter 2 showed why land and water heat so differently (Chapter 2, 2.4). Across a whole year, that difference shapes two kinds of climate.

A maritime climate, near the ocean or downwind of it, has mild summers, mild winters, and a small gap between them. The sea holds the temperature steady, and its lag pushes the seasons later.

A continental climate, deep inland, swings hard. In Yakutsk, Siberia, far from any ocean, January averages below −35°C (−31°F), and July is close to 20°C (68°F). That's a swing of more than 55 degrees Celsius (100 Fahrenheit) between the middle of winter and the middle of summer.

Most places fall somewhere between, and the wind decides which way they lean. A coast with the wind blowing off the sea gets the ocean's mild climate. A coast where the wind usually blows from the land, like much of eastern North America, gets the continent's.

Even within one town, climate varies. Cold air pools in low ground on still nights, and cities stay warmer after dark (Chapter 2, 2.9 and 2.10). These small local climates are called microclimates, and every gardener knows a few.

14.5 Rivers in the Sea

In the 1760s, Benjamin Franklin, then deputy postmaster for the American colonies, heard a complaint from London. The British mail ships took as much as two weeks longer to sail west to New York than American merchant ships took to cross the same ocean.

Franklin asked his cousin Timothy Folger, a whaling captain from Nantucket. Folger explained that the whalers knew a strong current ran up the coast and out across the Atlantic. They hunted along its edges and crossed it quickly. The British captains sailed straight into it and fought it for weeks.

Franklin and Folger drew the current on a chart around 1769 and 1770 and named it the Gulf Stream.

Ocean currents are the planet's other great heat carriers, sharing the atmosphere's work of moving heat toward the poles. The winds drive the surface currents, and Earth's rotation turns them into huge loops that fill each ocean basin.

Warm currents run poleward along the eastern coasts of the continents, like the Gulf Stream off the eastern United States. Cold currents run toward the equator along the western coasts, like the California Current off San Francisco and the Humboldt Current off Peru. Cold water chills the air above it, making it stable, foggy, and dry. Some of the driest deserts on Earth, such as Chile's Atacama, sit beside cold currents.

The Gulf Stream helps keep northwestern Europe mild for its latitude. London is farther north than Calgary. That is a simplification, though. Much of Europe's mildness comes from the westerly winds blowing in off the whole Atlantic, which stays warm in winter, as Section 14.4 showed.

Add mountains, which wring out the moisture on one side and cast a rain shadow on the other (Chapter 5, 5.10), and you have most of the reasons why climates differ at the same latitude.

14.6 The Great Belts of Wind

In 1735, an English lawyer named George Hadley sent a short paper to the Royal Society in London. Its title was "Concerning the Cause of the General Trade Winds." Sailors had known these steady winds for centuries. They blew from the northeast north of the equator and from the southeast south of it, so reliably that ships planned whole voyages around them. But no one could fully explain them.

Hadley's idea was simple. The Sun heats the tropics most, so the air there rises. High up, it spreads toward the poles. Cooler air near the ground flows back toward the equator to replace it. And because Earth spins, that returning air is turned aside, so it arrives as a wind from the east. Hadley's paper was largely forgotten, and several later scientists worked out the same idea on their own. Today his name is on the loop of air he described.

A Hadley cell circles in each hemisphere between the equator and about 30 degrees of latitude. It is the weather machine of Chapter 1 drawn at the size of the planet.

Where the trade winds from the two hemispheres meet, near the equator, the air rises in towering thunderstorms. This belt is the Intertropical Convergence Zone, or ITCZ. From space, it shows up as a ragged line of cloud circling the globe. Sailors knew it as the doldrums, where the wind died and ships could sit becalmed for days.

The rising air drops its rain, then flows poleward high up. Around 30 degrees north and south, it sinks. Sinking air warms and dries (Chapter 2, 2.7), and it builds broad belts of high pressure. Under them lie many of the great deserts: the Sahara, Arabia, the Australian outback, the Kalahari, and the deserts of northern Mexico and the American Southwest.

One loop of air explains the rainforest and the desert alike. The air rises wet over one and comes down dry over the other.

14.7 The Westerlies and the Wandering Jet

For years, Dutch ships bound for the spice islands of Indonesia followed the Portuguese route. They rounded southern Africa and worked their way north across the Indian Ocean, fighting calms and changing winds. The voyage could take a year.

In 1611, a Dutch captain named Hendrik Brouwer tried something else. From the Cape of Good Hope, he sailed south into the strong, steady winds of the Roaring Forties, between 40 and 50 degrees south. He rode them east for thousands of kilometers, then turned north for Java. He arrived in about half the usual time. By 1616, the Dutch East India Company required its captains to take his route.

Brouwer had found the westerlies, the winds that blow from west to east across the middle latitudes of both hemispheres. They are the reason weather in most of the United States, Canada, and Europe moves from west to east.

The tidy loops of Hadley's tropics don't work as neatly here. Meteorologists often draw two more loops in each hemisphere, the Ferrel cell in the middle latitudes and the polar cell near the pole. The polar cell is real: cold, heavy air sinks over the pole and spreads out as weak easterly winds. But the Ferrel cell is more of an average than a wheel. The middle latitudes move their heat with a parade of highs, lows, and fronts.

Where the cold polar air meets the milder air of the middle latitudes, the contrast feeds the polar jet stream (Chapter 5, 5.11). The jet follows the Sun through the year. In winter, the cold air grows and spreads south, the contrast sharpens, and the jet strengthens and drops south. It brings the storm track with it. In summer, the jet weakens and retreats north, and the storms go with it.

Some seasons the jet runs nearly straight. In others it buckles and stalls (Chapter 5, 5.12). What tips it one way or the other is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

14.8 The Seasonal Wind

On July 26, 2005, a monsoon storm stalled over Mumbai. At the weather station in the suburb of Santacruz, 944 millimeters (37 inches) of rain fell in 24 hours, more than many cities get in a year. Trains stopped, streets became rivers, and hundreds of thousands of people were stranded overnight. Across the state of Maharashtra, the floods killed more than 1,000 people.

Yet Mumbai depends on that rain. Most years, the monsoon brings the city, and most of India, about three-quarters of the year's rain in four months, from June to September.

A monsoon is not a storm. The word comes from the Arabic mawsim, "season." It is a seasonal shift in the prevailing wind that brings a wet season and a dry one.

It is the sea breeze of Chapter 2, made giant and stretched across months (Chapter 2, 2.5). In spring and early summer, the land of South Asia heats far faster than the Indian Ocean. The high Tibetan Plateau, heated by the Sun, warms the air above it even more. Low pressure builds over the land, and the warm, humid ocean air flows in. As it rises over the land and the mountains, it pours out its rain. The ITCZ itself swings north in this season, and the monsoon rides with it.

In winter, the land cools, the wind reverses, and the dry season begins.

North America has its own, smaller monsoon. From July to September, humid air flows north from the Gulf of California and the Gulf of Mexico into northwestern Mexico, Arizona, and New Mexico. It sets off afternoon thunderstorms over the desert, with lightning, dust storms, and flash floods (Chapter 11, 11.2). Tucson gets about half its year's rain in these few months.

A monsoon can fail, bringing drought, or overflow, as Mumbai's did. Predicting which kind of year is coming led one scientist to the far side of the Pacific.

14.9 The Pacific Seesaw

In the late 1800s, failures of the Indian monsoon brought terrible famines that killed millions. The British government in India wanted a way to see a bad year coming. In 1904, it put a Cambridge mathematician, Gilbert Walker, in charge of its weather observatories.

With only clerks and mountains of weather records, he searched for anything that rose or fell ahead of the monsoon, and found something strange. When air pressure ran high over the Indian and western Pacific Oceans, it tended to run low over the eastern Pacific, near Tahiti. And when it fell in the west, it rose in the east. The pressure seesawed across the widest ocean on Earth. In 1924, he named it the Southern Oscillation.

Many scientists doubted him, because he couldn't explain what tipped the seesaw. The answer was in the ocean.

In an ordinary year, the trade winds of Section 14.6 blow steadily from east to west across the tropical Pacific. They push the warm surface water west and pile it up near Indonesia, where the sea stands about half a meter (1.5 feet) higher than off South America. That warm pool is the warmest large stretch of ocean on Earth, about 8°C (14°F) warmer than the water off Ecuador.

Off South America, as the surface water is pushed away, cold water rises from the depths to replace it. This rising is called upwelling. The cold water is rich in nutrients, which is why the fishing off Peru is among the best in the world.

Over the warm pool, the humid air rises in giant thunderstorms. High up, it flows east, then sinks over the cold eastern Pacific. Near the surface, it flows back west as the trade winds. This great loop is the Walker circulation, named in his honor.

In 1969, the Norwegian-born meteorologist Jacob Bjerknes put the pieces together. Walker's seesaw and the fishermen's El Niño were the same thing. The ocean and the air push on each other, and each change makes the next one stronger.

14.10 El Niño and La Niña

In 1982, the warmest El Niño in a century began building in the Pacific, and almost no one noticed. That spring, the Mexican volcano El Chichón erupted and spread a haze of droplets high over the tropics. The haze fooled the satellites that measured sea temperatures, making the warm water look cooler than it was. The event wasn't detected until months after it had started. By then, floods were pouring through the deserts of Peru and Ecuador, and drought and fires were spreading across Australia and Indonesia.

The miss led scientists to put sensors in the ocean itself. By 1994, about 70 moored buoys stretched across the tropical Pacific, reporting the temperature of the water and the strength of the wind every day. When the next great El Niño came in 1997, it was forecast months ahead.

Here is how the seesaw tips. Now and then, the trade winds weaken, and the warm water piled up in the west starts to slosh back east. The upwelling off South America weakens, and the eastern Pacific warms. The warmer water weakens the trade winds further, which lets more warm water slide east. The thunderstorms follow the warm water toward the middle of the ocean. That is El Niño.

La Niña is the opposite. The trade winds strengthen, the cold upwelling grows, and a long tongue of cool water stretches west along the equator. The thunderstorms crowd into the far west.

Together, ocean and air make ENSO, short for El Niño–Southern Oscillation. When neither phase is in charge, ENSO is neutral.

To keep score, scientists watch a patch of ocean in the central Pacific called Niño 3.4. When the water there runs at least 0.5°C (0.9°F) warmer than normal for several months, and the winds and rain respond, it is El Niño. When it runs at least 0.5°C cooler, it is La Niña. El Niño returns every two to seven years and usually lasts about a year. La Niña sometimes hangs on for two or three winters in a row.

Half a degree sounds small. Over an ocean that wide, it is a vast amount of heat.

14.11 Loaded Dice

In the summer of 2015, one of the strongest El Niños on record was building, and a NASA scientist nicknamed it "Godzilla." California was four years into a severe drought, and many people expected a drenching winter to end it, especially in the south.

The rain went north instead. The Pacific Northwest got very wet. Northern California got some relief. Los Angeles ended the winter well short of its normal rain, and the drought held on in the south.

El Niño hadn't broken a rule, because it doesn't make rules. It shifts the odds.

Here is how a patch of warm water in the Pacific reaches North America. The thunderstorms over the warm water release enormous amounts of latent heat high in the atmosphere (Chapter 1, 1.6). That heat bends the flow aloft and sends huge waves rippling through the jet streams, thousands of kilometers away. These long-distance links are called teleconnections.

During El Niño winters, the subtropical jet often strengthens and runs across the southern United States, and the southern states tend to be wetter and cooler. The northern states tend to be milder. La Niña tends to bring the opposite: a drier, warmer South, a wetter Pacific Northwest, and colder outbreaks in the northern Plains. El Niño also tends to bring fewer Atlantic hurricanes, because it strengthens the high winds that shear storms apart (Chapter 9, 9.3). La Niña tends to bring more.

These are tendencies, not promises, and ENSO isn't the only pattern rolling the dice. The North Atlantic Oscillation and Arctic Oscillation decide whether the cold air stays locked near the pole or spills south. The Madden–Julian Oscillation, a pulse of tropical storminess that travels around the equator over a month or two, nudges the weather week by week. And a few big storms can make or break a season by themselves.

This is why seasonal outlooks are written as odds. Forecasters divide the past 30 years of a season into thirds: the coldest, the middle, and the warmest. With no signal, each has a one-in-three chance. An outlook might give the warmest third a 50 percent chance instead. Where there is no signal at all, the map says equal chances. That doesn't mean an ordinary season. It means anything is possible.

14.12 A Warming Ocean of Air

In August 1856, at a science meeting in Albany, New York, the physicist Joseph Henry stood up to read a paper by an amateur scientist named Eunice Foote. She had filled glass cylinders with different gases, set them in the sunlight, and measured how warm they got. The cylinder of carbon dioxide grew hottest and stayed warm longest. "An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature," she wrote. Her work was largely forgotten for more than 150 years. She was right. Carbon dioxide and a few other gases let sunlight in but catch some of the heat that Earth sends back out (Chapter 2, 2.2). Since the 1800s, burning coal, oil, and gas has raised the carbon dioxide in the air by about half. The planet has warmed by more than 1°C (about 2°F) since the late 1800s, and the ten warmest years on record have all come since 2015.

A degree sounds small. But picture all the summer days in a place as a hill-shaped curve: a few cool days, many ordinary ones, a few hot ones. Warming slides the whole curve toward the hot side. Cold days grow rarer, and heat that was once extreme becomes ordinary.

Warmer air also carries more water. For every degree Celsius of warming, air can hold about 7 percent more water vapor before it is saturated (Chapter 3, 3.2). When a storm wrings that air out, the downpours can be heavier. Yet the same heat dries soil faster between rains. Warming can sharpen both floods and droughts (Chapter 11).

Natural patterns still ride on top. A strong El Niño can push one year to a record, but the trend underneath keeps climbing.

Scientists now study single events too. This is called attribution. They compare the world as it is with computer models of a world without the extra greenhouse gases. After the heat dome of late June 2021, when Lytton, British Columbia, reached 49.6°C (121.3°F) (Chapter 5, 5.12), one team concluded that heat like that would have been virtually impossible without human-caused warming.

Climate sets the stage, and the weather still writes the scene. A heat wave still needs its ridge, and a flood still needs its storm. But the stage is warmer than it used to be.

Weather Safety

Climate Patterns Are Not Warnings

  • El Niño, La Niña, and seasonal outlooks help you prepare weeks or months ahead. Use them to check supplies, clear drains, and plan for a likely wet, dry, or hot season.
  • They never replace a watch or warning. A tornado, flood, hurricane, blizzard, heat, or fire warning tells you to act now.
  • Don't wait because a storm doesn't fit the season's pattern. A "dry" La Niña winter can still bring a flood, and a "mild" El Niño winter can still bring a blizzard.

For what watches and warnings mean, see Chapter 20. For each hazard's own safety advice, see Chapters 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11.

Field Observation

Build a Monthly Climate Log

For one month, write down each day's high, low, rainfall, and the main weather. At the end of the month, work out the average high and low, the total rain, and the number of rainy days. Then look up your town's climate normals for that month.

Was the whole month unusual, or did a few wild days pull the average? That question is the beginning of climate science.

Try It

Track the Noon Shadow

Stand a straight stick upright in open ground, or use a fence post. Near midday, mark the tip of its shadow and measure the shadow's length. Do it again on about the same date every month for a year, or at least from one solstice to the next.

The short shadows of June and the long ones of December are the tilted planet at work. You are repeating Eratosthenes' experiment, one season at a time.

Translate a Seasonal Outlook

Find the latest three-month outlook map for temperature or rain. Note the season it covers, the chance it gives your area, and whether you are in an "equal chances" area. Then rewrite it in one honest sentence.

"Winter will be warm and dry" is too sure. "A warmer-than-normal winter is a bit more likely than usual, but cold snaps are still possible, and there's no clear signal for rain" says what the map really means.

Chapter Summary

Weather is what the atmosphere does today. Climate is its long-term habits, measured against 30-year normals.

The seasons come from Earth's tilt. The hemisphere leaning toward the Sun gets higher sunlight and longer days. The hottest and coldest weeks lag behind the solstices, longest of all beside the sea.

Water steadies the climate. Maritime climates are mild, and continental climates swing hard. Ocean currents carry heat poleward, and cold currents bring fog and dryness to western coasts.

The winds fall into belts: rising air and rain near the equator, sinking air and deserts near 30 degrees, the trade winds between, and the westerlies beyond. The polar jet and its storms shift south in winter and north in summer. Monsoons bring a wet season when land heats faster than the sea.

ENSO is a seesaw of the tropical Pacific. El Niño warms it, La Niña cools it, and each bends faraway jet streams and shifts the odds of a wet, dry, warm, or cold season. Seasonal outlooks are odds, not promises.

A warming climate slides the whole range of weather toward the hot side and lets storms carry more water.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, the currents have seasons, and the seasons have moods.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Climate: The long-term habits of the weather in a place. Climate normal: A 30-year average used as the yardstick for "usual." Climate variability: Natural swings in climate from year to year or decade to decade. Anomaly: The difference from normal. Polar night: A stretch of days near the poles when the Sun doesn't rise. Seasonal lag: The delay between the longest or shortest day and the hottest or coldest weather. Maritime climate: A mild climate with a small seasonal range, shaped by the sea. Continental climate: A climate with hot summers and cold winters, far from the sea. Microclimate: A small local climate, such as a frost hollow or a warm city block. Gulf Stream: The warm current that runs up the eastern coast of North America and across the Atlantic. Hadley cell: The loop of air that rises near the equator and sinks near 30 degrees. Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ): The rainy belt near the equator where the trade winds meet. Doldrums: Sailors' name for the calm winds of the ITCZ. Trade winds: Steady tropical winds blowing from the east toward the equator. Westerlies: The winds blowing from west to east across the middle latitudes. Roaring Forties: The strong westerly winds between 40 and 50 degrees south. Ferrel cell: The middle-latitude loop in the simplified picture of the wind belts. Polar cell: The loop of cold air sinking over the pole and spreading out near the ground. Monsoon: A seasonal shift in the wind that brings a wet season and a dry one. Southern Oscillation: The seesaw of air pressure between the western and eastern tropical Pacific. Warm pool: The great mass of warm water in the western tropical Pacific. Upwelling: Cold water rising from the deep ocean to the surface. Walker circulation: The loop of air rising over the western Pacific and sinking over the eastern Pacific. El Niño: The warm phase of ENSO, when the central and eastern tropical Pacific warms. La Niña: The cool phase of ENSO, when the central and eastern tropical Pacific cools. ENSO: El Niño–Southern Oscillation, the ocean and air together. Niño 3.4: The patch of the central Pacific used to measure El Niño and La Niña. Teleconnection: A link between weather patterns in places far apart. Equal chances: An outlook with no clear signal toward above or below normal. Attribution: Working out how climate change affected the odds or strength of a weather event.
