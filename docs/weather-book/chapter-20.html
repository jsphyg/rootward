Chapter 20

Becoming a Weather Watcher: Final Projects, Safety Rules, and a Lifetime of Reading the Sky

For nearly forty years, the Reverend Gilbert White watched the weather from his village of Selborne, in Hampshire, in the south of England. He was not a scientist by trade. He was a country curate with a garden, a thermometer, and a notebook. He wrote down when the swallows came and went, when the trees leafed out, how the frost lay, and what the wind and rain did.

In the summer of 1783, he wrote about something stranger. For weeks, a dry, smoky haze hung over the country. The Sun at noon looked "as blank as a clouded moon" and threw a rust-colored light on the ground. White didn't know it, but a volcanic fissure called Laki, in Iceland, had begun erupting that June, and its fumes had spread across Europe. His diary is still read by scientists who study that eruption.

In 1789, he published The Natural History of Selborne. It has been in print ever since.

White's notebook is the best model for this last chapter. You don't need a laboratory to watch the sky well. You need the habit of looking, the patience to write it down, and the honesty to check what you thought you saw. This chapter gathers the habits the book has taught, explains the warnings that keep you safe, and suggests projects for a lifetime of looking.

20.1 The Watcher's Method

On November 8, 1870, a naturalist named Increase Lapham, working in Chicago for the new national weather service, issued a warning that a storm was coming to the Great Lakes. It was the first official storm warning in the United States. Lapham had spent years arguing for it. Ships were being lost on the Lakes every autumn, and he was sure that reports sent by telegraph from towns to the west could warn of storms before they arrived.

His idea was the heart of weather watching: what's happening upwind now is what may happen here next. Everything in this book builds on it. Here is the method, in four habits.

Observe before you explain. Write down what you see, feel, and measure before you decide what it means. "Cumulus growing fast in the southwest, wind shifting south, dew point rising" is an observation. "Storms are coming" is a conclusion.

Look for change. A single reading says little. A falling barometer, a rising dew point, a wind that backs from west to south, or a sky that thickens over three hours tells you something is on the move (Chapter 13, 13.3).

Think in ingredients. Each kind of dangerous weather needs certain things to come together. Ask which ones are present, and which are missing (Chapter 13, 13.6 and 13.7).

Use more than one source. Your sky, your instruments, radar, satellite, the forecast discussion, and the official alerts each show part of the picture. Trust the picture they make together, not any one alone.

For your daily routine, use the one in Chapter 13: the six steps, the morning, afternoon, and evening check-ins, and the three-sentence forecast (13.11). This book teaches it once, so that you can learn it once.

20.2 Outlook, Watch, Warning

In 1887, the US Army Signal Service, then in charge of the nation's weather forecasts, barred its forecasters from using the word "tornado." The fear was that it would cause panic. Forecasters were to speak of "severe local storms" instead. The Weather Bureau kept the ban after it took over in 1890. It was lifted for emergency managers in 1938, and in 1950 the bureau's chief, Francis Reichelderfer, ended it for the public.

Today the opposite fear rules. Forecasters worry less about frightening people than about failing to reach them. The words they use are carefully graded, and knowing them is one of the most useful things in this book.

An outlook looks days ahead. It says where dangerous weather may be possible. In the United States, the Storm Prediction Center ranks the risk of severe thunderstorms on five levels: marginal, slight, enhanced, moderate, and high. Outlooks are for planning.

A watch means conditions are favorable. The ingredients are in place, and dangerous weather could develop, usually within hours, over a large area. A watch is for preparing: know where you'll go, charge your phone, keep an eye on the sky and the alerts.

A warning means the dangerous weather is happening or about to happen, for a smaller area. A warning is for acting: take shelter, move to higher ground, or get out of the way now.

An advisory is a step below a warning. The weather will be a nuisance, and possibly dangerous if you're careless, such as dense fog or light freezing rain.

The simplest way to remember it: a watch means get ready, and a warning means go.

20.3 When the Words Get Stronger

On the evening of May 3, 1999, a violent tornado was heading for the south side of Oklahoma City and the suburb of Moore. At the National Weather Service office in Norman, David Andra watched it approach on radar. A tornado warning was already out. But Andra and his colleagues felt that the usual words were not enough for what was coming. They issued a statement with a new phrase: tornado emergency. It was the first one ever.

Since then, the strongest words have been kept for the rarest, worst events, so that they still mean something when they appear.

A tornado emergency means a large, violent tornado is about to strike a populated area, and there is a severe threat to life. A flash flood emergency means the same for flooding (Chapter 11, 11.2). Some watches are labeled particularly dangerous situation when the forecast calls for an unusually strong outbreak.

Many warnings now also carry tags that describe how bad the danger is. A tornado warning may say the tornado is "radar indicated" or "observed," and may add "considerable" or "catastrophic" damage. A severe thunderstorm warning may be marked "destructive" for the most violent hail or wind, and the most serious warnings can set off an alert on your phone.

Names change, too. In 2024, the National Weather Service in the United States renamed its wind chill warnings and advisories as extreme cold warnings and cold weather advisories, so that the words would describe the danger plainly (Chapter 10, 10.12). Expect more changes of this kind. Read the words, not just the color on the map.

If you see the word "emergency," don't wait to learn more. Act at once.

20.4 Hearing the Warning

On the afternoon of May 22, 2011, a tornado struck Joplin, Missouri. It was rated EF5, and it killed 158 people, the deadliest single tornado in the United States in more than sixty years. The town's sirens sounded about twenty minutes before it hit. Yet many people did not act on the warning or the sirens.

Joplin showed that a warning has two jobs. It must reach you, and it must make you move. Both depend partly on you.

Have at least two ways to hear a warning, and at least one that will wake you at night. Wireless Emergency Alerts, sent by authorities to phones in the danger area, need no app and no sign-up. A NOAA Weather Radio, in the United States, broadcasts warnings for your area and can sound an alarm when one is issued. Weather apps, television, and radio add more.

Don't depend on outdoor sirens. They are meant to warn people who are outside. Indoors, in a storm, with the windows shut, you may not hear them at all.

Know where you are. Warnings are issued for areas on a map. Know the name of your county or district, and the towns around you, so you know when a warning is yours.

Make the decision before the storm. Decide now where you'll shelter, how you'll get there, and what you'll take. Each hazard chapter has its own plan: tornadoes in Chapter 8, hurricanes in Chapter 9, winter in Chapter 10, and floods, heat, and fire in Chapter 11. When the warning comes, there should be nothing left to decide.

20.5 Don't Wait for Certainty

On the morning of September 21, 1938, a hurricane was racing north off the US East Coast. At the Weather Bureau in Washington, a 28-year-old forecaster named Charles Pierce studied the maps and concluded that the storm would not curve out to sea as expected. It would strike New England. At the noon meeting, the chief forecaster and the senior staff overruled him.

That afternoon, the hurricane came ashore on Long Island and again near New Haven, Connecticut. Few people had any idea it was coming. It killed about 682 people.

Pierce's story has nothing to do with bad luck. The possibility was there to be seen. It was simply judged too unlikely to act on.

Every forecast carries two questions, and they are different. How likely is it? And how bad would it be? A 10 percent chance of a light shower can be ignored. A 10 percent chance of a tornado at your child's school, or a storm surge at your beach house, cannot. Chapter 13 called this the reasonable worst case (13.10). Plan for the likely, and prepare for the worst.

Uncertainty is information, not failure. A forecast range of 10 to 30 centimeters of snow is telling you the truth: the storm's track is not settled yet (Chapter 13, 13.8). A 40 percent chance of rain means rain in some places or at some times, not a forecaster's shrug (Chapter 12, 12.12). And a forecast that "busts" for your yard may have been right a few kilometers away, as Fort Collins showed (Chapter 18, 18.12).

Two common mistakes come from forgetting this. One is trusting a single app icon, which hides the timing, the range, and the hazards behind a picture of a sun. The other is waiting too long, for one more update, one more look outside, or one more sign. When a warning is issued, the time for looking is over.

20.6 Projects for a Lifetime

On January 15, 1885, Wilson Bentley took the first photograph of a snowflake on his family's farm in Vermont (Chapter 18, 18.9). He was 19. He kept at it for the rest of his life, photographing thousands of snow crystals, one at a time, every winter, with the same camera and the same patience. His pictures taught the world that no two snowflakes look alike.

Bentley's lesson is that the best weather projects are small, steady, and long. Here are seven, from a month to a lifetime. Each builds on a chapter you have already read.

A thirty-day weather journal. Every day for a month, do the one-minute sky check (Chapter 19, Visual Spread 19.12), write your three-sentence forecast for tomorrow, and score yesterday's (Chapter 13, 13.11 and 13.12).

Follow one storm. Pick a storm days before it arrives. Save the outlook, the watches and warnings, a radar image every hour, and your own notes. Afterward, read the local weather office's summary, and compare it with what you saw.

A cloud portfolio. Photograph each of the ten main cloud types, and as many of the special features as you can find. Label each with the date, time, direction, and what the weather did next (Chapter 19, Visual Spread 19.2).

Map your microclimates. On a calm, clear evening, carry a thermometer around your neighborhood. Compare the hilltop with the hollow, the lawn with the parking lot, the park with the street (Chapter 2, 2.9 and 2.10).

Test a saying. Choose one piece of weather folklore and keep score for a season (Chapter 17, 17.12).

Build a station. Set up a home station, keep its log, and share its readings (Chapter 18).

Make a hazard plan. For every kind of dangerous weather where you live, write down how you'll hear the warning, where you'll go, and what you'll take. Put it on the refrigerator.

20.7 Wonder and Doubt

In the summer of 1752, Benjamin Franklin is said to have flown a kite into a thunderstorm in Philadelphia and drawn sparks from a key on the string, showing that lightning is electricity. The next summer, in St. Petersburg, a scientist named Georg Wilhelm Richmann was testing the same idea with an iron rod when a ball of lightning leaped out of his apparatus and struck him in the head. He was killed instantly. He is often called the first person to die doing electrical research.

The two stories belong together. Franklin's shows the wonder that draws people to the sky. Richmann's shows why that wonder must travel with caution, and with doubt.

A good weather watcher keeps both. Wonder keeps you looking up: at a halo around the Sun, at a storm's anvil glowing at sunset, at the first snow of the year falling past a streetlight. Doubt keeps you honest. Was that really a funnel cloud, or scud? Did the saying work, or did I remember only the hits? Is my thermometer measuring the air, or the wall? Was the forecast wrong, or was it right somewhere else?

Wonder without doubt believes too easily. Doubt without wonder stops looking. Together, they make a scientist, whether or not you ever work as one.

Weather Safety

The One-Page Quick Reference

Each hazard chapter has its own full safety box. This page points back to them. Tear it out, copy it, or put it on the refrigerator.

  • Lightning (Chapter 7). When thunder roars, go indoors, into a building or a hard-topped car. Stay in for 30 minutes after the last thunder.
  • Tornado (Chapter 8). On a warning, go to a storm shelter or basement, or else a small interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows. Cover your head. Leave a mobile home or vehicle for a sturdy building. Never shelter under an overpass.
  • Hurricane (Chapter 9). Run from water, hide from wind. Evacuate if you're told to. Never run a generator indoors or in a garage.
  • Winter storms and cold (Chapter 10). Stay off icy roads. Dress in layers and cover skin. If stranded in a car, stay with it, run the engine about 10 minutes each hour with a window cracked, and keep the exhaust pipe clear.
  • Flash floods (Chapter 11). Turn around, don't drown. Never drive or walk into floodwater. On a flash flood warning, get to higher ground now.
  • Heat (Chapter 11). Get to air conditioning, drink water, check on others, and never leave a child or pet in a parked car.
  • Wildfire and smoke (Chapter 11). Be ready to leave early. When smoke is heavy, stay inside with windows shut. A well-fitted N95 mask does far better than cloth.
  • High wind (Chapter 5). Stay away from trees and power lines. Treat every downed line as live.
  • Folklore (Chapter 17). Never let a saying overrule a warning.
  • Everything. Have two ways to hear a warning, one that wakes you. Know your area's name. Decide where you'll go before the weather comes.

Final Field Exercise

Read the Whole Sky

Go outside at the same time every day for a week. Spend five minutes, not one. Start with the ground: the wind, the temperature, the smell of the air. Then look at the low clouds, then the high ones, then all the way around the horizon. Finally, ask what has changed since yesterday. On the last day, read your seven entries together, and write one paragraph about what the week's weather was doing as a whole.

Final Project

Write a Complete Local Forecast

Choose a day three days away. Using the routine in Chapter 13, gather everything: the sky, your station, the surface and upper-air maps, radar, satellite, the models, the forecast discussion, and any outlooks or watches. Write a forecast for your town in plain words: what's happening now, the pattern, what you expect hour by hour, the hazards and when they're most likely, how confident you are and why, what could change, and what you would tell a neighbor to do. End with your three-sentence forecast (13.11). After the day passes, score yourself honestly (13.12), and compare your forecast with the official one.

Final Summary

This book began at the bottom of an ocean of air, and that is where it ends.

The atmosphere has weight, depth, and currents (Chapter 1). The Sun heats the Earth unevenly, and the air and water try to even things out (Chapter 2). Water changes form, and clouds and rain are what we see of it (Chapters 3 and 4). Pressure differences make the wind (Chapter 5). Air masses collide along fronts, and lows grow along them (Chapter 6). Rising air builds thunderstorms, and a few of them spin up tornadoes (Chapters 7 and 8). Warm oceans feed hurricanes (Chapter 9). Winter storms, floods, droughts, heat, and fire are the same machinery pushed to extremes (Chapters 10 and 11).

People learned to measure all this, map it, and forecast it (Chapters 12 and 13). The climate sets the stage for the weather (Chapter 14). The sky is full of light and color, each with a reason (Chapter 15). Records must be checked before they're trusted (Chapter 16). Old sayings hold a little truth and a lot of luck (Chapter 17). A backyard station, a field guide, and a good habit let anyone join in (Chapters 18 and 19).

And the warnings are there to keep you safe while you watch (Chapter 20).

Final Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Outlook: A forecast, days ahead, of where dangerous weather may be possible. Watch: Conditions are favorable for dangerous weather. Get ready. Warning: Dangerous weather is happening or about to happen. Act now. Advisory: Weather that is a nuisance, and hazardous if you're careless. Tornado emergency: The strongest tornado warning, for a violent tornado about to strike a populated area. Flash flood emergency: The strongest flash flood warning, for a severe threat to life. Particularly dangerous situation: A label on a watch when an unusually strong outbreak is expected. Wireless Emergency Alert: An official warning sent to every phone in a danger area. NOAA Weather Radio: A US radio service that broadcasts forecasts and warnings around the clock. Reasonable worst case: The worst outcome that is realistic, not just imaginable, used to decide how much to prepare.

Closing Note

Gilbert White never knew what made the haze of 1783. He wrote down what he saw anyway, and two centuries later his notes still help explain it.

That is the invitation of this book. You don't have to know everything to be useful, or to enjoy the sky. You have to look, write it down, check yourself, and stay safe.

The weather will keep changing. The sky will keep sending signs. Some will be beautiful. Some will be warnings. Most will be ordinary days that only a watcher notices.

Look up. You live at the bottom of the ocean, and the tides are always moving overhead.
