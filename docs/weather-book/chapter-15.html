Chapter 15

Optical Weather: Rainbows, Halos, Sundogs, Mirages, and the Colors of the Sky

On a November evening in 1883, people in New York, Poughkeepsie, and New Haven saw the western sky glowing a deep, angry red long after sunset. It looked like a great fire burning just over the horizon. Fire engines were called out to put it out.

There was no fire. Three months earlier and half a world away, the volcano Krakatoa in Indonesia had blown itself apart. It threw a fine haze of dust and sulfur droplets high into the stratosphere, and the winds carried that haze around the planet. For months, sunsets around the world burned in colors no one had seen before. In Honolulu, a minister named Sereno Bishop noticed a pale, brownish ring around the noonday Sun, and that ring still carries his name.

Nothing about the Sun had changed. What had changed was the air the sunlight passed through.

That is the idea behind this whole chapter. Every color in the sky, every rainbow, halo, and mirage, is sunlight bent, bounced, or scattered on its way down through the ocean of air. What floats in the air, and how the air is layered, decides what we see.

So the sky's colors are more than scenery. They are clues to what the air holds.

15.1 White Light and Its Four Tricks

In 1666, a young Isaac Newton darkened his room, let a thin beam of sunlight in through a small hole in the window shutter, and set a glass prism in its path. On the far wall, the white beam spread into a long band of color: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet.

Others had seen a prism make colors. Newton proved the colors were already in the sunlight. The glass only sorted them. Gathered back together, they made white again.

Light travels as waves, and each color has its own wavelength, the distance from one wave crest to the next. Red has the longest waves we can see. Violet has the shortest. White sunlight is all of them mixed together.

On its way down, sunlight meets air molecules, droplets, ice crystals, dust, smoke, and layers of warmer and cooler air. It can do four things.

It can reflect, bouncing off a surface the way it does off a mirror or a calm lake. It can refract, bending as it passes from one material into another, such as from air into water or ice. Because each color bends by a slightly different amount, refraction can spread white light into colors, which is called dispersion. That is what Newton's prism did. It can scatter, getting knocked off in new directions by tiny particles. And it can diffract, spreading slightly as it passes around very small objects, which makes rings of color.

Every display in this chapter is one of these four tricks, or a few of them working together.

15.2 Why the Sky Is Blue

In the 1780s, the Swiss scientist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure carried a strange instrument into the Alps: a ring of paper painted in 53 numbered shades of blue, from nearly white to nearly black. He called it a cyanometer, a "blue measurer," and held it up to match the sky.

High in the mountains, the sky matched darker and darker shades. Saussure guessed, correctly, that something floating in the air was making the color, and that there was less of it above him.

The answer came about a century later, from the British physicist Lord Rayleigh in 1871. The molecules of air are far smaller than the waves of light. When sunlight passes them, they scatter short waves much more strongly than long ones. Blue light is scattered several times more strongly than red. So as sunlight streams down through the ocean of air, its blue is knocked off in every direction and fills the whole sky. Look anywhere away from the Sun, and you are seeing blue light that was scattered toward you. This is called Rayleigh scattering.

Why not violet, which is shorter still? Sunlight carries less violet, and our eyes are much less sensitive to it.

The sky pales toward the horizon. There you look through a much longer path of air, with more haze and dust, and the light is scattered so many times that the colors mix back toward white. Distant hills turn blue for the same reason: the air between you and them adds its own blue glow. That is how the Blue Ridge Mountains got their name.

The deepest blue comes on clean, dry days, often just after a cold front (Chapter 6). A milky sky with soft shadows means haze, humidity, smoke, or a thin veil of high cloud.

15.3 Red Sunsets and a Blue Sun

In June 1950, a fire started in the forests of northern British Columbia and spread across the border into Alberta. Over the summer, the Chinchaga fire burned an area about the size of Connecticut, the largest fire ever recorded in North America. In late September, its smoke rode the jet stream across Canada and the Atlantic. Over Scotland and England, people looked up at a Sun that had turned blue, and that night the Moon was blue as well.

Smoke usually turns the Sun red. But Chinchaga's smoke had drifted for days, and many of its particles had grown to just the size that blocks red light a little more than blue. Change what floats in the air, and you change the color of the Sun.

On an ordinary evening, the story is simpler. At midday, sunlight comes down nearly straight through the ocean of air, a short trip. At sunset, it slants in along the bottom, and its path through the air is many times longer. Along the way, the blue is scattered out of the beam, then the green, until mostly yellow, orange, and red are left. Clouds overhead catch that red light and glow. Dust, smoke, and volcanic haze can deepen the colors, as Krakatoa did. Wildfire smoke can turn even the midday Sun orange (Chapter 11, 11.11).

The old saying "red sky at night, sailor's delight" has some truth in it, at least where weather moves from west to east. Chapter 17 tests it.

After sunset, turn around. Above the eastern horizon you may see a dark blue-gray band with a pink one above it. The dark band is the shadow of Earth itself, cast up into the sky. The pink glow above it, lit by the last reddened sunlight, is called the Belt of Venus. In the mountains, that same red light can turn snowy peaks pink, which is called alpenglow.

15.4 White Clouds, Dark Clouds, Green Clouds

On December 7, 1972, the crew of Apollo 17, on their way to the Moon, took a photograph of the whole Earth lit by the Sun. It became one of the most famous pictures ever taken. Africa and Antarctica are there, and the blue ocean. But much of the picture is cloud, brilliant white swirls of it.

Cloud droplets are far bigger than air molecules. Instead of picking out the blue, they scatter all colors about equally, and all the colors together make white.

Yet the same cloud can look dark gray from underneath. It doesn't need different water. It only needs to be thick. Each droplet scatters some light back up, and in a cloud a kilometer or more deep, so little gets through that the base sits in shadow. A cloud also looks darker against a bright sky, because our eyes judge by comparison.

Now and then, a severe thunderstorm turns an eerie green. Scientists who measured green storms with instruments in the 1990s found the color is real. The leading explanation is that the storm is so deep and watery that the light inside it is mostly blue, and late in the day it is lit by reddened sunlight. Blue and red-yellow mix toward green. A green sky means a very deep storm, often with hail, but not necessarily a tornado. Chapter 19 shows one.

When the Sun shines through gaps in the clouds, its beams light the haze in bright stripes called crepuscular rays. They seem to fan out from the Sun, but they are nearly parallel, spreading only the way railroad tracks seem to.

15.5 Inside a Raindrop

Around 1304, a German friar named Theodoric of Freiberg set out to solve the rainbow. He couldn't climb inside a raindrop, so he made a giant one. He filled a round glass flask with water, shone sunlight through it, and traced where the light went.

He found the path we still teach. Sunlight enters the front of the drop and bends. It crosses the drop, reflects off the inside of the back surface, and comes out the front again, bending a second time. Because each color bends by a slightly different amount, the light comes out spread into colors, with each color leaving at its own angle.

For red light, that angle is about 42 degrees from the direction of the incoming sunlight. For violet, it is about 40 degrees.

So no single drop paints the whole rainbow. Each sends you only the one color that leaves it at the right angle to reach your eye. Red comes from drops a little higher in the sky, violet from drops a little lower. As drops fall, new ones take their place.

Your rainbow is yours alone. A friend a few steps away sees light from different drops. A rainbow isn't a thing in one place. It is an angle between the Sun, the drops, and your eye.

15.6 The Rainbow's Geometry

Around the year 200, the Greek philosopher Alexander of Aphrodisias noticed something about double rainbows. The sky between the two bows is darker than the sky anywhere else. That dark stripe is still called Alexander's band.

To understand it, start with where a rainbow can appear. The Sun must be behind you, and rain must be falling in front of you. Find the shadow of your own head. The point it marks, straight opposite the Sun, is the center of the rainbow, called the antisolar point. The bow is a circle drawn 42 degrees around it.

A rainbow is really a full circle, but the ground cuts off the lower part. From an airplane, you can sometimes see the whole ring.

The lower the Sun, the higher the rainbow. When the Sun is more than 42 degrees up, the whole bow is below the horizon, so rainbows are morning and late-afternoon sights. Morning bows are in the west, and since weather usually moves from west to east, a morning rainbow often means a shower is on its way to you. An evening bow in the east means the shower has passed.

Some light reflects twice inside the drop before it comes out. That makes a secondary rainbow, about 51 degrees from the center, outside the first. It is fainter, because some light escapes at each bounce, and its colors are reversed: red on the inside, violet on the outside.

The drops between the two bows send almost no light toward you. That is Alexander's band.

15.7 Pale Bows and Extra Bands

Look just inside a bright rainbow, and you may see faint stripes of pink and green below the violet. These are supernumerary bows, and for centuries no one could explain them.

In the early 1800s, the English scientist Thomas Young showed that they come from light behaving as a wave. Two paths through the same drop arrive slightly out of step. Where their waves line up, they make a bright band. Where crest meets trough, they cancel. The stripes show best when drops are small and all the same size.

Make the drops smaller still, as in fog, and the bow changes completely. Fog droplets are so tiny, smaller than about a twentieth of a millimeter, that the light spreads as it leaves them, and the colors smear into each other. The result is a broad, ghostly arc, white with only a faint red edge outside and blue inside. This is a fogbow, sometimes called a white rainbow. Look for one with the low Sun at your back and a fog bank ahead.

At sunrise and sunset, the sunlight itself is already reddened by its long path through the air (Section 15.3). There is little blue or green left for the drops to sort. The bow comes out almost all red and orange: a red rainbow, short-lived and unforgettable.

15.8 Rings of Ice: Halos

On the morning of April 20, 1535, the people of Stockholm looked up and saw the sky full of rings. Around the Sun was a circle of light. Bright false suns sat on either side. Other arcs crossed the sky. The display lasted about two hours, from around seven to nine in the morning.

Many took it as an omen, and a painting was made. The original is lost, but a copy from 1636 hangs in Stockholm's great church, the Storkyrkan. It is the oldest known picture of the city, and an accurate record of a fine halo display.

Those rings were made of ice. Thin, high clouds such as cirrostratus are made of ice crystals (Chapter 4, 4.3), most of them tiny six-sided prisms shaped like short pencils or flat plates. Sunlight passing through one is bent, the way Newton's prism bent it.

A six-sided crystal bends light by at least 22 degrees, never less. With millions of crystals tumbling every which way, the light piles up at that angle, making a bright ring 22 degrees around the Sun or Moon. This is the 22-degree halo, the most common of all. Its inner edge is sharp and reddish, and the sky inside it is darker.

To measure 22 degrees, stretch out your arm and spread your hand wide. From the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger is about 20 to 22 degrees for most adults.

A halo means there is ice high overhead. Because a veil of cirrostratus often runs ahead of a warm front, a halo can be the first sign of rain or snow a day away (Chapter 4, 4.9). It is a hint, not a promise. Many halos fade with nothing following.

15.9 Sundogs, Pillars, and Arcs

In March 1629, the Jesuit astronomer Christoph Scheiner, near Rome, watched a halo display with a ring, a pair of false suns, and a white band across the sky. Reports of the "parhelia of Rome" reached the French philosopher René Descartes, who set aside his other work to explain the sky's optics. His essay on weather, published in 1637, worked out the rainbow's angle.

The false suns are sundogs: bright spots of color, red toward the Sun, about 22 degrees to each side of it and level with it. They form in flat, plate-shaped crystals, which fall like leaves, lying nearly flat. So the bent light gathers into two spots instead of a ring. Sundogs are brightest when the Sun is low.

The same flat plates act like tiny mirrors. Near sunrise or sunset, sunlight reflecting off them makes a tall column of light above the Sun, a sun pillar. On very cold, calm nights, crystals near the ground do the same with streetlights, raising light pillars into the dark. Light reflecting off the crystals' sides draws a white line across the sky at the Sun's height, the parhelic circle that Scheiner saw.

The brightest colors come from two arcs in the same plates. When the Sun is under about 32 degrees, look straight up for a short, vivid arc like an upside-down rainbow: the circumzenithal arc. Few people notice it because few look straight up. When the Sun is above about 58 degrees, a flat band of color can appear far below it: the circumhorizontal arc, sometimes called a "fire rainbow," though it is neither. It is a summer sight, and rare as far north as London.

The Moon makes all of these too, though they look nearly white, because our eyes see little color in dim light.

15.10 Rings of Water: Coronas and Glories

In 1894, a young Scottish scientist named Charles Wilson spent a few weeks at the weather observatory on the summit of Ben Nevis, the highest mountain in Britain. When the Sun shone on the fog and cloud around the peak, he saw rings of color circling the Sun and, more beautiful still, circling his own shadow on the clouds below.

Back at Cambridge, he set out to make clouds in a glass jar to study them. That work led to the cloud chamber, which shows the tracks of invisible atomic particles as trails of droplets, and won him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1927.

The small colored rings close around the Sun or Moon are a corona. They form not by bending through ice, like a halo, but by light spreading around tiny water droplets, which is diffraction. A corona hugs the Sun or Moon within a few degrees, bluish-white in the middle and reddish at the edge. A halo is a full hand span out, with its red on the inside.

The corona measures the cloud. The smaller the droplets, the bigger the rings, and same-size droplets give clean colors. As the droplets grow, the corona shrinks and blurs. The same diffraction, in the thin edges of young clouds, makes soft patches of pink and green called iridescence.

The rings around Wilson's shadow are a glory. A glory sits exactly opposite the Sun, and from an airplane it often circles the plane's shadow on the clouds. On a misty mountain, your shadow on the fog can look huge, framed by its glory. This is the Brocken spectre, named for a peak in Germany's Harz Mountains. It seems giant only because the fog gives no clue to its distance.

15.11 Mirages and the Land That Wasn't There

In 1906, the explorer Robert Peary reported snowy peaks far out over the Arctic Ocean from northern Canada, and named them Crocker Land. In 1913, an expedition led by Donald MacMillan set out to reach it.

On April 21, 1914, after weeks of sledging across the sea ice, MacMillan and Fitzhugh Green saw it: hills, valleys, and snow-capped peaks. Their Inuit guide, Piugaattoq, told them it was an illusion. The land kept moving ahead of them, and in the evening it vanished. Days later, MacMillan admitted Piugaattoq was right. There was nothing there but sea ice.

A mirage is not a hallucination. It is real light, bent on its way to your eye. Light bends between warm and cold air, because cold air is denser. When the air is layered, light curves through the layers and can arrive from a direction the object isn't in.

On a hot day, the air just above a sunny road is far hotter than the air higher up. Light from the sky, heading down, curves back up through the hot layer into your eye. Your brain assumes light travels straight, so it places that patch of sky on the road, where it looks like shining water. This is an inferior mirage, because the image appears below the real thing.

Over cold water or ice, the layers flip: cold air below, warmer air above, the inversion of Chapter 2 (Chapter 2, 2.9). Now light bends down, following the curve of the Earth. Distant ships and ice appear lifted above the horizon, even from beyond it. This is a superior mirage. When the layers are complicated, the images stack and stretch into towers and cliffs that change by the minute. That is a Fata Morgana, named for the enchantress Morgan le Fay, and almost certainly what MacMillan saw.

15.12 The Low Sun's Tricks

In the winter of 1596 to 1597, the Dutch explorer Willem Barentsz and his crew were trapped by ice on the Arctic island of Novaya Zemlya, waiting out the polar night (Chapter 14, 14.3).

By their reckoning, the Sun should not return for another two weeks. But on January 24, 1597, Gerrit de Veer and another sailor saw the edge of the Sun above the horizon. Three days later, the whole crew saw it. For centuries, people assumed they had lost track of the date. They hadn't. A strong layer of cold air over the ice had bent the sunlight around the curve of the Earth. This rare display is now called the Novaya Zemlya effect.

A smaller version happens at every sunset. The ocean of air is densest at the bottom, so it always bends sunlight slightly down around the Earth's curve. That lifts the Sun's image by about half a degree, roughly its own width. When the bottom of the Sun touches the horizon, the real Sun is already below it, which adds a few minutes of daylight at each end of the day. The lower edge is lifted more than the top, so the setting Sun looks flattened.

On a clear evening with a sharp, distant horizon, usually over the sea, watch the last sliver of the setting Sun. For a second or two, its top edge may flash green. The air lifts the green image of the Sun's edge a little higher than the red, so the red sets first and, for a moment, only green is left. Blue would be lifted higher still, but it has been scattered away. This is the green flash.

The same restless air makes stars twinkle, as bubbles of warmer and cooler air bend their light this way and that.

Weather Safety

Beautiful Does Not Mean Safe

  • Never look straight at the Sun, with your eyes, binoculars, or a camera's viewfinder. To look for halos, sundogs, or iridescence, stand so a building, pole, or tree blocks the Sun. A green flash is watched only when the Sun is a thin sliver on the horizon.
  • A rainbow can sit beside a thunderstorm. If you can hear thunder, go inside (Chapter 7).
  • A red or orange Sun at midday can mean heavy smoke. Check air quality before you go out (Chapter 11).
  • Don't stop on a highway shoulder or walk onto ice for a photo. Pull off somewhere safe, or let the sight go.
  • Light pillars mean dangerous cold. Dress for it (Chapter 10).

For each hazard's own safety advice, see Chapters 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11.

Field Observation

Find Earth's Shadow

On a clear evening, just after sunset, turn your back to the sunset and look low over the opposite horizon. Find a dark blue-gray band lying along the horizon, with a pink band above it. Watch for 20 minutes as the dark band rises and the pink fades.

The dark band is the shadow of the planet you are standing on, cast up into the ocean of air. The pink is the Belt of Venus. Before sunrise, try it again, facing west.

Try It

Make a Rainbow

On a sunny morning or late afternoon, stand with your back to the Sun and spray a fine mist from a garden hose in front of you. Look about 42 degrees from the shadow of your head, two spread hands. Move the spray until a bow appears.

Now take a few steps to the side. The rainbow moves with you, because it is an angle, not a thing. If the spray is fine and the light strong, look for a faint second bow outside the first, with its colors reversed.

Field Observation

Keep an Optical Sky Log

For one month, write down every rainbow, halo, sundog, corona, mirage, and colored sunset you see. For each, note the date and time, where it was compared with the Sun, how many hand spans away, the color order, and the cloud type. Then note the weather over the next day.

To name what you saw, ask four questions. Where is it compared with the Sun? How far away? What kind of cloud? Which way do the colors run? Visual Spread 15.12 has the answers.

Chapter Summary

Every color in the sky is sunlight that has been reflected, bent, scattered, or spread on its way down through the ocean of air. White sunlight holds all the colors.

Air molecules scatter blue light most, so the sky is blue. At sunset, light travels a long road through the air and loses its blue, leaving red. Dust, smoke, and volcanic haze change the colors. Clouds are white because their droplets scatter all colors, and dark underneath because they are thick.

A rainbow is sunlight bent and reflected inside raindrops, 42 degrees around the point opposite the Sun. A secondary bow lies outside it with its colors reversed. Tiny fog droplets make a pale fogbow.

Ice crystals bend light into halos 22 degrees from the Sun, and sundogs, pillars, and arcs. A halo means ice overhead, sometimes ahead of a warm front. Tiny water droplets make coronas and the glory around your shadow.

Layers of warm and cold air bend light into mirages, flatten the setting Sun, and give the green flash its moment.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, the light that reaches us has been shaped by everything it passed through, and the sky's colors tell us what that was.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Wavelength: The distance from one wave crest to the next. Each color of light has its own. Reflection: Light bouncing off a surface. Refraction: Light bending as it passes from one material or air layer into another. Dispersion: White light spreading into colors, because each color bends by a different amount. Scattering: Light knocked off in new directions by tiny particles. Diffraction: Light spreading slightly as it passes very small objects, making rings of color. Cyanometer: A ring of numbered blue shades for measuring the blueness of the sky. Rayleigh scattering: The strong scattering of blue light by air molecules, which makes the sky blue. Belt of Venus: The pink band above Earth's shadow, opposite the Sun at twilight. Alpenglow: The pink or red light on mountains near sunrise or sunset. Crepuscular rays: Beams of sunlight and shadow through gaps in clouds. Antisolar point: The point in the sky straight opposite the Sun, at the shadow of your head. Primary rainbow: The main bow, 42 degrees from the antisolar point, red on the outside. Secondary rainbow: A fainter bow outside the primary, with its colors reversed. Alexander's band: The dark sky between the primary and secondary rainbows. Supernumerary bows: Faint pink and green stripes just inside a rainbow. Fogbow: A broad, nearly white bow made in tiny fog droplets. Halo: A ring of light around the Sun or Moon, made by ice crystals. 22-degree halo: The most common halo, about one spread hand from the Sun. Sundog: A bright colored spot beside the Sun, level with it, made by flat ice crystals. Sun pillar: A column of light above or below a low Sun, reflected by ice crystals. Light pillar: A column of light above a streetlight or other light on a cold night. Parhelic circle: A white line across the sky through the Sun, at the Sun's height. Circumzenithal arc: A bright, upside-down-rainbow arc high overhead, with a low Sun. Circumhorizontal arc: A flat band of color far below a high Sun. Corona: Small colored rings close around the Sun or Moon, made by water droplets. Iridescence: Soft patches of pink and green near the edges of thin, young clouds. Glory: Colored rings around your shadow on cloud or fog. Brocken spectre: Your shadow cast on fog, looking huge and far away. Mirage: A real image made by light bending through layers of warm and cold air. Inferior mirage: A mirage below the real object, such as the "water" on a hot road. Superior mirage: A mirage above the real object, over cold water or ice. Fata Morgana: A complicated, changing superior mirage that stacks and stretches images. Novaya Zemlya effect: The Sun appearing above the horizon when it is really below it, bent by cold air. Green flash: A brief green flash at the top edge of the setting or rising Sun.
