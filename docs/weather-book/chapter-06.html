Chapter 6

Air Masses and Fronts: Where Weather Systems Meet

The sea is not one body of water.

Oceanographers can follow a tongue of warm, salty water out of the tropics for thousands of kilometers, or a cold current flowing down from the Arctic. Each keeps its own temperature long after it leaves home, and where two meet, the sea churns.

The ocean of air works the same way. Air that sits for a week over the frozen north becomes cold and dry. Air that drifts over the Gulf of Mexico becomes warm and steamy. These huge bodies of air are called air masses, and when the wind sets them moving, they carry their weather with them.

Inside one air mass, the weather is usually fairly steady. The drama happens at the edges. Where cold air meets warm, the warm air is forced to climb, and as Chapter 3 showed, climbing air builds clouds. The wind shifts, the temperature jumps or tumbles, and rain begins. These edges are called fronts, and they are where the atmosphere does much of its work of evening things out.

This chapter follows air masses from their homes, watches them collide, and follows the great spinning storms that organize the collisions. By the end, the colored lines on a weather map should look like what they are: places where one kind of air meets another, and something has to give.

6.1 Where Air Masses Come From

An air mass is made by staying put.

If air rests for days over a broad surface that is the same in every direction, it slowly takes on that surface's character. Over snow and ice, it is chilled from below and dries out. Over a warm ocean, it is heated from below and soaks up water vapor. Over a desert, it bakes. The place where this happens is the air mass's source region.

A good source region is big and even, and its winds are light, so the air lingers. The Arctic, the snowy plains of northern Canada and Siberia, the deserts of the American Southwest and northern Mexico, and the tropical oceans all qualify. A mountain range does not, because its surface changes every few kilometers. Neither does the middle of North America, where the next storm sweeps the air along before it can settle.

Air masses are enormous, often a thousand kilometers or more across and several kilometers deep. So an air mass is a sample of somewhere else. When Arctic air reaches Texas, Texas gets a few days of the Arctic.

6.2 Five Kinds of Air

Meteorologists name air masses with a two-letter code that says where they came from.

The first letter says whether the air formed over land or sea. Continental air (c) formed over land and is dry. Maritime air (m) formed over the ocean and is moist. The second letter says how far toward the pole or the equator it formed: Arctic (A), Polar (P), or Tropical (T). The system was worked out in the 1920s by the Swedish meteorologist Tor Bergeron.

Five combinations shape most of North America's weather.

Continental Arctic (cA) air is the bitterest air on the continent. It forms over the ice and snow of the far north in winter. When it plunges south, it brings the coldest days of the year.

Continental Polar (cP) air comes from northern Canada and Alaska. It is cold and dry too, but less extreme. In winter, it brings clear, cold days. In summer, warmed on its trip south, it brings the crisp, clear air that follows a cold front.

Maritime Polar (mP) air forms over the cold North Pacific and North Atlantic. It brings the cool, gray, showery weather of the Pacific Northwest, and the raw northeast winds of New England.

Continental Tropical (cT) air forms over the deserts of northern Mexico and the American Southwest, mostly in summer. It is hot and very dry. Where it spreads out, it brings heat waves and dangerous fire weather.

Maritime Tropical (mT) air comes from the Gulf of Mexico, the Caribbean, and the warm Atlantic. It makes summer nights sticky, and it fuels most of the thunderstorms, tornadoes, and heavy rain east of the Rockies.

The quickest way to tell them apart is the dew point, from Chapter 3. Temperature swings with the time of day, but the dew point changes much less, so a big jump in it usually means new air has arrived.

6.3 Air Masses Change as They Travel

No air mass keeps its identity forever.

As soon as it leaves home, the ground beneath it starts to change it. This is modification, and the rule is simple. The air takes on the character of whatever it passes over.

What matters most is whether the surface warms the air from below or cools it. Cold air moving over warmer ground or water is heated at the bottom. As Chapter 2 showed, warm air beneath cold air is unstable, so the air begins to churn, building heaped clouds and showers. Arctic air crossing the unfrozen Great Lakes picks up heat and moisture so quickly that it can bury the downwind shore in snow. That is lake-effect snow, which Chapter 10 covers.

Warm air moving over a colder surface does the opposite. It is chilled from below, becomes stable, and settles into a flat layer. The result is fog, low stratus, and drizzle. Mild, moist air flowing over the cold waters off California is why San Francisco's summers are famously gray.

Moisture changes too. Dry air gains water over lakes and forests, and moist air loses it over mountains, as the rain shadows of Chapter 5 show.

So the cP air that reaches Georgia is not the air that left Canada. Warmed and moistened over a thousand kilometers, it may arrive as simply "cooler, drier air," which in a Georgia July is a gift.

6.4 Where Air Masses Meet

Just after the First World War, a small group of meteorologists in Bergen, Norway, gave the edges of air masses their name.

Led by Vilhelm Bjerknes and his son Jacob, they studied a dense new network of weather stations along the Norwegian coast and saw that the weather changed suddenly, along lines where cold air met warm. Europe had just spent four years reading about battle lines, and they called these boundaries fronts. The name stuck, and so did their picture of how storms work, which section 6.8 follows.

A front is not a wall standing straight up. Cold air is denser than warm air, so where the two meet, the cold air slides along the ground as a wedge and the warm air rests on top of it. The boundary between them leans over at a shallow slope. The line on a weather map marks only the spot where that slope touches the ground.

The slope makes the weather. Warm air moving against the wedge has to climb it, and climbing air cools to its dew point and builds clouds. A steep wedge shoves the warm air up fast, making tall clouds along a narrow band. A gentle one lifts it slowly, spreading layered clouds over a wide area.

A front's strength depends on how different the two air masses are and how hard the winds push them together. A strong front can bring thunder, a wind shift, and a 10°C (18°F) temperature drop in an hour. A weak one can pass with nothing more than a change in the breeze.

6.5 The Cold Front

A cold front arrives like a plow.

A cold front is the leading edge of advancing cold air. On a weather map, it is a blue line with triangles pointing the way it is moving. The cold air is dense and often moving fast, so its front edge is steep, and it shoves the warm air ahead of it sharply upward. If that warm air is moist and unstable, towering cumulus and thunderstorms grow along a narrow line.

Before the front arrives, the day is often warm and muggy, with a south or southwest wind and falling pressure. Then the front passes, often within an hour. The wind swings to the west or northwest and gusts. Rain comes hard but briefly. The temperature drops, and the dew point drops even more. The pressure, which reached its lowest point as the front went by, begins to climb.

Behind the front, the air is cooler, drier, and clearer, and distant landmarks look sharper than they have in days. In winter, the cold air flowing over warmer ground can stir up snow showers.

Not every cold front follows this script. On some, called anafronts, the warm air keeps rising all along the slope, so rain lingers for hours behind the front. On others, katafronts, dry air sinks behind the front, and the sky clears fast. And some bring no rain at all, because there is no moisture to lift. A dry cold front may bring only a wind shift, blowing dust, and a chill. In fire country, that sudden turn of the wind can send a wildfire racing in a new direction, as Chapter 11 explains.

The fiercest are Arctic fronts, the leading edges of continental Arctic air. Behind one, the temperature can fall 20°C (36°F) or more in a few hours, and wet roads can freeze into sheets of ice.

6.6 The Warm Front

A warm front arrives slowly, and it announces itself a day ahead.

A warm front is the leading edge of advancing warm air. On a map, it is a red line with half-circles pointing the way it is moving. Warm air can't shove cold air out of the way, because the cold air is heavier and hugs the ground. So the warm air rides up over it, climbing a long, gentle slope that may stretch 1,000 kilometers (600 miles) ahead of the front on the ground.

So the first signs appear very high and very far ahead. Chapter 4 told this story in clouds: cirrus, then a cirrostratus veil, then gray altostratus, then the steady rain or snow of nimbostratus, as Visual Spread 4.9 shows. Meanwhile, the pressure falls slowly, and the wind blows from the east or southeast.

Warm-front rain is usually gentle but long-lasting. In winter, it is also treacherous. Warm air aloft melts the falling snow into rain, and the rain then falls into a thin layer of freezing air still trapped at the ground. That is how freezing rain and sleet form, as Chapter 10 explains. The ice often comes many hours before the warm air finally reaches the ground.

When the front at last passes, the rain tapers off, sometimes to drizzle and fog. The wind swings to the south or southwest, the temperature and dew point rise, and the pressure steadies. You are now in the warm sector, the wedge of warm, humid air between the warm front and the cold front coming behind it. In spring, the warm sector is where the ingredients for severe thunderstorms gather.

6.7 Fronts That Stall

Sometimes neither air mass wins.

When the winds on both sides of a front blow along it instead of across it, the front stops moving. This is a stationary front, drawn on maps as blue triangles and red half-circles on opposite sides of the line. It can sit over the same area for days.

A stationary front is not necessarily quiet. Warm, moist air keeps gliding up over the cold side, so clouds and rain can linger. Worse, storms can form over the same places again and again, like the cars of a train passing a crossing. This training is a common cause of flash floods, covered in Chapter 11. One side of a city can be raw and drizzly at 8°C (46°F) while the other is humid at 24°C (75°F).

Fronts are not permanent. They strengthen when winds push contrasting air together, which is frontogenesis, or "front birth." They weaken when the two sides mix, which is frontolysis. A front drawn boldly on yesterday's map may be gone today.

A stalled front has two possible endings. It fades away, or a new storm forms on it and sets it moving again. That second ending is how most of the great storms of the middle latitudes begin.

6.8 The Life of a Storm

Most of the big storms that cross North America, Europe, and Asia are born on a front.

They are called midlatitude cyclones, the ordinary lows of the belt between the tropics and the poles. (As Chapter 5 explained, "cyclone" means any large circulation around a low.) Often 1,500 kilometers (1,000 miles) or more across, they bring most of the rain and snow outside the tropics. The Bergen meteorologists described their life cycle in 1922, and the picture still holds up.

It begins with a stationary front. A disturbance in the jet stream above, often one of the shortwaves from Chapter 5, passes over and starts the air rising. The pressure falls, and the front buckles into a kink, like a wave forming on the boundary.

At the kink, a low forms, and the air begins to spiral counterclockwise around it (in the Northern Hemisphere). The spin pushes warm air north on the storm's east side, turning that part of the front into a warm front. It pushes cold air south on the west side, making a cold front. Between them opens the warm sector. Over a day or two, the low deepens and the rain spreads. Seen from space, the storm becomes a great comma of cloud.

Then the cold front, moving faster, closes in on the warm front. Where they meet, the warm air is squeezed off the ground and lifted aloft. The result is an occluded front, drawn in purple. (This catch-up picture is simplified. The two fronts really wrap around the low together, but the map looks the same.) Occlusion usually marks the storm's peak. Cut off from warm air at the ground, the low slowly fills and winds down.

Sometimes a storm deepens explosively. If the pressure at its center falls about 24 hPa in 24 hours (somewhat less closer to the equator), meteorologists call it a bomb cyclone, and the process bombogenesis. It sounds like a disaster movie, but it is a research term from 1980. Bombs bring hurricane-force winds, heavy snow, and coastal flooding. Chapter 10 covers the great winter storm types, such as nor'easters, Alberta clippers, and Panhandle hooks.

Why do these storms exist at all? They are the atmosphere evening things out. Each one swirls warm air toward the pole and cold air toward the equator. Every few days, another one comes through, doing its share.

6.9 Which Side of the Storm You're On

What a storm does to you depends on where its center passes.

If the low tracks to your north, you get the whole sequence. High clouds spread in, the pressure falls, and warm-front rain begins. Then the rain eases, the wind swings south, and the humid warm sector arrives. Hours later, the cold front brings a line of showers or storms, the wind shifts to the northwest, and the sky clears.

If the low tracks to your south, you stay on the cold side the whole time. The wind blows from the east, then backs around through the north. The temperature barely changes, and the rain or snow lasts longer. In winter, this is the snowy side of the storm.

So two towns a few hundred kilometers apart can live through the same storm and describe different days. One had a thunderstorm. The other had a snowstorm.

This is why forecasters care so much about a storm's track, the path of its center. A shift of 100 kilometers (60 miles) can be the difference between cold rain and 30 centimeters (a foot) of snow.

6.10 The Dryline

On the Great Plains, one of the most important fronts has almost no temperature difference at all.

A dryline separates moist air from dry air. To the east lies humid air from the Gulf of Mexico, with dew points often above 18°C (65°F). To the west lies hot, dry air from the desert Southwest, with dew points below 5°C (40°F). The temperatures on both sides may be nearly the same, but the dew point can fall by 15°C (27°F) or more in the space of a short drive.

Drylines form most often in spring and early summer, running north to south across western Texas, Oklahoma, and Kansas. They often push east in the afternoon, as the Sun stirs the dry air, and drift back west at night.

It is one of the most reliable places on Earth for severe thunderstorms to begin. Winds from both sides flow together along the line and push the moist air upward. But there is a catch. Hot, dry air from the high deserts often slides east over the top of the moist air, leaving a warm layer a kilometer or two up. This is the cap, an inversion like the lids in Chapter 2, and it stops rising air in its tracks.

Through the morning, the cap holds, and heat and moisture build up beneath it. If the afternoon heating and the lift along the dryline finally break through, storms can explode upward in minutes. If the cap holds, the day stays quiet, no matter how much fuel is waiting. Chapter 7 shows how forecasters measure both the fuel and the lid.

6.11 Fronts in Miniature

Not every boundary is big enough to make the national weather map.

When a thunderstorm's downdraft hits the ground, it spreads out as a pool of rain-cooled air. Its leading edge is an outflow boundary, also called a gust front. It behaves like a small cold front, with a sudden gust, a wind shift, and a temperature drop, and the shelf cloud from Chapter 4 often rides along it. It can race well ahead of the rain, so the wind is often the first sign of a storm. Outflow boundaries can travel a hundred kilometers or more and set off new storms hours after their parent has died. Chapter 7 follows them.

The sea breeze from Chapter 2 makes another kind. Its leading edge, the sea-breeze front, lifts the warm land air ahead of it, often under a line of cumulus. Large lakes make lake-breeze fronts the same way. Where two small boundaries collide, the air has nowhere to go but up, and some of the day's strongest storms form at the meeting point.

On a summer afternoon with no front in sight, these little boundaries are often where the storms begin.

6.12 Finding a Front

A front is a place where several things change at once.

No single clue is enough. A wind shift can come from a sea breeze, and a temperature drop from a passing cloud. But when the temperature, dew point, wind, and pressure all change together along a line, you have found a front.

The dew point is often the clearest clue. At night or under clouds, the temperature may differ by only a few degrees across a front, but the dew point usually differs much more. A sticky evening with a dew point of 22°C (72°F) can turn crisp in an hour as the dew point drops to 9°C (48°F).

The wind shifts described in 6.5 and 6.6 are the next best clue, and the pressure helps too. It is lowest as a front passes, and on a map the front usually lies in a trough, a line of lower pressure where the isobars bend into a V.

From above, satellites show a storm's comma of cloud and the thin cloud band along a cold front. Radar can show a boundary as a faint line even where no rain is falling, made by insects and dust swept together in the converging air. Chapter 12 explains how to read these images.

One last surprise: the line on the map is not always where the action is. The strongest storms often form ahead of a cold front, out in the warm sector. A front is a trigger, not the fuel. The fuel is warm, moist, unstable air, and Chapter 7 shows what happens when a trigger finds it.

Field Observation

Catch a Front Passing

Pick a day when a cold front is forecast to pass. Every hour, write down the temperature, dew point, pressure, wind direction and strength, the clouds, and any rain. A phone weather app can give you the dew point and pressure.

Look for the hour when several numbers change at once. That is the front, written in numbers. Compare your log with the panels in Visual Spread 6.5.

Try It

Make an Air-Mass Map

Sketch a map of North America. Shade the source regions of the five air masses from Visual Spread 6.2, and draw arrows showing where each one travels.

Then mark your hometown. Which air mass brings your coldest days? Your hottest? Your stickiest?

Try It

Follow a Front for Three Days

National weather services post surface weather maps online. In the United States, the Weather Prediction Center publishes them several times a day.

Follow one front for three days. Note where it is, which way it moves, and which low it belongs to. Does it strengthen, stall, or occlude?

Fronts make more sense as moving systems than as colored lines frozen in time.

Chapter Summary

An air mass is a huge body of air that has taken on the temperature and moisture of the surface where it formed.

Air masses are named by where they formed: continental (dry) or maritime (moist), and Arctic, Polar, or Tropical. Five kinds shape most of North America's weather: cA, cP, mP, cT, and mT.

As air masses travel, the ground beneath them changes them. Cold air over warm water turns unstable and showery. Warm air over cold water turns stable and foggy.

Fronts are the sloping boundaries between air masses. Warm air climbs the slope, cools, and builds clouds.

Cold fronts are steep and fast, with a narrow band of showers or storms and cooler, drier air behind. Warm fronts are gentle and slow, with thickening clouds, steady rain, and in winter, freezing rain.

Stationary fronts can hold clouds, rain, and repeating storms in place for days.

Most midlatitude storms are born on a front. They grow from a kink into a spinning low with a warm front, a cold front, and a warm sector, then occlude and fade. Which side of the track you are on decides your weather.

Drylines, gust fronts, and sea-breeze fronts are smaller boundaries that often set off storms.

To find a front, look for the place where temperature, dew point, wind, and pressure all change together.

Where air masses meet, the ocean of air stops being calm, and the weather begins.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Air mass: A large body of air with fairly even temperature and moisture. Source region: The area where an air mass forms. Continental and maritime: Formed over land (dry) or over the ocean (moist). Arctic, Polar, and Tropical: Formed over the far north, high latitudes, or low latitudes. Modification: The change in an air mass as it travels over new ground or water. Front: A boundary between two different air masses. Cold front: The leading edge of advancing cold air. Warm front: The leading edge of advancing warm air. Anafront and katafront: Cold fronts with rain behind them, or with rain only ahead. Arctic front: The leading edge of continental Arctic air. Warm sector: The warm air between a warm front and a cold front. Stationary front: A front that has stopped moving. Training: Storms passing over the same place one after another. Frontogenesis and frontolysis: The strengthening and the weakening of a front. Midlatitude cyclone: A large low-pressure storm with fronts, common between the tropics and the poles. Occluded front: The front that forms when a cold front catches up with a warm front. Triple point: The spot where the cold, warm, and occluded fronts meet. Bomb cyclone (bombogenesis): A storm that deepens very quickly. Storm track: The path of a storm's center. Dryline: A boundary between moist air and dry air. Cap: A warm layer aloft that stops air from rising. Outflow boundary (gust front): The leading edge of cool air spreading out from a thunderstorm. Sea-breeze front: The leading edge of a sea breeze moving inland. Trough: A line of lower pressure, where fronts often lie.
