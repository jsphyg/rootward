Chapter 8

Tornadoes: Rotation, Forecasting, and Survival

A tornado is the smallest storm with the biggest punch.

A hurricane can cover a whole region. A winter storm can bury several states. A tornado is often narrower than a city block. But inside that narrow path, the wind can be the fastest found anywhere at the bottom of the ocean of air. It can lift a car, peel a house off its foundation, and erase a street in less than a minute.

Tornadoes frighten us because they are small, sudden, and hard to pin down. Forecasters can name the region at risk days ahead. They can name the county an hour ahead. But they still cannot say which farm or which neighborhood a tornado will cross until the storm is already spinning.

Chapter 7 followed the supercell, the rotating thunderstorm that makes nearly all of the strongest tornadoes. This chapter picks up where the spin reaches the ground. It follows the tornado from its birth in the last kilometer of the storm, through the shapes and sizes it takes, to the way scientists rate it from the wreckage it leaves.

Then it turns to the part that matters most. Forecasting narrows the danger from days, to hours, to minutes. Survival depends on using those minutes well.

8.1 What Counts as a Tornado

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm down to the ground.

Both ends matter. If the spinning air touches the ground but not a storm cloud, it is something else, such as a dust devil. If it hangs from the cloud but doesn't reach the ground, it is a funnel cloud. A funnel cloud can become a tornado at any moment, so it deserves the same respect.

The funnel you see is not the tornado itself. It is cloud. Inside the spinning column, the pressure drops sharply. Air flowing into that low pressure expands and cools, just as rising air does in Chapter 2, and its water vapor condenses into a visible cone or tube. In dry air, the funnel may stop partway down while the spinning wind below it is already tearing at the ground.

So the first sign of a tornado is often at the bottom, not the top: a swirl of dust, a spray of debris, a cloud of dirt rising under the storm. This debris cloud means the wind has reached the ground, whatever the funnel above it looks like.

The damaging wind can also spread wider than the funnel. A thin funnel can sit inside a much broader ring of destructive wind. The visible part is only the costume. The wind is the actor.

8.2 Where Tornadoes Happen

Tornadoes have been seen on every continent except Antarctica. But one country gets far more than any other.

The United States averages more than 1,000 tornadoes a year. The reason is geography. As Chapter 7 explained, supercells need warm, moist air near the ground, dry air above it, and strong winds aloft. In spring, the central United States gets all three. Humid air flows north from the Gulf of Mexico. Hot, dry air slides east off the high deserts and the Rockies. And the jet stream of Chapter 5 races overhead. No other place on Earth brings them together so often, over so much flat land.

The Great Plains, from Texas to the Dakotas, is the famous Tornado Alley. Its season peaks in May and June, as the warm, moist air pushes north. But the Southeast, from Louisiana to Tennessee and Georgia, has as many deadly tornadoes, or more. Its season starts earlier, in late winter and spring, and returns in late fall. Section 8.11 explains why its tornadoes are so deadly.

Most tornadoes form in the late afternoon and evening, when the ground has heated the air all day and the storms are at their strongest. But a tornado can come at any hour and in any month.

8.3 The Last Kilometer

A supercell's spin starts high. A tornado needs spin at the ground.

Chapter 7 showed how the storm gets its rotation. Wind shear rolls the air into an invisible horizontal tube. The updraft tips it upright and stretches it, and the storm becomes a mesocyclone, a rotating updraft several kilometers wide. But a mesocyclone turns slowly, and it sits a few kilometers above the ground. Most never produce a tornado. Perhaps one in four does.

What makes the difference happens in the lowest kilometer of the storm.

Near the ground, the storm makes spin of its own. Along the edge of the rain-cooled air under the forward-flank downdraft, cool air meets warm air, and the air between them rolls over, like the tube of shear in Chapter 7. The rear-flank downdraft, curling down around the back of the updraft, adds more. It wraps that spinning air around the base of the storm and feeds it toward the updraft.

When the updraft passes over this spinning air near the ground, it stretches it upward. A column that turned once a minute, a kilometer wide, gets pulled into a column a hundred meters wide. Like a figure skater pulling in their arms, it spins far faster. That is the tornado.

The rear-flank downdraft has to be just right. If it is too cold, its air is too heavy to be lifted, and it spreads out along the ground and cuts the updraft off from the spin below. In many tornadic storms, the downdraft air is only a little cooler than the inflow.

Why one supercell manages this and its twin does not is still not fully understood. In 2009 and 2010, a field project called VORTEX2 surrounded supercells with mobile radars, weather balloons, and instrumented cars to catch the moment of birth.

8.4 Signs in the Sky

A tornado often announces itself before it forms, if you know where to look.

Look first at the rain-free base, the dark, dry cloud under the updraft of Chapter 7. Under it, a block of cloud may drop lower than the rest. This is the wall cloud, where the storm is drawing in air cooled by its own rain. A wall cloud on its own is not a warning. Many never spin. The dangerous one does three things at once: it lasts, it rotates steadily around one center, and its ragged pieces rise quickly into it.

Other features gather around it. A narrow band of cloud may stretch from the wall cloud toward the rain, like a tail. This inflow tail shows the direction from which moist air is feeding the storm. It is not an arrow pointing at the tornado.

Behind the wall cloud, a patch of bright sky may appear, as the rear-flank downdraft carves a clear slot in the cloud. The wall cloud may wrap into a horseshoe around it. That brighter sky is not the storm breaking up. Paired with tightening rotation, it is one of the strongest signs that a tornado is near.

Then the funnel may lower, smooth or ragged, or appear suddenly halfway down. Watch the ground beneath it for the swirl of debris that means contact.

At night, there may be nothing to see but power flashes, bright blue or green bursts as power lines and transformers fail. A line of flashes marching under a storm can mark a tornado's path.

None of these clues is required. A tornado can arrive without any of them in view, hidden by rain, darkness, or trees.

Many clouds look like these features but aren't. Low, ragged scraps of cloud called scud, and the shelf cloud of a gust front, are common look-alikes. Chapter 19 sets them side by side.

8.5 Tornadoes Without Supercells

Not every tornado comes from a supercell. Several other kinds are weaker on average, but all of them can do real damage.

A landspout grows from the ground up. Along a boundary, such as a gust front or the dryline of Chapter 6, the wind can shift sharply over a short distance and set the air spinning in small, slow swirls. When a fast-growing cumulus tower passes over one, its updraft stretches the swirl upward into a thin, glassy tube, often under a cloud that isn't yet raining. There is no mesocyclone, so landspouts are hard to see on radar and hard to warn for. They are usually brief and weak, but not always.

A squall-line tornado forms in a line of storms like those in Chapter 7. Forecasters often call these QLCS tornadoes, for quasi-linear convective system, a long line of storms. A small kink in the line starts to spin and spins up a tornado in a few minutes. They are often brief, wrapped in rain, and hidden among straight-line winds, and they can form and vanish between two radar scans. Many come at night.

A tropical-cyclone tornado forms in the rainbands of a hurricane or tropical storm, often far from the center and well inland. Chapter 9 explains where in the storm they tend to form.

Two spinning things are often mistaken for tornadoes. A gustnado is a brief whirl along the gust front of a storm, kicking up dust, but not connected to the cloud above. A dust devil forms on hot, sunny, nearly calm days, when the ground heats a shallow layer of air until it rises in a spinning column, under clear sky. Neither is a tornado, though a strong one can still toss lawn furniture and tear up a tent.

8.6 Waterspouts

A waterspout is a spinning column of air over water. There are two kinds, and they are very different.

The common kind, the fair-weather waterspout, is the ocean's version of a landspout. It forms on light-wind days, under a line of growing cumulus clouds, and it grows from the water up. First a dark spot appears on the sea. Then a spiral pattern forms around it, then a ring of spray, and finally a visible funnel joins the water to the cloud. These are common in warm, shallow waters. The Florida Keys see hundreds in a typical year. Most are weak and short-lived, but they can still capsize a small boat.

The other kind, the tornadic waterspout, is simply a tornado over water. It comes from a supercell or a squall line, it can be as strong as any tornado on land, and it can move onshore.

Either way, the rule for boats is the same: stay well away, and move at a right angle to its path.

8.7 Shapes, Sizes, and Paths

No two tornadoes look alike, and a single tornado can change its look as it goes.

Storm spotters use a few names. A rope tornado is thin and twisting, often curved like a hanging rope, and it is common as a tornado dies. A cone is wide at the cloud and narrow at the ground. A stovepipe has nearly straight sides. A wedge looks wider than it is tall and can fill the horizon. A tornado wrapped in rain may look like nothing at all, just a dark gray curtain moving across the ground.

Shape does not tell you strength. A rope can still destroy a house. A wedge can look huge partly because rain and low cloud surround it. And a tornado can grow from one shape to another in a minute.

Size and path vary just as much. Most tornadoes are less than a hundred meters (a few hundred feet) wide and last less than ten minutes. A few become monsters. The widest on record, near El Reno, Oklahoma, on May 31, 2013, grew 4.2 kilometers (2.6 miles) across. The longest, the Tri-State Tornado of March 18, 1925, crossed Missouri, Illinois, and Indiana on a track of about 350 kilometers (219 miles). It killed 695 people, more than any other tornado in American history.

Width, duration, and strength are three separate things. A narrow tornado can be violent. A wide one can be weak in places.

Tornadoes usually move with their parent storm, which in the United States most often means from southwest to northeast. But they can turn, loop, stall, or speed up. Most travel at about 50 km/h (30 mph). Some top 100 km/h (60 mph), faster than many people can drive away on a crowded road.

8.8 Many Tornadoes in One

After a strong tornado, a survey team may find one house reduced to its slab and the house next door with its windows still in place.

For years, people explained this by saying a tornado "skips" or "jumps." The real answer came from the air. Ted Fujita, the meteorologist who discovered the downburst in Chapter 7, flew over tornado tracks in small planes and photographed them from above. In the fields, he found curved marks, loops of flattened crops and scoured soil, curling along inside the main damage path.

They were the tracks of smaller tornadoes inside the big one.

A multiple-vortex tornado contains several small whirls, called suction vortices or subvortices, circling around inside the main column like children holding hands on a spinning playground ride. Each one spins on its own while being carried around the center. Where a small vortex's spin adds to the speed of the main tornado, the wind can be far stronger than anywhere else in the storm, but only along a narrow streak a few meters wide.

So one house can be hit by a subvortex while its neighbor is passed by. The tornado didn't jump. The strongest wind inside it was simply very uneven.

8.9 Reading the Wreckage

Nobody can easily measure a tornado's wind. Instruments in its path are usually destroyed, and the tornado rarely passes over one anyway. So, for most tornadoes, scientists measure the damage instead and work backward.

Ted Fujita invented the way to do it. In 1971, he and Allen Pearson of the National Weather Service introduced a scale from F0 to F5, matching kinds of damage to the wind speeds he believed had caused them. It gave every tornado a number for the first time.

Fujita's wind speeds turned out to be too high for the strongest ratings. Well-built houses failed at lower speeds than he had guessed. So in 2007, the United States switched to the Enhanced Fujita scale, or EF scale. It still runs from EF0 to EF5, but it is more careful about what was hit.

After a tornado, a survey team walks the path. They look at 28 kinds of objects, from homes and schools to barns, power poles, and trees. For each one, they ask two questions. How badly was it damaged? And how well was it built? A roof torn off a house that was only nailed to its walls means a weaker wind than the same roof torn off a house bolted down with metal straps. The worst well-built damage along the path sets the rating.

This means a rating describes damage, not the tornado's true peak wind. The El Reno tornado of 2013, the widest ever recorded, was rated EF3, because its strongest winds crossed mostly open fields. Mobile radar showed winds inside it far stronger than that.

The ratings also come afterward. During a warning, nobody knows what a tornado's rating will be. Most tornadoes are EF0 or EF1. Only one or two in a hundred reach EF4 or EF5, but those violent few cause most of the deaths.

8.10 From Days to Minutes

On March 20, 1948, a tornado struck Tinker Air Force Base near Oklahoma City and wrecked dozens of aircraft. No one had warned the base. Two Air Force meteorologists, Major Ernest Fawbush and Captain Robert Miller, were asked whether it could have been foreseen. They studied the weather patterns behind it.

Five days later, they saw the same pattern again. On the afternoon of March 25, they told the base commander to expect a tornado. That evening, one struck the base again. It was the first successful tornado forecast, and modern severe-weather forecasting grew from it.

Today, the forecast narrows in stages.

Days ahead, forecasters look at the big pattern: a deep trough in the jet stream, Gulf moisture flowing north, and a strong low forming on a front. They can outline a region of several states where severe storms are possible.

The day before and the morning of, they refine it. Weather balloons and surface stations show how much fuel there is (the CAPE of Chapter 7), how strong the lid is, and how sharply the wind turns with height near the ground. That last measure, called storm-relative helicity, estimates how much spin a storm could draw in. Morning storms can spoil the day by using up the fuel, or make it worse by leaving behind a boundary that later storms can spin up along. The real atmosphere always gets the final edit.

Hours ahead, forecasters issue a tornado watch, covering many counties. It means conditions are right for tornadoes. Prepare.

Minutes ahead, they issue a tornado warning for a small area when radar shows strong rotation or someone reports a tornado. It means go to shelter now. Chapter 20 explains watches, warnings, and how to receive them.

Doppler radar is the key to that last step. It can see wind moving toward it and away from it, side by side, inside a storm. That pattern of spin often appears before a tornado touches down. Newer radars can even spot debris lofted into the air. Chapter 12 explains how to read them. The average warning now comes about ten minutes before the tornado arrives. Sometimes it comes much earlier, and sometimes, with squall-line tornadoes, there is barely any warning at all.

8.11 Unseen Tornadoes

The most dangerous tornado is the one nobody sees coming.

Some are hidden by rain. In the high-precipitation supercells of Chapter 7, heavy rain wraps all the way around the tornado. From the ground, it looks like a dark wall of rain, until the wind arrives. Squall-line tornadoes and tropical-cyclone tornadoes are often hidden the same way.

Some are hidden by the land. In forested or hilly country, a tornado may be only a few hundred meters away before it comes into view.

And some are hidden by the dark. Night tornadoes are far deadlier than daytime ones. People are asleep. Rain and darkness hide the funnel. Outdoor sirens, built to warn people outside, often can't be heard through walls and over a storm. This is part of why the Southeast loses so many lives to tornadoes. More of its tornadoes come at night, more are wrapped in rain, and more people there live in mobile homes.

All of this leads to one rule. Do not wait to see a tornado, or to hear one. People often describe the sound as a freight train or a jet engine, but by the time it is that loud, there may be only seconds left. A warning is your signal, whatever the sky looks like.

On a stormy night, prepare before you sleep. Turn your phone's emergency alerts on and loud. A weather radio with an alarm is even better. Put shoes and a flashlight by the bed.

8.12 Why the Safety Rules Work

Every tornado safety rule comes from what a tornado actually does.

Most tornado deaths and injuries are caused not by the wind itself, but by what it carries. A tornado fills the air with broken wood, glass, metal, and gravel moving at highway speed or faster, and it brings walls and roofs down. Most deaths come from blows to the head and neck.

So the goal is to put as much as possible between you and the flying debris. A storm shelter or basement puts the earth itself in the way. On the lowest floor, a small interior room, closet, or hallway puts several walls between you and the outside. A small room has a short roof span, and its walls brace each other. The wide roofs of gyms, auditoriums, and big stores are the first to collapse when their walls fail. A helmet, even a bicycle helmet, protects the part of you most likely to be hurt.

A mobile home offers almost none of this. Even when tied down, it can be rolled or torn apart by an EF1 or EF2 tornado. Close to half of all tornado deaths in the United States happen in mobile homes, so the plan has to be to leave early, before the warning, for a sturdy building nearby.

A car is little better. And the overpass is a trap. In 1991, a television crew filmed themselves riding out a tornado under a highway overpass in Kansas. The tornado passed to one side, and they lived. The video made overpasses look safe. On May 3, 1999, during the tornado outbreak near Oklahoma City, people sheltered under overpasses, and three were killed. The narrow space under a bridge can speed the wind up, and it leaves you higher up, in the full blast of debris.

Some old beliefs are simply wrong. Opening windows to "equalize the pressure" does nothing but waste time; houses fail because of wind and debris. The southwest corner of the basement is not safer than any other spot. And tornadoes do cross rivers, climb hills, and strike cities. Nothing about the ground keeps them away.

Weather Safety

Tornadoes

  • A watch means prepare. A warning means shelter now. Don't wait to see or hear the tornado.
  • Go low and go in. A storm shelter or basement is best. Otherwise, a small interior room, closet, or hallway on the lowest floor, away from windows.
  • Cover your head and neck. Use a helmet if you have one, or a mattress, heavy blanket, cushions, or your arms. Wear sturdy shoes.
  • In a basement, stay away from spots under heavy objects on the floor above, such as a refrigerator or piano.
  • In an apartment, go to the lowest floor, to an interior hallway or stairwell. Don't use the elevator.
  • In a mobile home, leave before storms arrive for a sturdy building or community shelter. Tie-downs do not make it safe.
  • In a car, drive at a right angle to the tornado's path to a sturdy building if there is time. If there isn't, stay buckled, get below the windows, and cover your head. Never shelter under an overpass.
  • In schools, stores, and offices, follow the building's plan, and avoid gyms, auditoriums, and other wide-roofed rooms.
  • At night, have more than one way to get warnings, with alerts turned on and loud.
  • Afterward, stay away from downed power lines, and treat them all as live. If you smell gas, leave the building and call from outside. Stay out of badly damaged buildings, and wear shoes and gloves around glass and nails.

Field Observation

Study a Tornado Warning Afterward

After a tornado event in the news, look up the warnings, radar images, and damage path online. Make a timeline: when the watch went out, when the warning went out, and when the tornado touched down.

How much warning was there? What did the radar show first? Was the tornado visible, or hidden by rain or darkness? This is the safest way to study a tornado: afterward, indoors.

Try It

Make a Home Tornado Plan

Write down your best shelter spot and a backup, the route to it from each bedroom, how you will get warnings, and who brings the children and pets. Put a small kit there: helmets, sturdy shoes, a flashlight, a phone charger, a weather radio, water, a first-aid kit, a whistle, and any medicines you need.

Then practice moving there, and time it. The goal isn't speed. It is removing the hesitation.

Chapter Summary

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air reaching from a thunderstorm to the ground. The funnel is cloud. The debris at the ground is the proof of contact.

The United States gets more tornadoes than anywhere else, because Gulf moisture, desert air, and the jet stream meet over its flat middle.

Most strong tornadoes come from supercells. The storm makes spin near the ground with its downdrafts, and its updraft stretches that spin into a tornado. Why one supercell does it and another doesn't is still an open question.

Landspouts, squall-line tornadoes, tropical-cyclone tornadoes, and waterspouts form in other ways. Dust devils and gustnadoes are not tornadoes.

A tornado's shape, width, and lifetime don't tell you its strength. Multiple vortices inside it explain its uneven damage.

The EF scale rates tornadoes after the fact, from the damage they leave and how well the damaged buildings were built.

Forecasts narrow from regions days ahead to watches hours ahead and warnings minutes ahead.

When the warning comes, get low, get in, and cover your head. Small, fast, and sudden, the tornado is the ocean of air at its most concentrated, and the minutes of warning are the best defense against it.

Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

Tornado: A violently rotating column of air reaching from a thunderstorm to the ground. Funnel cloud: A rotating funnel of cloud that hasn't reached the ground. Debris cloud: Dust and debris swirling at the ground under a tornado. Tornado Alley: The Great Plains region where tornadoes are most common. Wall cloud: A lowering under a storm's updraft. Dangerous when it rotates steadily. Inflow tail: A band of cloud showing where moist air is feeding a storm. Clear slot: A gap of bright sky carved by the rear-flank downdraft. Power flash: A burst of light from failing power lines, often a sign of a tornado at night. Landspout: A tornado that grows from spin near the ground under a growing storm, without a mesocyclone. QLCS tornado: A tornado in a line of storms. Gustnado: A brief whirl along a gust front, not connected to the cloud. Dust devil: A whirl of air rising from hot ground under clear sky. Not a tornado. Waterspout: A spinning column of air over water. Fair-weather waterspouts are usually weak. Tornadic waterspouts are tornadoes. Rope, cone, stovepipe, wedge: The common tornado shapes. Multiple-vortex tornado: A tornado with smaller whirls, called suction vortices, circling inside it. Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale: A scale from EF0 to EF5 that rates tornadoes by their damage. Storm-relative helicity: A measure of how much spin a storm can draw in from the low-level wind. Tornado watch: Conditions are right for tornadoes. Prepare. Tornado warning: A tornado is happening or about to. Shelter now.
