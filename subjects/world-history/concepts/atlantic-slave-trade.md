## Why the trade began

Sugar plantations needed huge numbers of workers doing brutal labor in heat. In the Americas, most native people had died of disease. Europeans already bought enslaved Africans for sugar islands off Africa's coast. From the early 1500s, and on a large scale from the 1600s, they shipped them across the Atlantic.

## How it worked

Ships often sailed a three-part route, sometimes called the triangular trade.

| Leg | What was carried |
|---|---|
| Europe to West Africa | Guns, cloth, metal goods, alcohol |
| West Africa to the Americas | Enslaved Africans: the {{middle-passage|Middle Passage}} |
| The Americas to Europe | Sugar, tobacco, cotton, coffee |

*Notice: each leg made a profit, and each depended on the forced labor of enslaved people.*

## Inside Africa

Europeans rarely went inland. Most captives were taken in wars and raids by African states and traders, then sold at the coast in exchange for European goods, especially guns. Some kingdoms, such as Dahomey and Asante, grew powerful from the trade. Guns made raids easier, which led to more captives. Whole regions lost large numbers of young people.

## The Middle Passage

Captives were chained below deck, packed tightly with little air, food or water. The voyage took weeks or months. Disease and despair killed about one in seven of those who boarded.^[n_slave_numbers] Some resisted by revolting or refusing to eat.

## Slavery in the Americas

| Destination | Share of enslaved Africans who arrived |
|---|---|
| Brazil | About 45 percent |
| The Caribbean | About 40 to 45 percent |
| Spanish mainland America | About 5 percent |
| North America (the future United States) | About 4 percent |

This was {{chattel-slavery|chattel slavery}}: enslaved people were treated as property, could be bought and sold, and their children were born enslaved. Slavery became tied to race, and racist ideas grew up to justify it.

Enslaved people resisted in many ways: escaping, working slowly, keeping their cultures, music and religions alive, and sometimes rising in revolt.

## The end of the trade

Campaigners, including formerly enslaved people such as Olaudah Equiano, fought to end it. Britain banned the trade in 1807 and the United States in 1808. The last large shipments went to Cuba and Brazil, ending by the 1860s. Slavery itself lasted longer: Brazil abolished it last in the Americas, in 1888.

:::practice
q: How did the Atlantic slave trade affect Africa?
answer: It took millions of young people, mostly men, away from West and Central Africa, weakening many societies. The demand for captives encouraged wars and raids, and guns traded for captives made these wars deadlier. Some states, such as Dahomey and Asante, grew rich and powerful from the trade, while others were destroyed by it.
:::
