## Counting before writing

Early cities collected and stored huge amounts of goods: grain, oil, sheep, cloth. Officials needed to know who had paid what, and who was owed what. Memory was not enough.

So, around 3200 BCE in Mesopotamia, officials began pressing marks into wet clay tablets. At first the marks were small pictures of things, with signs for numbers: "5 sheep", "10 jars of oil".

## From pictures to sounds

Pictures are good for things, but not for names, actions or ideas. The breakthrough was letting signs stand for **sounds**. A sign for a word could be borrowed for any word, or part of a word, that sounded the same.

With sound signs, people could write anything they could say. Over time the pictures were simplified into wedge-shaped marks pressed with a cut reed. This script is called {{cuneiform|cuneiform}}, from the Latin for "wedge".

## Invented more than once

Writing was not invented once and passed around. It was invented separately in at least four places.

| Where | Script | About when |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamia | Cuneiform | 3200 BCE |
| Egypt | {{hieroglyphs|Hieroglyphs}} | 3200 BCE |
| China | Oracle bone script, the ancestor of Chinese characters | 1200 BCE |
| Mesoamerica | Zapotec and later Maya scripts | Before 300 BCE |

*Notice: the Mesopotamian and Egyptian dates are so close that historians still debate whether one influenced the other.*

Some scripts are still a mystery. The Indus Valley script has never been deciphered.^[n_undeciphered]

## Scribes and power

Early scripts had hundreds of signs and took years to learn. Only a few people, the {{scribe|scribes}}, could read and write. They worked for temples and kings, which made writing a tool of power: of taxes, laws and royal boasts.

Much later, around 1800 BCE, people in the region of Egypt and Sinai developed signs for single sounds. This became the first {{alphabet|alphabet}}, with only a few dozen signs. It passed through the Phoenicians to the Greeks and Romans, and it is the ancestor of the letters on this page.

## The line between prehistory and history

Writing changes what we can know. The time before written records is called {{prehistory|prehistory}}. We know it only through {{archaeology|archaeology}}. After writing, we have [[primary-sources|sources]] in people's own words: names, laws, prayers, complaints.^[n_rosetta]

**Why it matters.** Writing let a state count its people and goods, send orders far away and keep laws fixed. It let knowledge outlive the people who had it. Large states and empires would have been very hard to run without it.

:::practice
q: Why is the time before writing called "prehistory", and how do historians learn about it?
answer: "History" in the strict sense is built from written records, and before writing there are none. Historians learn about prehistory from archaeology: tools, bones, buildings, art and other things people left behind.
:::
