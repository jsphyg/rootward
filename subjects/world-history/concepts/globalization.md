## A world of connections

People have traded across long distances for thousands of years, along the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean and the Atlantic. But after the Cold War, connections became faster, cheaper and far more widespread than ever.

| Driver | What changed |
|---|---|
| Shipping | Standard steel {{shipping-container|shipping containers}} made moving goods across oceans very cheap |
| The internet | The World Wide Web, invented in 1989, then smartphones, linked billions of people |
| Trade rules | The World Trade Organization, from 1995, lowered barriers to trade; China joined in 2001 |
| The end of the Cold War | Former communist countries joined the world market economy |
| Regional unions | The European Union created a single market, and many members share a currency, the euro |

## Winners

- China became the world's largest manufacturer, and hundreds of millions of Chinese left poverty.
- India became a center of software and services.
- The share of the world's people in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by the late 2010s.
- Consumers everywhere could buy cheaper goods, from phones to clothes.

## Losers and worries

| Problem | What it meant |
|---|---|
| Lost jobs | Factories in rich countries moved to cheaper places, and some regions declined |
| Inequality | Gaps between the richest and everyone else grew in many countries |
| Shared crises | The financial crisis of 2008 spread from the United States around the world |
| Pandemics | The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 spread worldwide within weeks along travel routes |
| Climate change | Burning fossil fuels, which powers the global economy, is warming the planet |
| Culture | Some fear local cultures and languages are being replaced by a global, often American, culture |

*Notice: the same connections that spread wealth also spread crises. A problem in one country can quickly become everyone's problem.*

## Pushback

Some people and governments have pushed back, with new tariffs, limits on migration and calls to bring factories home. Others argue the answer is more cooperation, since problems like climate change and pandemics cross every border. How to manage globalization is one of the central questions of our time.

:::practice
q: Why do some people in rich countries oppose globalization even though it makes goods cheaper?
answer: Many factories moved from rich countries to places with lower wages, so some workers lost good jobs and whole towns declined. The gains, in cheaper goods and higher profits, were spread widely or went mostly to the well-off, while the losses fell heavily on particular people and places. This sense of unfairness fuels calls for tariffs and limits on trade.
:::
