## From hand to machine

For all of history, most goods had been made by hand, using human or animal muscle, wind or water. The Industrial Revolution replaced much of that work with machines and new sources of power.^[n_industrial_name]

It started with cloth. Cotton was spun and woven by families at home. New machines changed that.

| Year | Invention | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| about 1764 | The spinning jenny | One worker could spin many threads at once |
| 1769 | Richard Arkwright's water frame | A water-powered spinning machine, too big for homes, so it needed a {{factory|factory}} |
| 1776 | James Watt's improved steam engine | Burned coal to power machines anywhere, not just by rivers |
| 1785 | The power loom | Machines wove cloth |
| 1830 | The Liverpool to Manchester railway | The first railway between two cities pulled by steam locomotives |

## Why Britain first?

| Advantage | Why it mattered |
|---|---|
| Coal and iron | Plenty of both, often close together |
| Money | Banks and rich merchants ready to invest |
| Markets | An empire and trade network to sell goods to, and to supply raw cotton |
| Farming changes | More efficient farms needed fewer workers, who moved to towns |
| Stable government | Laws protected property and inventions |

Much of the raw cotton came from plantations worked by enslaved people in the American South, so British factories were tied to slavery.

## Life in the new cities

Manchester grew from a town of about 25,000 in the 1770s to a city of about 300,000 by 1850. Workers crowded into dirty, damp housing, often with no clean water.

Factory work was hard and dangerous. Shifts of 12 to 14 hours were common. Children, some as young as five or six, worked in mills and mines because they were cheap and small. Slowly, laws limited child labor, starting with Britain's Factory Act of 1833.

*Notice: the same changes made some people very rich and left many others poor and exhausted. That gap gave rise to new political ideas, such as socialism.*

## Spreading the change

By the mid-1800s industry had spread to Belgium, France, Germany and the United States, and by the late 1800s to Russia and Japan. New industries grew: cheap steel, chemicals, electricity and oil.

Industrial nations became far richer and could build better weapons, railways and ships. This gap in power let them dominate much of the rest of the world.

:::practice
q: Why did child labor become so common in early factories?
answer: Factory owners wanted cheap workers, and children could be paid far less than adults. Their small size let them crawl under machines or into narrow mine tunnels. Poor families needed every wage to survive. Only slowly, as reformers exposed the conditions, did laws start to limit children's hours and set minimum ages.
:::
