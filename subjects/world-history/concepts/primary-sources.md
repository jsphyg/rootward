## How we know anything

Nobody alive today saw ancient Rome. Everything we know about it comes from things that survived: writing, buildings, coins, bones, pictures. Historians call these traces evidence.

For most of human history, nobody wrote anything down. Writing was only invented around 3200 BCE. For the long stretch before that, and for peoples who did not write, we rely on {{archaeology|archaeology}}: the study of what people left behind.

## Primary and secondary

Historians sort sources into two kinds.

A {{primary-source|primary source}} was made at the time by people who took part in or saw the events. A {{secondary-source|secondary source}} was made later by someone studying the past.^[n_both]

| Source | Kind | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A soldier's letter home in 1916 | Primary | Written by someone in the war, at the time |
| A Roman coin | Primary | Made and used in Roman times |
| A textbook chapter on World War I | Secondary | Written long after, from other sources |
| A 1990 documentary about ancient Egypt | Secondary | Made long after, by people studying Egypt |

*Notice: what makes a source primary is when and by whom it was made, not what it is about.*

## Asking questions of a source

Every source was made by someone, for a reason. So every source has a {{point-of-view|point of view}}. That does not make it useless. It means you have to read it with questions in mind:

- **Who made it?** What did they know? What did they gain?
- **When?** At the time, or years later, from memory?
- **Why, and for whom?** To inform, to persuade, to boast, to obey an order?
- **What does it leave out?**

## The silences

What survives is uneven. Rich and powerful people wrote more and built in stone, so their lives are better recorded. Poor people, women and enslaved people appear far less often in written records, and often only through the eyes of others. Historians work hard to fill these gaps, with archaeology, with records such as court cases and tax lists, and by reading sources against their grain.

**Why this matters.** History is not a list of facts handed down. It is a set of conclusions built from evidence. Knowing where the evidence comes from tells you how confident to be.

:::example
title: How reliable is Julius Caesar's own account of his war in Gaul (58 to 50 BCE)?

1. Decide what kind of source it is. Caesar wrote it during the war, about his own campaigns, so it is a primary source.
   why: It was made at the time by someone who took part.
   uses: []

2. Ask who made it and why. Caesar was a politician as well as a general. He wanted Romans at home to see him as a great leader.^[n_caesar]
   why: A writer's aims shape what they put in and leave out.
   uses: []

3. Decide what to trust. The places, the order of events and the general outcome are probably reliable. The huge numbers of enemies he says he defeated are probably exaggerated.
   why: Readers in Rome could check where Caesar went, but not how many enemies he faced, and big numbers made him look better.
   uses: []

4. Compare with other evidence. Archaeologists have dug at sites he describes, such as the siege of Alesia, and found traces of the siege works he reports.
   why: Where an independent source agrees, confidence goes up.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: You have a diary kept by a soldier during a battle in 1916, and a 1990 documentary about the same battle. Which is the primary source? Give one weakness of each.
answer: The **diary** is the primary source. Its weakness: one soldier saw only a small part of the battle and may not have known the bigger picture. The documentary is secondary. Its weakness: it was made long after, and depends on which sources its makers chose and how they told the story.
:::
