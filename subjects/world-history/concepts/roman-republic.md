## No more kings

By tradition, Rome was founded in 753 BCE and ruled by kings. In 509 BCE the Romans drove out their last king and set up a {{republic|republic}}: a state with no king, run by officials chosen by citizens.

The Romans built their system to stop any one person from taking too much power.

| Part | Who | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Consuls | Two officials, elected each year | Led the government and the army; each could veto the other |
| {{senate|Senate}} | Former officials, mostly from rich families, for life | Advised the consuls and controlled money and foreign affairs |
| Assemblies | Citizens | Elected officials and voted on laws |
| Tribunes | Officials elected by the common people | Could block actions that harmed ordinary citizens |

*Notice: power was split and checked. The writers of the United States Constitution studied this system closely.*

## Rich and poor

Roman society was divided between {{patrician|patricians}}, old noble families, and plebeians, the common people. Over two centuries, the plebeians won more rights, including tribunes to protect them and written laws, the Twelve Tables, around 450 BCE.

Rome took much from the Greeks: its gods, styles of building and, later, philosophy and literature. Educated Romans learned Greek.

## Conquest

Rome first conquered Italy. Then it fought three great wars, the Punic Wars (264 to 146 BCE), against Carthage, a rich trading city in North Africa. In the second, the Carthaginian general Hannibal crossed the Alps with elephants and won great victories in Italy, but Rome would not surrender. In 146 BCE Rome destroyed Carthage. The same year it destroyed Corinth in Greece. Rome now dominated the Mediterranean.

## How the Republic fell

Conquest brought enormous wealth, but not to everyone. Rich families bought up land and worked it with enslaved captives, while many small farmers lost their land. Generals began to recruit poor men who were loyal to their commander, not to the Republic.

Rival generals fought civil wars. In 49 BCE Julius Caesar led his army into Italy, defeated his rivals, and made himself dictator. In 44 BCE senators who feared he would become king stabbed him to death. More civil war followed, until Caesar's adopted heir, Octavian, defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra in 31 BCE. In 27 BCE the Senate gave Octavian the name Augustus. He kept the forms of the Republic, but in reality he was Rome's first emperor.^[n_caesar_title]

**Why it matters.** The Roman Republic is the model for many later republics. Its ideas of elected leaders, a senate, checks on power and written law live on in governments today.

:::practice
q: Why did the Romans elect two consuls instead of one?
answer: After getting rid of their king, the Romans feared one person holding too much power. With two consuls, each able to block the other, and both changing every year, no single leader could become a king.
:::
