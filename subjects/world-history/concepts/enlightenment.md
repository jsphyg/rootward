## Reason applied to everything

The Scientific Revolution had shown that nature follows laws that reason can discover. Thinkers of the 1700s asked: could reason also discover the best way to run society?

They questioned old authorities: kings who claimed to rule by God's will, churches that punished different beliefs, and customs that gave a few people privileges by birth. The German philosopher Immanuel Kant summed up the spirit in 1784: "Dare to know!" The century of religious wars after the Reformation had also left many people eager for tolerance.

## Big ideas

| Thinker | Big idea |
|---|---|
| John Locke, England (1632 to 1704) | People have {{natural-rights|natural rights}} to life, liberty and property. Government exists by the {{consent-of-the-governed|consent of the governed}}, and people may overthrow a government that abuses them |
| Montesquieu, France (1689 to 1755) | Split power between lawmakers, rulers and judges, the {{separation-of-powers|separation of powers}}, so no one can become a tyrant |
| Voltaire, France (1694 to 1778) | Freedom of religion and freedom of speech; he mocked the Church's intolerance |
| Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Geneva (1712 to 1778) | Legitimate government follows the "general will" of the people |
| Adam Smith, Scotland (1723 to 1790) | Free markets, with buyers and sellers following their own interest, create wealth best |
| Mary Wollstonecraft, England (1759 to 1797) | Women deserve the same education and rights as men |

*Notice: many of these ideas, such as rights, consent and checks on power, are now written into constitutions around the world.*

## How ideas spread

- Books and pamphlets, now cheap to print.
- The Encyclopédie, a huge French work edited by Denis Diderot from 1751, which tried to gather all human knowledge.
- Salons, meetings in the homes of educated women where writers and thinkers debated.
- Coffeehouses, where people read newspapers and argued about politics.

Some rulers, called enlightened despots, such as Frederick the Great of Prussia and Catherine the Great of Russia, liked the new ideas and made reforms, but kept all their own power.

## Limits

The Enlightenment's ideas of rights were often meant only for some people.^[n_enlightenment_limits] Many thinkers still accepted slavery, the rule of men over women, or the idea that Europeans were superior. But the ideas themselves could be turned against these injustices, and later campaigners used them to do exactly that.

:::practice
q: Why is Montesquieu's idea of separation of powers meant to protect freedom?
answer: If one person or group makes the laws, carries them out and judges them, nothing stops it from abusing its power. Splitting these jobs between separate branches means each can check the others. No single part of government can become a tyrant.
:::
