## The caliphs

After Muhammad died in 632, the Muslim community chose a leader called the {{caliph|caliph}}, meaning "successor." The first four caliphs were close companions of Muhammad. The fourth was Ali.

Arab armies burst out of Arabia. Both great empires next door, the Byzantines and the Persians, were exhausted from decades of fighting each other.

| Dates | Rulers | Capital | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 632 to 661 | The first four caliphs | Medina, later Kufa | Arabs take Syria, Egypt and the whole Persian Empire |
| 661 to 750 | Umayyad caliphs | Damascus | The empire reaches from Spain to the borders of India |
| 750 to 1258 | Abbasid caliphs | Baghdad | A golden age of trade and learning; real power slowly passes to local rulers |

*Notice: in about 100 years the caliphate became one of the largest empires the world had seen.*

## Ruling many peoples

At first most people in the conquered lands were not Muslims. Christians and Jews were usually allowed to keep their faith, as "People of the Book," if they paid a special tax called the {{jizya|jizya}}. They were protected but had fewer rights than Muslims. Over centuries, most people in these lands became Muslims and many came to speak Arabic.

## Baghdad's golden age

The Abbasids built a new capital, Baghdad, in 762. It became one of the largest and richest cities in the world. Goods arrived from China, India, Africa and Europe.

The caliphs paid scholars to translate books from Greek, Persian and Sanskrit into Arabic. A center called the House of Wisdom became famous for this work. Scholars across the Islamic world built on it.

| Field | Achievement |
|---|---|
| Mathematics | Al-Khwarizmi wrote the book that gave us the word "algebra." The word "algorithm" comes from his name |
| Numbers | Scholars adopted Indian numerals, which later reached Europe as "Arabic numerals" |
| Medicine | Ibn Sina (Avicenna), a Persian scholar, wrote a medical encyclopedia used in European universities for centuries |
| Paper | Papermaking spread from China into the Islamic world, and Baghdad had paper mills by about 800^[n_paper] |

*Notice: much of this was building on older Greek, Persian and Indian learning. When Europeans later recovered Greek works, many came through Arabic translations.*

## Breaking apart

The empire was too large to hold together. In 756 a surviving Umayyad prince set up his own state in Spain, at Córdoba. Later, rival caliphs ruled Egypt and Spain. Turkish soldiers took real power in Baghdad, leaving the caliph as a figurehead. In 1258 the Mongols sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph there.

:::practice
q: How did the Islamic caliphates help preserve ancient Greek learning?
answer: Abbasid caliphs paid scholars, especially at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, to translate Greek works on philosophy, science and medicine into Arabic. Muslim scholars studied, used and added to these works. Centuries later, Europeans translated many of them from Arabic into Latin, recovering knowledge that had been lost in the west.
:::
