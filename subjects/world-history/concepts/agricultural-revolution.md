## From gathering to growing

For hundreds of thousands of years, people found their food. Then, about 12,000 years ago, some began to grow it.

They learned to {{domesticate|domesticate}} plants and animals: to choose and breed them until they became crops and herds that depended on people, and people on them.

This happened first in the {{fertile-crescent|Fertile Crescent}} of southwest Asia, around 10,000 BCE. Then it happened again, on its own, in other parts of the world.^[n_independent]

| Region | About when | First crops and animals |
|---|---|---|
| Fertile Crescent (southwest Asia) | 10,000 BCE | Wheat, barley, lentils, sheep, goats |
| China | 7000 BCE | Rice in the south, millet in the north, pigs |
| Mesoamerica (Mexico and Central America) | 7000 BCE | Maize (corn), squash, beans |
| The Andes (South America) | 6000 BCE | Potatoes, llamas |
| Africa south of the Sahara | 3000 BCE | Sorghum, yams |

*Notice: the dates are rough, and they differ by thousands of years. Each region farmed the wild plants that grew there.*

## Why then?

The Ice Age ended about 11,700 years ago. The climate became warmer, wetter and steadier. Wild grains spread, and people who gathered them began to plant them too. Over many generations, gathering became farming.

## What farming changed

Farming produces far more food from a small area of land than hunting does. That one fact changed nearly everything.

- **People settled down.** Farmers stay near their fields, so villages grew up. Jericho and Çatalhöyük were early ones.
- **Populations grew.** More food fed more children.
- **Food could be stored.** A good harvest left a {{surplus|surplus}}, food beyond what people needed right away.
- **Property and inequality grew.** Land, herds and stored food could be owned, so some families became richer than others.

## Not all better

Farming was not simply progress. Skeletons show that early farmers were often shorter and less healthy than hunter-gatherers. Their diet was narrower, the work was hard, and living close to animals and to each other spread disease. A bad harvest could mean famine. But farming could feed far more people, so farming peoples grew in number and spread.

**Why it is a turning point.** Every later step on this site, from cities to empires, rests on farming. Without a surplus of food, there are no cities, no kings and no full-time scribes or soldiers.

:::example
title: How farming led to a surplus, and why a surplus mattered.

1. Farmers grew more grain than their families needed in a good year.
   why: A planted field yields far more food per acre than wild land.
   uses: []

2. They stored the extra grain in pits, jars and granaries.
   why: Grain keeps for months or years if kept dry.
   uses: []

3. The stored grain could feed people who did not farm.
   why: Some people were now free to do other work, such as making pots, building or leading rituals.
   uses: []

4. Whoever controlled the stores gained power over others.
   why: Food is the most basic need. Controlling it is one root of rulers and social classes.
   uses: [causation]
:::

q: Give one way farming made life harder for people, compared with hunting and gathering.
