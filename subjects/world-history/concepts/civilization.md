## Villages to cities

Farming villages stayed small for thousands of years. Then, in a few places, some grew into true {{city|cities}}: thousands or tens of thousands of people living packed together.

The first cities appeared in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, by about 3500 BCE. Uruk was one of the biggest. By 3000 BCE it may have held tens of thousands of people.

## The signs of a civilization

Historians use "civilization" for this new kind of society.^[n_civ_word] There is no single test, but most civilizations shared these features:

| Feature | What it means |
|---|---|
| Cities | Large, crowded, permanent settlements |
| Food surplus | Farmers grow enough to feed people who do not farm |
| Specialized jobs | Potters, metalworkers, priests, soldiers, scribes |
| Social classes | Rulers and priests at the top, farmers and slaves at the bottom |
| Government | Rulers who make laws, collect taxes and organize work |
| Big public works | Temples, walls, palaces, canals |
| Record keeping | Usually writing, to track taxes and goods |

*Notice: almost every feature depends on the one before it. Without a surplus, nobody can be spared to be a potter or a priest.*

{{specialization|Specialization}} is the key change. In a village, almost everyone farms. In a city, many people do one particular job, and they trade or pay taxes for food. This created {{social-class|social classes}}: groups with very different wealth and power.

## Why rivers?

The first civilizations all grew up along great rivers: the Tigris and Euphrates in Mesopotamia, the Nile in Egypt, the Indus in South Asia and the Yellow River in China.

Rivers gave water in dry lands, rich soil from floods, and an easy way to move goods. But using a river well took work. {{irrigation|Irrigation}}, digging canals to carry water to fields, needed many people working together, and someone to plan and direct them. That need for organization helped create governments.

**Why it matters.** Civilization is not a reward for being better. It is a way of living that a big food surplus made possible, and it brought both great achievements and great inequality.

:::example
title: How a river valley could grow into a civilization.

1. Farmers in a dry river valley dug canals to bring river water to their fields.
   why: Without irrigation, the land was too dry for big harvests.
   uses: [agricultural-revolution]

2. Large canal systems needed planners, organizers and many workers.
   why: One family cannot dig and maintain miles of canals alone.
   uses: []

3. Irrigated fields produced a large surplus, which leaders collected and stored.
   why: Controlling the food gave leaders the means to feed workers, priests and soldiers.
   uses: []

4. Cities grew around temples and stores, full of people in specialized jobs, under rulers who kept records.
   why: The surplus let many people stop farming. The rulers needed records to track what they collected.
   uses: []
:::

q: Why did the earliest civilizations grow up along rivers?
