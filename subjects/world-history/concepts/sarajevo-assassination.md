## The visit

In June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, came to Bosnia to watch army exercises. On Sunday 28 June he and his wife Sophie drove through Sarajevo, Bosnia's capital, in an open car.

The date was a bad choice. 28 June was a Serbian national day.^[n_vidovdan] Bosnia had been taken over by Austria-Hungary only six years before, in a move that had angered Serbs (see [[balkan-tensions|tensions in the Balkans]]).

## The plot

Waiting along the route were several young men from {{young-bosnia|Young Bosnia}}, a group of mostly student revolutionaries. They wanted to end Austro-Hungarian rule over the {{south-slavs|South Slavs}}. Most of them, including 19-year-old Gavrilo Princip, were Bosnian Serbs.

Their pistols and bombs came from Serbia. Officers of the {{black-hand|Black Hand}}, a secret society inside the Serbian army, armed them and helped them cross the border.

## The day

In the morning, one plotter threw a bomb at the archduke's car. It missed, bounced off and exploded under the next car, wounding several people. The archduke's car sped on.

Later that day, the archduke set off to visit the wounded in hospital. His driver took a wrong turn and stopped to reverse. By chance, Princip was standing nearby. He stepped forward and fired twice, killing both Franz Ferdinand and Sophie.^[n_princip]

## Who was responsible?

Princip and his friends were subjects of Austria-Hungary, not of Serbia. But their weapons came from Serbian officers. The head of the Black Hand was also the head of Serbia's military intelligence.

How much the Serbian government itself knew is still debated.^[n_serbian_govt] Austria-Hungary did not wait to find out. Its leaders saw a chance to deal with Serbia once and for all.

**Why one killing could matter so much.** On its own, a murder does not cause a war. This one landed in a region already full of tension, between two countries that already distrusted each other. It gave Austria-Hungary a reason to act on a fear it already had.
