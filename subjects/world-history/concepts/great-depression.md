## The boom

In the 1920s the United States was the richest country in the world. Factories turned out cars, radios and refrigerators. Many people bought shares on the stock market with borrowed money, expecting prices to keep rising.

Europe depended on American money. Under a plan agreed in 1924, American loans helped Germany pay reparations to Britain and France, which then repaid their own war debts to the United States.

*Notice: the money went around in a circle. If the American loans stopped, the whole chain would break.*

## The crash

In October 1929 share prices on Wall Street, New York's stock market, began to fall, then collapsed. On 29 October, "Black Tuesday," panic selling wiped out fortunes.

| What happened | Why it spread |
|---|---|
| Banks failed | They had lent money for shares, and people rushed to take out their savings. About 9,000 American banks closed between 1930 and 1933 |
| Businesses closed | People and companies stopped spending, so factories cut workers |
| Loans were recalled | American banks called back loans from Europe, especially Germany |
| Trade collapsed | Countries raised {{tariff|tariffs}} on imports to protect their own industries; others did the same, and world trade fell by about two thirds |

## Hard times

| Country | Unemployment at the worst |
|---|---|
| United States | About 25 percent, in 1933 |
| Germany | About 30 percent, with 6 million out of work, in 1932 |
| Britain | About 20 percent of insured workers |

Families lost homes and farms. Hungry people stood in bread lines. In the American Midwest, drought and dust storms, the Dust Bowl, drove farm families from their land.

## Different answers

| Country | Response |
|---|---|
| United States | President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, from 1933: public works jobs, bank rules, unemployment insurance and old-age pensions |
| Germany | Desperate voters turned to the Nazis, who promised jobs, order and national revival |
| Soviet Union | Its planned economy was cut off from world markets and its leaders pointed to the crisis as proof that capitalism was failing |

The British economist John Maynard Keynes argued that in a slump, governments should spend more, even if they had to borrow, to put people back to work. His ideas shaped economic policy for decades.

:::practice
q: Why did raising tariffs make the Depression worse?
answer: Each country raised taxes on imports to protect its own industries. But other countries did the same in return, so every country's exports fell. With world trade collapsing by about two thirds, factories that depended on selling abroad closed, and even more people lost their jobs.
:::
