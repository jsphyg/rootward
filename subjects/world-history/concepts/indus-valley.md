## Rediscovered cities

For thousands of years this civilization was forgotten. Then, in the 1920s, archaeologists digging at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, in today's Pakistan, found the ruins of great cities. It is often called the Harappan civilization after the first city found.

At its height, about 2600 to 1900 BCE, it covered a larger area than Egypt or Mesopotamia.

## Planned cities

The cities stand out for how carefully they were planned.

| What archaeologists found | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Streets laid out in a grid | Someone planned the cities in advance |
| Baked bricks of the same proportions in many cities | Shared rules across a wide area |
| Covered drains and bathrooms in houses | Strong concern with water and cleanliness |
| A large public pool, the "Great Bath" | Perhaps bathing rituals, but we do not know |
| Standard stone weights | Organized trade, with agreed measures |

*Notice: every line in the right-hand column is a guess from objects. Without texts, that is all archaeology can give.*

Indus merchants traded far away. Their stamp {{seal|seals}} have been found in Mesopotamia.

## The great puzzle

The seals carry short inscriptions in the Indus script. No one has been able to {{decipher|decipher}} it.^[n_undeciphered] So we do not know what language these people spoke, what they called themselves, or who ruled them.

Strangely, archaeologists have found no grand palaces or royal tombs and few weapons. Power may have been organized differently from Egypt or Mesopotamia, but this is uncertain.

## Decline

After about 1900 BCE, the cities slowly emptied. Most historians now link this to changes in climate and rivers, which shifted course or dried up, together with a decline in trade. An older idea, that invaders destroyed the cities, has been largely rejected because there is little evidence of conquest.

**Why it matters.** The Indus Valley shows how much we can learn from objects alone, and how much we cannot. It is also a reminder that early civilization was not only a story of kings and wars.

:::practice
q: Why have most historians dropped the idea that invaders destroyed the Indus cities?
answer: The evidence shows a slow decline over centuries, not sudden destruction. There are few signs of attack. Changes in climate and in the rivers, and falling trade, fit the evidence better.
:::
