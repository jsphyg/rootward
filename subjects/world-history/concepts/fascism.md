## What fascism is

{{fascism|Fascism}} takes its name from the fasces, a bundle of rods carried by officials in ancient Rome, a symbol of strength through unity.

| Fascist belief | What it meant |
|---|---|
| The nation above all | Individuals exist to serve the nation |
| One leader | A single leader embodies the nation's will and must be obeyed |
| Against democracy | Elections and debate are weak and divisive |
| Against communism | Fascists promised to destroy the left |
| Violence | Uniformed gangs beat and killed opponents; war is glorious |
| Enemies | The nation's troubles are blamed on outsiders and minorities |

It is a form of {{totalitarianism|totalitarianism}}: the state tries to control every part of life.

## Mussolini's Italy

Italy had been on the winning side in World War I but felt cheated of the land it was promised. Strikes and unrest followed. In 1922 Benito Mussolini's fascist Blackshirts threatened a "March on Rome," and the king appointed Mussolini prime minister. By 1926 he ruled as a dictator.

## Hitler's rise

Adolf Hitler, an Austrian-born veteran of World War I, led the National Socialist German Workers' Party, the Nazis. In his book Mein Kampf, he blamed Germany's problems on Jews and communists and called for Germany to win "living space" in the east.

| Election | Nazi share of the vote |
|---|---|
| 1928 | About 3 percent |
| 1930 | About 18 percent |
| July 1932 | About 37 percent, the largest party |

*Notice: the Nazis' vote soared only after the Depression struck. In good times, few Germans voted for them.*

## From chancellor to dictator

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 30 January 1933 | President Hindenburg appoints Hitler chancellor; conservative politicians think they can control him |
| February 1933 | The parliament building, the Reichstag, burns; Hitler uses it to suspend civil rights |
| March 1933 | The Enabling Act lets Hitler make laws without parliament |
| 1933 to 1934 | Other parties and unions banned; Hitler has rivals within his own party murdered |
| 1935 | The Nuremberg Laws strip Jews of citizenship |
| November 1938 | Kristallnacht: Nazis attack synagogues, Jewish shops and homes across Germany |

The secret police, the Gestapo, arrested opponents and sent them to concentration camps. Propaganda filled newspapers, radio and films. Big public works and rearmament brought down unemployment, which won Hitler more support.

## Elsewhere

Militarists gained power in Japan in the 1930s. In Spain, General Francisco Franco won a civil war from 1936 to 1939, helped by Hitler and Mussolini.

:::practice
q: How did the Treaty of Versailles help the Nazis?
answer: Many Germans saw the treaty as a humiliation: blame for the war, lost land, a tiny army and heavy reparations. Hitler promised to tear it up and restore Germany's pride and power. He also spread the false claim that Germany had been betrayed by Jews and socialists in 1918. The treaty's unpopularity gave his message a ready audience.
:::
