## A web, not a road

The Silk Roads were not one road. They were a web of routes across deserts, mountains and grasslands, linking China, Central Asia, India, Persia and the Mediterranean. The name was invented much later, in 1877, by a German geographer.^[n_silk_name]

They opened in the Han dynasty. Around 138 BCE, the emperor sent an envoy, Zhang Qian, west to find allies. He brought back news of rich kingdoms and fine horses, and trade grew.

## Passed hand to hand

Almost no one traveled the whole route. Goods moved in stages through a chain of {{caravan|caravans}} and middlemen, especially Persian and Central Asian traders. Oasis cities such as Samarkand and Kashgar grew rich from the trade.

| Going west | Going east |
|---|---|
| Silk, paper, porcelain (later), tea (later) | Horses, glass, gold and silver |
| Spices from India and Southeast Asia | Wool, wine, grapes |

*Notice: each side wanted what it could not easily make. Rome had no idea how silk was made; China prized the strong horses of Central Asia.*

Rich Romans loved Chinese silk. Yet Rome and China knew of each other only by rumor. A Chinese envoy reached the Persian Gulf around 97 CE but turned back.

## More than goods

The most important things carried were often not goods.

- **Religions.** Buddhism spread from India to China in the first centuries CE. Later, Christianity and Islam traveled the routes too.
- **Inventions.** Papermaking spread west, reaching the Islamic world in the 700s and, much later, Europe.
- **Disease.** Germs traveled with traders. Plagues could spread along the routes.

**Why it matters.** The Silk Roads were an early form of {{globalization|globalization}}: distant peoples, who never met, became connected by trade. Religions, technologies and art traveled thousands of kilometers, shaping civilizations across Asia and Europe.

title: How a bolt of Chinese silk could end up in Rome.

1. Chinese merchants sold the silk to traders at China's western frontier.
   why: Each merchant knew only their own section of the route.
   uses: [qin-han-china]

2. Central Asian traders carried it by camel caravan across deserts to cities such as Samarkand.
   why: Camels could cross dry land that other animals could not.
   uses: []

3. Persian merchants bought it and carried it west toward the Mediterranean.
   why: Persia sat between east and west and profited as the middleman.
   uses: []

4. Traders in Syria or Egypt sold it to Romans, at many times its original price.
   why: Each middleman added profit and transport costs, so silk was a luxury by the time it reached Rome.
