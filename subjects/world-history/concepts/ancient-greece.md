## A land of mountains and sea

Greece is a land of rocky mountains, small valleys and many islands. Travel over land was hard; travel by sea was easier. So the Greeks never formed one country. Instead they lived in hundreds of independent {{city-state|city-states}}, each called a polis.

Each polis had its own government, laws and army. They often fought each other. But they shared a language, the same gods, and festivals such as the Olympic Games, first held, by tradition, in 776 BCE. Greeks thought of all non-Greek speakers as outsiders.

## Athens and Sparta

The two most famous city-states were very different.

| | Athens | Sparta |
|---|---|---|
| Government | {{democracy|Democracy}}: citizens voted on laws | Two kings and a council of elders |
| What it valued | Trade, art, ideas, the sea | Military discipline, obedience |
| Boys' education | Reading, music, sport, speaking | Army training from age 7 |
| Who did the farm work | Citizens, and many enslaved people | Helots, a huge enslaved population |

*Notice: both societies depended on enslaved labor. Freedom for some rested on unfreedom for others.*

Athens' democracy began around 508 BCE. Every adult male {{citizen|citizen}} could speak and vote in the assembly. It was a direct democracy, where citizens voted on laws themselves.

## War with Persia, and with each other

When Persia invaded, the Greek city-states united and won, at Marathon (490 BCE) and Salamis (480 BCE). Afterwards Athens grew rich and powerful, and in its golden age built the Parthenon temple.

But Athens and Sparta became rivals. Their long war, the Peloponnesian War (431 to 404 BCE), ended in Athens' defeat and left Greece weakened and divided.

## Ideas that lasted

Greeks asked big questions about nature, truth, justice and how to live. This kind of thinking is called {{philosophy|philosophy}}. Socrates questioned everything and was put to death by Athens in 399 BCE. His student Plato wrote about the ideal state. Plato's student Aristotle wrote on science, politics and logic.

Greeks also created theater, both tragedy and comedy, and the writing of history. Herodotus, who wrote about the Persian Wars, is often called the "father of history".

**Why it matters.** Democracy, philosophy, theater and the study of history all have Greek roots. Rome borrowed heavily from Greece, and through Rome these ideas passed into Europe and the wider world.

:::practice
q: In what ways was Athenian democracy different from democracy in most countries today?
answer: It was direct: citizens voted on laws themselves, instead of electing representatives. And it was limited: only adult male citizens could take part. Women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded, so most adults in Athens had no vote.
:::
