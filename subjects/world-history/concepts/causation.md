## More than one cause

Ask "why did this happen?" about almost any event, and you will find more than one answer.

Think of a forest fire. It might need months of dry weather, a strong wind and a lightning strike. Take away any one of them and there may be no fire, or a much smaller one. History works the same way.

## Long-term causes and triggers

Historians often sort causes by how long they took to build up.

| Kind of cause | What it does | Forest fire | World War I |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Builds up over years and makes the event possible | Months of dry weather | Rival alliances, nationalism |
| Trigger | Sets the event off at one moment | A lightning strike | The killing of an archduke in 1914 |

A {{long-term-cause|long-term cause}} is a condition, such as debt, rivalry or a new idea spreading. A {{trigger|trigger}} is an event. Without the conditions, the trigger might have changed little. In 1914, [[sarajevo-assassination|one assassination]] could start a world war only because so much tension had already built up.

## First is not the same as cause

Just because one thing happened before another does not mean it caused it.^[n_post_hoc] To show a cause, you need a link: a way the first thing actually led to the second. Evidence for that link comes from sources.

## Causes become consequences

Every event has {{consequence|consequences}}, and those consequences become causes of later events. That is why history is a web, not a list. Each page on this site that leads to another is one strand of that web.

**Why historians argue.** Historians usually agree on the list of causes. They disagree about which mattered most. Weighing causes is a judgment, made from evidence, and new evidence can change it.

:::example
title: Sort the causes of the French Revolution of 1789.

1. France's government was deeply in debt, partly from paying for wars, including its help to the American revolutionaries.
   why: Debt built up over decades. It is a long-term cause: it made a crisis possible.
   uses: []

2. Taxes fell mostly on ordinary people, while nobles and the Church paid little.
   why: Resentment grew over a long time. Another long-term cause.
   uses: []

3. New ideas spread that rulers' power should come from the people and that all should be equal before the law.
   why: Ideas take years to spread. They gave people reasons to demand change, so they are long-term causes too.
   uses: []

4. A bad harvest in 1788 made bread very expensive. In May 1789 an assembly the king had called to fix the debt, the Estates-General, met for the first time in 175 years.
   why: These were short-term causes. The meeting gave angry people a place to act, and it set the Revolution off.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A student writes: "The western Roman Empire fell in 476 CE because its last emperor was a teenager." What is wrong with this as an explanation?
answer: It names, at most, a detail of the final moment, not the causes. The last emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was a teenager who was removed by a Germanic general, Odoacer. But the empire was already very weak after long-term problems: years of invasions, civil wars, money troubles and a split between east and west. Those long-term causes made the fall possible.
:::
