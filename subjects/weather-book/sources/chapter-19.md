# Chapter 19: Notes on Sources

*Kept out of the chapter file to match Chapters 13 to 18.*

- **Roger Tory Peterson** (*A Field Guide to the Birds*, 1934; first printing 2,000 copies, sold out within a week; the Peterson Identification System based on field marks): Wikipedia, "Roger Tory Peterson." The chapter does not say his plates used arrows, because that source did not confirm it for the first edition.
- **First International Cloud Atlas** (1896; Hildebrandsson, Riggenbach, and Teisserenc de Bort for the International Meteorological Committee's Clouds Commission) and the **2017 edition**: Wikipedia, "International Cloud Atlas."
- **John Constable** ("I have done a good deal of skying," letter to Fisher, October 23, 1821; notes on the backs of sky studies giving weather, light direction, and time of day; influence of Luke Howard): Wikipedia, "John Constable." The chapter doesn't give a count of studies or say "Hampstead," because that source didn't confirm them.
- **Contrails after September 11, 2001** (about 4,000 US stations; diurnal temperature range widened about 1.1°C, the largest in 30 years; later studies attributed it to natural cloud changes): Wikipedia, "Global dimming." The original study is Travis, Carleton, and Lauritsen, *Nature* 418 (2002); its authors were not confirmed from the fetched page, so the chapter doesn't name them.
- **William Rankin** (July 26, 1959; F-8 Crusader; engine failure at about 47,000 ft, 14,300 m, over a thunderstorm; ejected into about −50°C; about 40 minutes aloft; landed in a North Carolina forest): Wikipedia, "William Rankin." The hail, rain, and lightning come from his own account as widely retold.
- **Asperitas** (Jane Wiggins, Cedar Rapids, Iowa, June 20, 2006; Cloud Appreciation Society and Gavin Pretor-Pinney; proposed 2009; accepted into the Atlas in March 2017 as a supplementary feature; the first formation added since cirrus intortus in 1951; usually fades without a storm): Wikipedia, "Asperitas (cloud)."
- **Wall cloud and Fujita** (first identified and linked to tornadoes by Ted Fujita after a detailed study of the 1957 Fargo tornado): Wikipedia, "Wall cloud." The chapter says he "identified" it rather than "named" it, following that source.
- **George Alford's photographs** (Wills Point, Texas, tornado of May 25, 1907; "some of the first-ever photographs of a tornado"): Wikipedia, "History of tornado research." The chapter says "among the first," not "the first."
- **Wokingham storm** (1959; studied by Keith Browning and Frank Ludlam, 1962; the first storm identified as a supercell type): Wikipedia, "Supercell." Who first used the word "supercell" was not confirmed, so the chapter doesn't say.
- **Morning Glory** (southern Gulf of Carpentaria, Burketown; late September to early November; up to 1,000 km long, often 100 to 200 m above the ground, 10 to 20 m/s; glider pilots ride it): Wikipedia, "Morning Glory cloud."
- **mPING** (free app from NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory for public precipitation-type reports, released in the winter of 2012 to 2013; reports compared with dual-polarization radar): NOAA, "New smart phone app lets public report rain, hail, sleet and snow to NOAA"; NSSL news, January 2013.
- **Black Sunday** (April 14, 1935; Oklahoma and Texas panhandles; an estimated 300,000 tons of topsoil; Robert E. Geiger and "the Dust Bowl"): Wikipedia, "Black Sunday (storm)."
- **Vincent Schaefer** (dry-ice discovery in his cold box at General Electric, summer 1946; seeded clouds over the Berkshires from an airplane, November 13, 1946; *A Field Guide to the Atmosphere*, 1981, with John Day, in the Peterson Field Guide series): Wikipedia, "Vincent Schaefer."
- **Dust-storm driving advice** (pull off, lights off, foot off the brake): the NWS "Pull Aside, Stay Alive" guidance, standard.
