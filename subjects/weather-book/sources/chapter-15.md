# Chapter 15: Notes on Sources

*Kept out of the chapter file to match Chapters 13 and 14.*

- Krakatoa sunsets and fire engines in New York, Poughkeepsie, and New Haven: Wikipedia, "1883 eruption of Krakatoa" (the chapter says "a November evening" because the exact date was not confirmed). Bishop's ring named for Sereno Bishop of Honolulu, who reported it in 1883 (standard atmospheric-optics histories).
- Newton's prism (1666, published 1672): standard histories of Newton's optics.
- Saussure's cyanometer (53 shades, Alps and Mont Blanc measurements): Wikipedia, "Cyanometer"; Nautilus, "The Mountain Man Who Measured the Sky's Brilliance." The chapter avoids a specific summit reading, which could not be confirmed.
- Rayleigh scattering (1871) and why the sky isn't violet: standard physics references.
- Blue Sun of September 1950: Wikipedia, "Chinchaga fire" (14,000 to 17,000 km², northern British Columbia and Alberta, plume over Scotland September 27, 1950; blue suns and moons in Europe and the US); Wilson, "The blue Sun of 1950 September," Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 111 (1951); University of Alberta Folio, "The year the sun and moon turned blue" (2015).
- Apollo 17 "Blue Marble" photograph: December 7, 1972 (NASA).
- Green thunderstorms: kept general ("measured with instruments in the 1990s"). Based on Bohren and Fraser (1993) and Gallagher, Beasley, and Bohren, Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society (1996). The details of the papers were not checked in this thread, so no names or numbers were used.
- Theodoric of Freiberg's water-filled globes (about 1304 to 1311): Wikipedia, "Theodoric of Freiberg."
- Alexander of Aphrodisias and the dark band (about AD 200): standard rainbow histories.
- Thomas Young and supernumerary bows (early 1800s): standard rainbow histories. Kept to "the early 1800s" because an exact year was not confirmed.
- Vädersolstavlan (April 20, 1535, about 7 to 9 a.m.; 1636 copy by Jacob Heinrich Elbfas in the Storkyrkan): Wikipedia, "Vädersolstavlan."
- Scheiner's parhelia near Rome (March 1629) and Descartes' Les Météores (1637): standard histories of halo science. Scheiner was at Frascati, near Rome.
- C. T. R. Wilson on Ben Nevis (1894, fascinated by glories) and the 1927 Nobel Prize: Wikipedia, "C. T. R. Wilson"; NobelPrize.org biography.
- Crocker Land (Peary 1906; MacMillan, Green, Piugaattoq, April 21, 1914; MacMillan's "a mirage or loom of the sea ice"): Wikipedia, "Crocker Land Expedition."
- Novaya Zemlya effect (De Veer, January 24, 1597, about two weeks early; whole crew on January 27): Wikipedia, "Novaya Zemlya effect"; van der Werf and others, Applied Optics 42 (2003).
- Angles: rainbow 42 degrees (red) and 40 degrees (violet), secondary about 51 degrees, halos 22 and 46 degrees, circumzenithal arc with Sun below about 32 degrees, circumhorizontal arc with Sun above about 58 degrees, horizon refraction about half a degree: standard atmospheric-optics references (for example, Les Cowley's Atmospheric Optics site).
