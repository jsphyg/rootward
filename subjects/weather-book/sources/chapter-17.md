# Chapter 17: Notes on Sources

*Kept out of the chapter file to match Chapters 13 to 16.*

- **The Wreck of the Hesperus** (blizzard beginning January 6, 1839, about 12 hours; wreck of the *Favorite* on Norman's Woe, Gloucester; Longfellow's diary, December 1839; first printed in the *New World*, January 1840; the moon-ring stanza and the skipper's "scornful laugh"): Wikipedia, "The Wreck of the Hesperus"; poem text at americanliterature.com. The poem combines two wrecks, so the chapter calls it "invented" and keeps only the storm and the wreck as fact.
- **Theophrastus** (about 371 to 287 BC; Aristotle's successor at the Lyceum; *On Weather Signs*): Wikipedia, "Theophrastus"; Loeb edition by Hort (1926), "Concerning Weather Signs"; Sider and Brunschön edition (Brill, 2007). Authorship is debated, so the chapter says "works linked to his name." No count of signs is given because none was checked.
- **Red sky**: Matthew 16:2–3, King James Version. Mechanism and the "works best where weather comes from the west" caveat: Met Office, "Red sky at night" (weather.metoffice.gov.uk). Failure at low latitudes: Wikipedia, "Red sky at morning." No published hit rate was found, which the chapter says plainly.
- **Donora smog** (began October 27, 1948; Monongahela River, southeast of Pittsburgh; zinc works and steel-wire plant; temperature inversion; more than 6,000 of 14,000 sickened; 20 deaths by the rain of Sunday, October 31; about 50 more within a month; helped start the US clean-air movement): Wikipedia, "1948 Donora smog." The chapter says "for days" because sources say both four and five days.
- **Petrichor** (Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, CSIRO; "Nature of Argillaceous Odour," *Nature*, March 1964; Greek *petra* and *ichor*; geosmin from soil bacteria): CSIRO, "The smell of rain: how our scientists invented a new word" (2015); Wikipedia, "Petrichor." **MIT raindrop study**: Joung and Buie, "Aerosol generation by raindrop impact on soil," *Nature Communications* 6, 6083 (2015). A number for geosmin sensitivity was left out because sources disagree.
- **Golden-winged warblers** (Cumberland Mountains, eastern Tennessee; testing geolocators; left more than 24 hours before the storm; about 1,500 km in 5 days; outbreak of 84 confirmed tornadoes, at least 35 deaths; infrasound suspected; Streby and others, "Tornadic Storm Avoidance Behavior in Breeding Songbirds," *Current Biology*, December 18, 2014): Cell Press release via ScienceDaily (December 18, 2014). The number of birds tagged and where they went were left out because they were not checked. That they came back follows from the trackers being recovered.
- **Blacktip sharks and Tropical Storm Gabrielle** (September 2001, Florida Gulf Coast; left for deeper water in unprecedented numbers; returned a week or two later; pressure drop as the cue; Heupel, Simpfendorfer, and Hueter, 2003): Fox Weather, "How do hurricanes and tropical storms influence sharks?"; UT Marine Science Institute, "Hurricane Victims" (Science and the Sea, 2006). The bay is not named in the chapter because the name was not checked.
- **Cows lying down**: Met Office, "Red sky at night" page ("no scientific evidence").
- **Dolbear's law** (Amos Dolbear, "The Cricket as a Thermometer," *The American Naturalist* 31: 970–971, 1897; Margarette W. Brooks, *Popular Science Monthly*, 1881; snowy tree cricket; 8 seconds plus 5 for Celsius, fairly accurate 5 to 30°C; 15 seconds plus 40 for Fahrenheit): Wikipedia, "Dolbear's law"; NOAA, "Can crickets tell temperature? The answer is in their chirp."
- **Storm glass** (popular in the 1860s, promoted by FitzRoy; his wind-direction claim; Charles Tomlinson, 1863, "a crude kind of thermoscope"; Tanaka and others, *Journal of Crystal Growth*, 2008, temperature the sole cause): Wikipedia, "Storm glass."
- **Pinecones** (open when dry, close when damp): Met Office, "Red sky at night" page; Wikipedia, "Conifer cone." The 1997 *Nature* study by Dawson, Vincent, and Rocca was not used for details because it could not be read. Hair hygrometers since the 1700s (Saussure, 1783) is standard history.
- **Rain and joint pain, Harvard** (Jena, Olenski, Molitor, and Miller, *BMJ* 359: j5326, December 13, 2017; 1,552,842 Medicare adults aged 65 and over; 11,673,392 outpatient visits, 2008 to 2012; no relation): PubMed Central PMC5728253.
- **Cloudy with a Chance of Pain** (Dixon and others, "How the weather affects the pain of citizen scientists using a smartphone app," *npj Digital Medicine* 2, 105, 2019; data through 2016; 2,658 people with about six months of daily data; humidity strongest, then low pressure and wind; rain and average temperature not linked; about 20 percent higher chance, 5 in 100 to 6 in 100): University of Manchester press release, October 24, 2019.
- **Woolly bears** (C. H. Curran, curator of insects, American Museum of Natural History; fall 1948, Bear Mountain State Park, New York; about eight years; *New York Herald Tribune*; Isabella tiger moth; bands reflect age and moisture; "having fun with an old tradition"): Iowa DNR, "Winter coming? Can the woolly bear predict it?"; Old Farmer's Almanac, "Woolly bear caterpillars and weather prediction."
- **Punxsutawney Phil** (since 1887 by legend; 35 percent over the past 20 years against US February and March temperatures; 17th of 19 groundhogs): NOAA Heritage, "Grading the groundhogs" (updated February 2, 2026). NCEI's figure of 30 percent over 10 years was not used, to keep one number.
- **Old Farmer's Almanac** (founded 1792 by Robert B. Thomas; formula in a black tin box in Dublin, New Hampshire; Walsh and Allen, "Testing the Farmer's Almanac," *Weatherwise* 34: 212–215, 1981, 50.7 percent temperature and 51.9 percent precipitation in the right direction): Wikipedia, "Old Farmer's Almanac." The almanac's own accuracy claim was left out because it was not checked.
- **Finley's tornado forecasts**: reused from Chapter 13, 13.12.
