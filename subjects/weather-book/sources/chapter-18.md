# Chapter 18: Notes on Sources

*Kept out of the chapter file to match Chapters 13 to 17.*

- **Medici network** (1654, Ferdinand II of Tuscany; coordinated by the Vallombrosan monk Luigi Antinori, 1654 to 1667; stations in Italy and abroad including Vallombrosa, Innsbruck, and Warsaw; comparable instruments and uniform recording): Museo Galileo, "Medici meteorological network" (brunelleschi.imss.fi.it and catalogue.museogalileo.it). No station count is given because the sources don't give one.
- **Richard Hendrickson** (began 1930 at 18 in Bridgehampton, New York; temperature, wind direction, precipitation twice a day; more than 80 years; about 150,000 observations; NWS 80-year award named for him, 2014; died January 2016 at 103): East Hampton Star obituary (January 2016); Portland Press Herald (January 12, 2016).
- **Cooperative Observer Program** (1890; over 8,000 volunteers; daily max and min temperature and 24-hour precipitation): Wikipedia, "Cooperative Observer Program."
- **Fahrenheit** (experiments from 1713; mercury thermometers perfected by about 1721; 1724 Royal Society paper) and **Celsius** (1742, 0 at boiling and 100 at freezing; inverted by Christin, 1743, and Linnaeus, 1744): Wikipedia, "Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit" and "Celsius." The chapter says "within two years" to cover both.
- **Six's thermometer** (James Six, 1780; U-tube, two steel markers, magnet reset): Wikipedia, "Six's thermometer." (Chapter 16's handoff note did not give a year.)
- **Stevenson screen** (Thomas Stevenson, father of Robert Louis Stevenson; reported 1864; wooden, white, double-louvered) and the **WMO thermometer height** (1.25 to 2 m): Wikipedia, "Stevenson screen."
- **Hygrometers** (Leonardo prototype, 1480; Saussure hair hygrometer, 1783; psychrometer principle): Wikipedia, "Hygrometer."
- **Périer and Pascal** (Puy de Dôme, September 19, 1648; mercury about 26 inches 3½ lines at the base and 23 inches 2 lines at the top, in old French inches): Wikipedia, "Blaise Pascal." "More than three inches (about 8 centimeters)" is worked out from those two readings. **Aneroid barometer** (Lucien Vidi, 1844): Wikipedia, "Barometer."
- **Cup anemometer** (Thomas Romney Robinson, Armagh Observatory; four-cup design 1846) and **sonic anemometers** (1950s; time of flight of sound pulses): Wikipedia, "Anemometer."
- **Cheugugi** (iron gauges made in 1441 under King Sejong; standardized system and a gauge for each provincial office ordered in 1442; about 32 cm deep and 15 cm across; ruler reading in *pun*; start and end times of rain recorded): Wikipedia, "Ch'ŭgugi"; "first standardized rain gauge," Wikipedia, "Rain gauge."
- **Tipping-bucket gauge** (Christopher Wren with Robert Hooke, 1662): Wikipedia, "Rain gauge."
- **Wilson Bentley** (first snowflake photograph, January 15, 1885): Wikipedia, "Wilson Bentley." The photo's exact place is not given in that source, so the chapter says only "a young farmer in Vermont."
- **James Church and the Mount Rose snow sampler** (University of Nevada; 1906; the first instrument for measuring the water content of snow; still in use): Wikipedia, "James Edward Church."
- **Franklin's lightning bells** (September 1752; rod on the house, one bell connected to the rod and one to the ground, a metal clapper between): Wikipedia, "Franklin bells."
- **Curiosity's REMS** (built by Spain's Centro de Astrobiología; on August 21, 2012, one of two wind sensors returned bad data and was judged broken, probably hit by a rock during landing): Wikipedia, "Rover Environmental Monitoring Station." Landing date (August 6, 2012, UTC) is standard.
- **Spring Creek flood and CoCoRaHS** (late July 1997, Fort Collins; more than 14 in. over southwest Fort Collins and less than 2 in. 3 to 4 miles east; five deaths; Nolan Doesken, then assistant state climatologist; about 300 volunteers answered; more than 20,000 by March 2015; US, Canada, Bahamas): Wikipedia, "CoCoRaHS," quoting Doesken. The flood's exact night is given only as "late July" because it was not checked against a primary source.
- **Siting rules** (wind at 10 m and about ten times an obstacle's height away; rain gauge at least twice an obstacle's height away; the 4-inch CoCoRaHS gauge with a tenfold inner tube; snow-board method): standard WMO and NWS observing guidance, stated in round numbers. The lightning-detector range is given only as "a few tens of kilometers" because product ranges vary.
