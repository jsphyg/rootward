# Weather Book Style Guide

*Working version 2, drawn from the revised Chapter 1. Update it as new decisions are made. Read it together with [book-map.md](book-map.md).*

---

## The short version

1. **Tell a story, don't teach a lesson.** Each section moves forward like a narrative, one idea leading to the next.
2. **One idea per paragraph.** Short paragraphs. Short sentences when the idea is important.
3. **Let the pictures carry the detail.** Numbers, lists, and side facts go into illustrations and visual spreads, not the main text.
4. **Use the book's big metaphors, and keep them consistent.**
5. **Cut anything that wanders away from weather near the ground.**

---

## 1. Voice

- **Direct, illustrated-guide character** (modeled on the 1991 reference), with a **modern scientific voice**.
- Confident and plain. Say what is true, then show why.
- Write for a curious adult with no science background. If a term is needed, define it in the same sentence it appears.
- Light wit is fine, once in a while. It should never slow down the explanation.
- Avoid textbook phrases such as "To understand X, start with four ideas," "Consider the following," or "In this section we will."

## 2. The recurring metaphors

These are the book's "mental hooks." Return to them often so the reader builds on one picture instead of collecting many.

| Metaphor | What it explains |
|---|---|
| **The ocean of air.** We live at the bottom of it. | The atmosphere has weight, depth, and currents. Weather happens near the bottom. |
| **Weather is the atmosphere trying to even things out.** | Why air moves at all: uneven heat, moisture, and pressure. |
| **The basic weather machine.** The Sun supplies energy, Earth spreads it unevenly, air and water carry it. | The chain that every later chapter extends. |

Rules for metaphors:

- **Don't mix them.** Stay inside the ocean picture where possible (the bottom, the depths, currents) rather than switching to unrelated images such as a basement or a house. *(This is why 1.2 now ends "at the bottom of the ocean" instead of "in the basement.")*
- **Add new metaphors sparingly.** A new one should earn its place by doing work the main three can't.
- **Note when a metaphor is simplified.** Say so briefly if it could mislead.

## 3. Structure of a section

- Open with a **one-line hook** that states the main idea or connects it to a metaphor.
- Then: what it is → how it works → why it matters for weather.
- **Prose over bullet lists.** Use a list only when items are truly parallel and the reader will scan them.
- **Tables** are fine for small sets of numbers (for example, the gases in air).
- End on a **short, memorable sentence** when one comes naturally. Don't force it.
- Use **bold** only the first time a key term is defined.

## 4. What to cut

- **Opening outlines and numbered "principles"** that interrupt momentum. Fold the ideas into the narrative instead.
- **Digressions away from weather.** For example, the upper atmosphere gets a sentence or two plus a picture, not a full section.
- **Repeated explanations.** Each concept gets one home, listed in [book-map.md](book-map.md). Other chapters point back to it in a sentence. *(Example: Chapter 1's sections on temperature, density, and stability overlap heavily with Chapter 2. That repetition is a major source of the book's length.)*
- **Hedging and stacked qualifiers.** One "usually" is enough.
- **Inline source links** in the body text (including the "utm_source=chatgpt.com" links from the draft). Keep sources in notes at the end of each chapter or the book.

## 5. Illustrations and visual spreads

- **Illustration:** one picture that makes one idea visible. Short description, plus any caption.
- **Visual spread:** a full one- or two-page picture that holds the details the text left out: numbers, scales, names, side facts.
- Write each as: a title, a description of the picture in italics, then **callouts** (labels with a short caption each).
- A spread should be **readable on its own.** A reader who only looks at the picture should still learn something.
- When the text is cut, move the detail to the spread instead of deleting it.
- Replace the draft's text diagrams (the ASCII boxes and arrows) with illustration descriptions.

## 6. Length targets (proposed)

- **Section:** about 150–350 words of main text.
- **Chapter opening:** about 200–300 words.
- **Keep** Field Observation and Try It boxes, but trim them to the steps and one line on what the reader is seeing.

## 7. Facts and numbers

- Give metric first, then US units in parentheses: "8 kilometers (5 miles)."
- Round numbers in the main text ("about three-quarters"). Exact numbers go in tables or spreads.
- Accuracy comes first. A simplification is fine. A wrong statement is not.

- From Chapter 2: illustrations are numbered in order within a chapter (Illustration 1, 2, 3...). Visual spreads take their section number (Visual Spread 2.5).
- From Chapter 3: the "jar" picture (Illustrations 1 and 2) is the book's way of showing saturation and relative humidity. Later chapters can reuse it. The shortcut "warm air holds more water" is allowed, with the simplification noted once in 3.2.
