# Chapter 10
## Winter Weather: Snow, Sleet, Freezing Rain, Blizzards, and Dangerous Cold

A snowflake's life is one long fall through the ocean of air.

It is born several kilometers up, in a cloud far below freezing. Then it sinks, slowly, through layer after layer of air on its way to the bottom. Some of those layers are cold. Some may be a degree or two above freezing. The flake has no say in the matter. Each layer it passes through can leave it alone, melt it, or freeze it again.

By the time it reaches the ground, that journey has decided everything. It may land as soft snow, as a bouncing pellet of ice, or as an ordinary-looking raindrop that turns to glass the instant it touches a tree.

Winter storms are built from the same three things as any other storm: moisture, rising air to wring it out, and in this case, cold. Chapter 3 explained how clouds make snow, and Chapter 6 explained the great lows that carry it. This chapter follows the snow, and the ice, the rest of the way down. Then it follows the cold itself, which hurts people even on days when nothing falls at all.

The temperature on your porch tells only the last part of the story. The rest happened overhead, in layers you can't see.

## 10.1 The Long Fall

In winter, almost everything that falls from a cloud starts as snow.

Chapter 3 explained why. In the cold upper part of a cloud, ice crystals grow by stealing vapor from supercooled droplets, and the crystals clump into snowflakes. What reaches the ground depends on the air between the cloud and you.

Meteorologists picture that air as a column, with the temperature marked at each height. A graph of it, made from a weather balloon, is called a **sounding**. The line that matters most on it in winter is the freezing line, 0°C (32°F).

There are four basic ways down.

If the whole column is below freezing, the flake survives and lands as **snow**.

If the flake falls through a layer of warm air, it melts. If that layer reaches all the way to the ground, it lands as **rain**.

If there is warm air aloft and a deep layer of cold air beneath it, the flake melts and then refreezes into a pellet of ice on the way down. That is **sleet**.

If the cold layer beneath is shallow, the melted drop has no time to refreeze. It arrives as a liquid colder than freezing and turns to ice the moment it touches something. That is **freezing rain**.

The layer of warm air aloft has a name. On a sounding, it pokes out to the right of the freezing line like a nose, so forecasters call it the **warm nose**. How warm and how deep it is decides whether snow melts. The depth of the cold air beneath it decides whether the drop becomes sleet or glaze.

The differences can be tiny. A degree or two, or a cold layer a few hundred meters thicker or thinner, can turn a snow forecast into an ice storm.

## 10.2 Building a Snowflake

A single crystal of ice is a **snow crystal**. A **snowflake** is usually many of them stuck together.

Chapter 3 showed how a crystal's shape records the temperature it grew in: plates near freezing, needles and columns a little colder, and the big branching stars near −15°C (5°F). As the crystals fall, they bump into each other and stick. This clumping is called **aggregation**.

Clumping works best near freezing, when the crystals are slightly wet and sticky. That is why the biggest, fluffiest flakes, sometimes several centimeters across, fall on mild winter days. In bitter cold, the crystals stay small and dry, and they fall as fine, glittering powder.

Sometimes a crystal falls through a crowd of supercooled cloud droplets. The droplets freeze onto it on contact, coating it in tiny beads of ice. This is **riming**. A lightly rimed crystal looks frosted, its fine lines softened. A heavily rimed one becomes a soft, white, crumbly pellet called **graupel**, sometimes called snow pellets or soft hail.

Graupel falls most often from winter showers with strong rising air. It bounces a little and squashes easily between your fingers. It is not sleet, which is melted snow that froze again. And it is not hail, which grows in summer thunderstorms. Graupel does play a part in hail and lightning, though, as Chapter 7 explained.

## 10.3 Snow on a Warm Day

It can snow when the thermometer reads above freezing.

The snowflake needs time to melt. A large flake falling through a shallow layer of air at 1 or 2°C (34 or 36°F) can reach the ground before it has finished melting. In dry air, snow can survive in air even warmer than that.

The dry air helps for a reason that surprises people. When snow starts falling into dry air, some of it melts and some evaporates. Both take heat from the air around them, as Chapter 1 explained for evaporation. So the falling snow cools the air it falls through.

A thermometer wrapped in a wet cloth shows how far that cooling can go. Evaporation from the cloth chills it to the **wet-bulb temperature**, which is lower than the ordinary air temperature whenever the air is not saturated. Once heavy snow or rain gets going, the air often cools close to the wet-bulb temperature. A morning of 3°C (37°F) with dry air can cool to freezing within an hour of the first flakes. Rain turns to snow, and the snow begins to stick.

Heavy snow cools the air faster than light snow does. So a storm can surprise forecasters by snowing harder than expected, cooling itself, and then making even more snow.

The ground matters too. Snow that falls after a warm spell melts on roads and sidewalks, which hold the earlier warmth, while it piles up on grass, cars, and roofs. The official temperature is measured in a shelter above the ground. A car roof or a bridge deck may be colder.

Winter storms can make some of their own cold.

## 10.4 Heavy Snow, Light Snow

Not all snow weighs the same.

**Wet snow** falls near freezing. Its flakes are big, partly melted, and full of water. It packs well, which makes it good for snowballs and snowmen. It also clings to everything. It loads tree branches and power lines, strains roofs, and is heavy work to shovel.

**Dry snow** falls in colder air. It is light and powdery, full of air between the crystals. It doesn't stick to branches, and it is easy to sweep. But the wind picks it up easily, and it drifts.

The difference shows up when you melt it. The **snow-to-liquid ratio** compares the depth of the snow with the depth of water it melts down to. The old rule of thumb is 10 to 1: 10 centimeters of snow melts to 1 centimeter of water. In fact, the average across most of the United States is closer to 13 to 1, and the real number swings widely. Wet snow can be 5 to 1. Fluffy snow downwind of the Great Lakes can be 20 to 1 or more. So the same storm, with the same water in it, could leave 5 centimeters of slush or 20 centimeters of powder.

That melted amount is called the **snow water equivalent**. It is what water managers care about, because in the mountains of the West, the winter snowpack is the summer's water supply. It also tells you how heavy the snow on your roof is.

Snow settles once it lands. Its own weight presses it down, the crystals break, and the wind packs it. So two numbers get reported. **Snowfall** is how much fell during a storm. **Snow depth** is how much is lying on the ground right now. A storm that drops 25 centimeters (10 inches) may leave only 18 centimeters (7 inches) on the ground the next morning, without any melting at all.

## 10.5 Sleet: Snow That Froze Twice

**Sleet** is a fall of small, clear pellets of ice. In the United States, that is what the word means. It began as snow, melted in a warm layer aloft, and then refroze as it fell through a deep layer of cold air near the ground.

You usually hear sleet before you notice it. The pellets tick against windows and rattle on car roofs. They bounce off the pavement. On the ground, they pile up like coarse, gritty sand.

A heavy fall of sleet behaves like dense, heavy snow. It is much harder to plow and shovel than the same depth of snow, and it can take days to melt. But it doesn't coat trees and wires the way freezing rain does. The pellets are already frozen when they land, so they don't stick. For that reason, forecasters dreading an ice storm are often relieved when the precipitation turns to sleet instead.

Sleet is also easy to confuse with hail. Both are balls of ice, but they come from very different places. Sleet forms in the layered clouds of a winter storm, and the pellets are small, usually under 5 millimeters across. Hail grows in the strong updrafts of thunderstorms, mostly in the warm months, and it can grow much larger, as Chapter 7 described.

One more caution. In Britain and much of the rest of the English-speaking world, "sleet" means a mix of rain and snow falling together, not ice pellets. A traveler reading a forecast overseas should not expect the American meaning.

## 10.6 Freezing Rain and Black Ice

**Freezing rain** looks like ordinary rain. That is what makes it dangerous.

It starts the same way as sleet: snow falls into a warm layer aloft and melts completely. But beneath that warm layer, the cold air near the ground is too thin for the drops to freeze. They reach the bottom as liquid, though they are now colder than freezing. They are **supercooled**, the same as the cloud droplets of Chapter 3, waiting for something solid to freeze onto. When they hit a road, a branch, a wire, or a windshield, they freeze in an instant.

The result is **glaze**, a smooth, clear coat of ice that wraps every surface it touches. On a tree, it is beautiful. On a road, it is close to invisible. The pavement looks merely wet, because the clear ice lets the dark road show through. That is why thin ice on roads is called **black ice**. The ice isn't black. It just isn't visible.

Black ice doesn't need freezing rain. It also forms when snowmelt or rain on the road freezes again after dark, or when fog or dew freezes on cold pavement. It is most common at night and in the early morning, and in places the Sun doesn't reach.

Bridges and overpasses freeze first. An ordinary road sits on the ground, which stores heat and gives a little of it back. A bridge has cold air above and below it, so it loses heat from both sides and gives nothing back. It can be coated in ice while the road leading up to it is still just wet. Shaded curves, ramps, and roads beside water are often next.

A lighter cousin of freezing rain is **freezing drizzle**: tiny supercooled droplets falling from low, shallow clouds. It can form without any ice in the cloud at all, from droplets that never froze to begin with. It looks harmless. But even a film of it can turn a parking lot into a skating rink.

## 10.7 The Ice Storm

In early January 1998, a stalled weather pattern parked a warm nose over southern Quebec, eastern Ontario, northern New York, and New England, with a shallow layer of cold air trapped beneath it. For five days, rain fell into that cold layer and froze on contact. In places, more than 100 millimeters (4 inches) of freezing rain fell.

The ice built up layer by layer, on every branch and every wire. Trees split and fell. Power lines snapped. Then the great steel towers that carried electricity to Montreal buckled and folded like paper. Over 1,000 transmission towers and about 35,000 wooden utility poles came down. More than 4 million people lost power, some for weeks, in the middle of a Canadian winter. Canada sent in more than 15,000 troops to help. Dozens of people died, many of them from cold, fires, and carbon monoxide in homes without heat.

An **ice storm** is a freezing-rain event that builds up enough ice to do damage. In the United States, a warning is usually issued when about 6 millimeters (a quarter of an inch) or more of ice is expected to build up.

The buildup is called **ice accretion**. It wraps each branch and wire on all sides, so even a thin coat adds a lot of weight. Trees that still have leaves, or are already weak, fail first, and wind makes it worse. The danger outlasts the rain. Loaded limbs can break hours or days later, with a crack like a gunshot.

Power is the biggest problem. The damage covers so much ground, and iced roads slow the crews so much, that repairs take days or weeks. Without power, many homes lose their heat, and that is when people bring generators, grills, and camp stoves inside. Chapter 9's safety box gives the rule: **generators run outdoors only**. Carbon monoxide has no color or smell.

## 10.8 The Battle Line

Where cold air and warm air meet in winter, there is rarely a clean line between rain and snow. There is a messy zone where the two are fighting.

On one side, it snows. On the other, it rains. In between, the precipitation may be any mix of snow, sleet, freezing rain, and rain, and it may change from one to another several times in an hour as the layers above shift. This is the **wintry mix**. Two towns 15 kilometers (10 miles) apart can get completely different storms.

Height matters. A hilltop may poke up into the warm nose and get rain, while the valley below stays in the cold air and gets glaze.

The cold side often holds on longer than the forecast expects. Cold air is dense, so it hugs the ground and is hard to push out. In the eastern United States, cold air from a high over New England often slides south along the eastern side of the Appalachian Mountains and gets wedged against them. Warm, moist air from the Atlantic rides up over the top but can't scour out the wedge. Forecasters call this **cold-air damming**. It gives the Carolinas and Virginia some of the country's worst ice storms, and computer models tend to wear the wedge down too soon.

On the plains, a shallow layer of Arctic air can do the same without a mountain, sliding south a few hundred meters deep while warm air streams north above it.

In winter, the coldest air is often the thinnest, and it still decides what lands on you.

## 10.9 The Great Winter Storms

On March 12, 1993, a low formed over the Gulf of Mexico and set off north along the East Coast. In the next two days, it spun up tornadoes and a storm surge in Florida, dropped 43 centimeters (17 inches) of snow on Birmingham, Alabama, buried the mountains of Tennessee and North Carolina under more than a meter, and pushed blizzard conditions all the way to Maine and Canada. More than 200 people died. It became known as the Storm of the Century.

Most big winter storms are midlatitude lows like this one, the storms whose life story Chapter 6 told. The same low can bring rain and thunderstorms to its warm side and heavy snow to its cold side.

The heaviest snow usually falls north and northwest of the low, where moist air wraps around the center into the cold. On a satellite picture, this is the head of the storm's comma of cloud. Inside it, the snow often gathers into narrow **bands**, only a few tens of kilometers wide, where snow can fall at 5 centimeters (2 inches) an hour, sometimes with thunder. That is why one town gets a foot of snow and the next gets three inches.

Different paths make different storms. A **nor'easter** runs up the East Coast, named for the northeast wind it drives onto the shore, bringing heavy snow, coastal flooding, and pounding waves. An **Alberta clipper** dives fast out of western Canada, with light, powdery snow, strong wind, and sharp cold behind it. A **Colorado low** or **Panhandle hook** swings northeast across the middle of the country, and it can bring a blizzard to one side and tornadoes to the other on the same day.

A small change in track can move the snow a long way. That is why good forecasts give a range, such as "10 to 20 centimeters," or the chance of each amount. Chapter 13 explains how to read them, and Chapter 20 covers winter watches and warnings.

## 10.10 Lake-Effect and Mountain Snow

On November 17, 2014, a wall of snow stood over the southern side of Buffalo, New York. For three days, Arctic air blew down the length of Lake Erie and dumped its snow in a band only 25 to 30 kilometers (15 to 20 miles) wide. South Cheektowaga got 165 centimeters (65 inches). The Buffalo airport, a few kilometers north of the band, got 16 centimeters (6 inches). Streets less than 3 kilometers (2 miles) apart got a few inches and several feet. Thirteen people died.

That is **lake-effect snow**, and it is made by the lake, not by a storm.

In late fall and early winter, the Great Lakes still hold summer's warmth. When frigid air blows across the open water, the lake heats it from below and fills it with moisture. As Chapter 2 explained, warm air under cold air is unstable, so it churns upward into rows of snow clouds. At the downwind shore, the land slows the air and pushes it higher, and the snow comes down hard.

It takes air well above the lake that is much colder than the water, a long stretch of open water for the wind to cross, called the **fetch**, and a steady wind. When the wind blows down the length of a lake, the snow can gather into one intense band that stays put for days. A small shift in the wind swings the band onto another town. The strongest bands even make **thundersnow**, thunder muffled by the falling snow. Once the lake freezes, the machine shuts off.

Mountains wring snow out of the air too. As Chapter 5 described, moist air forced up a mountainside cools and drops its moisture on the windward slopes. A valley may get rain while the slopes above get a meter of snow. The height where rain turns to snow is the **snow level**. It sits a few hundred meters below the freezing level, because snow takes time to melt as it falls. Moist air flowing gently uphill onto high plains, east of the Rockies for example, can bring hours of **upslope snow** even with no mountain wall.

## 10.11 Blizzards, Squalls, and Whiteouts

A blizzard is about wind and sight, not about how much snow falls.

In the United States, a **blizzard** means wind of at least 56 km/h (35 mph), sustained or in frequent gusts, with falling or blowing snow that cuts visibility to under 400 meters (a quarter mile), for at least three hours. The cold and the deep snow often come with it, but neither is required.

In fact, no new snow is needed at all. Strong wind behind a storm can pick up dry powder that is already on the ground and fill the air with it. That is a **ground blizzard**. It can begin after the snow has stopped, under a clear blue sky overhead.

The wind piles the snow into **drifts**. It scours one stretch of field down to bare grass and dumps waist-deep snow in the lee of the next fence, building, or road cut. A road plowed in the morning can be closed again by noon.

Inside a blizzard, the world can vanish. In a **whiteout**, the snow in the air and the snow on the ground blend into one blank white. There is no horizon and no shadow. You can't tell up from down, near from far, or road from ditch. On the Great Plains, settlers caught in the blizzards of the 1880s died within sight of their own houses. Some farm families tied a rope from the back door to the barn.

A **snow squall** is a short, violent burst of snow and wind, often along an Arctic cold front. It may last less than an hour and leave only a centimeter or two of snow. But it arrives like a curtain. On a highway, drivers go from clear road to zero visibility in seconds, the road turns slick, and the cars behind can't stop. Squalls cause some of the worst pileups on record. In the United States, they now get their own short-fuse snow squall warning.

## 10.12 Dangerous Cold

Sometimes the most dangerous winter weather falls from a clear sky.

A **cold wave** is a sharp drop to temperatures that are dangerous for the place. The number depends on where you live. A morning of −25°C (−13°F) is routine in Minnesota. Half that cold can freeze pipes, kill crops, and overwhelm power grids in Texas or Georgia, where homes and systems aren't built for it.

The worst cold waves come when a huge dome of Arctic air, the continental Arctic air of Chapter 6, spills south under a strong high. Near the center of the high, the sky is clear and the wind is calm. As Chapter 2 explained, the ground then loses its heat to space all night, and fresh snow makes the cooling deeper. The coldest air drains into the valleys. On the edges of the high, the pressure changes quickly, and the wind blows.

That wind makes the cold far more dangerous. Chapter 2 explained **wind chill**: wind strips away the thin layer of warm air around your skin, so you lose heat faster. At −18°C (0°F) with a 24 km/h (15 mph) wind, the wind chill is −28°C (−19°F), and bare skin can freeze in about 30 minutes. Colder and windier, it can happen in five.

**Frostbite** is freezing of the skin and the flesh beneath it. It strikes the parts farthest from the heart first: fingers, toes, ears, nose, and cheeks. The first sign is numbness, then skin that looks pale, waxy, or gray and feels hard. Because it is numb, people often don't notice it.

**Hypothermia** is when the whole body loses heat faster than it can make it, and its core temperature falls below about 35°C (95°F). It begins with hard shivering. Then come clumsiness, slurred speech, confusion, and drowsiness. Late on, the shivering may stop, which is a bad sign, not a good one. The confusion means people often can't tell that it is happening to them. And it doesn't need extreme cold. Cold rain, wind, wet clothes, or a fall into cold water can bring it on in air well above freezing.

Snow and ice injure people mostly in roundabout ways. Most winter storm deaths come from car crashes, from cold, from carbon monoxide, and from heart attacks while shoveling, not from the snowflakes themselves.

# Weather Safety
## Winter Storms and Cold

- **Get ready before it starts.** Charge phones and power banks, refill medicines, and stock food, water, flashlights, and batteries while roads are still clear. Test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.
- **Keep heat safe.** Never heat your home with a gas oven, a grill, a camp stove, or a car running in the garage. Run generators outdoors only, as Chapter 9 explains.
- **Protect your pipes.** Let warm air reach pipes on outside walls, disconnect garden hoses, and know where your main water shutoff is. Never thaw a frozen pipe with a flame.
- **Stay off the roads in ice and blizzards.** Four-wheel drive helps you start moving. It does not help you stop or steer on ice. Treat every wet-looking road below freezing as ice, and every bridge as worse.
- **If you must drive,** tell someone your route, keep the tank or battery at least half full, and carry blankets, water, food, a flashlight, a shovel, and a charger.
- **If you're stranded, stay with the car.** It is shelter, and rescuers can find it. Run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for heat, with a window cracked, and keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.
- **In a snow squall,** slow down gradually, turn on your lights, and don't stop in a traffic lane. Better yet, wait until it passes.
- **After an ice storm,** stay out from under loaded trees and wires. Treat every downed line as live.
- **In a whiteout,** stay inside. If you must go out, keep the building in sight or hold a line.
- **Shovel with care.** Push rather than lift, take small loads, and rest often. Stop for chest pain, dizziness, or bad shortness of breath.
- **For frostbite,** get to warmth and get medical help. Don't rub the skin. Rewarm it in warm, not hot, water, and don't thaw it if it could freeze again.
- **For hypothermia,** call for help. Get the person out of the cold, replace wet clothes with dry ones, and warm the body's core first: chest, neck, and groin. No alcohol.

*For watches, warnings, and advisories and the one-page quick reference, see Chapter 20. For wind chill itself, see Chapter 2.*

# Field Observation
## What Is Falling?

During a winter storm, watch from a doorway or window. Is it soft and fluffy, settling gently? That is **snow**. Hard, clear pellets that bounce and tick on the glass? **Sleet**. Soft white pellets that squash between your fingers? **Graupel**. Rain that leaves a clear coat of ice on railings, branches, and car mirrors? **Freezing rain**.

Note the time and temperature each time it changes. You are watching the layers overhead shift, one degree at a time. Check the sidewalk with your eyes and a hand on the railing, not by stepping out boldly.

# Try It
## Measure the Water in Snow

After a fresh snowfall, push a straight-sided can or jar straight down into the snow on a flat, open spot, away from drifts and roofs. Measure the depth of the snow in it. Bring it inside, let it melt, and measure the water.

Divide the snow depth by the water depth. If 16 centimeters of snow melts to 1 centimeter of water, the ratio is 16 to 1. Try it again in the next storm, and compare a mild, wet snowfall with a cold, powdery one.

# Chapter Summary

Most winter precipitation starts as snow. The layers of air below the cloud decide whether it lands as snow, sleet, freezing rain, or rain. Sleet melted aloft and refroze in deep cold air. Freezing rain melted aloft and froze only on contact.

Snow can fall above freezing, and falling snow can cool the air enough to make more of it. How much water snow holds varies widely. The 10-to-1 rule is only a rough guide.

Freezing rain makes glaze and black ice. Enough of it makes an ice storm, which can bring down trees, power lines, and steel towers.

Most big winter storms are midlatitude lows, with snow on their cold side and rain on their warm side. Lakes and mountains make their own heavy snow.

Blizzards are about wind and visibility, not snowfall. Snow squalls are brief but deadly on the road.

Cold and wind together cause frostbite and hypothermia. Most winter deaths come from crashes, cold, carbon monoxide, and overexertion.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, the most dangerous winter surface may not be the one buried under a foot of snow. It may be the one that only looks wet.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Sounding:** A graph of temperature with height, usually from a weather balloon.
**Warm nose:** A layer of above-freezing air aloft, over colder air near the ground.
**Snow crystal:** A single crystal of ice formed in a cloud.
**Snowflake:** One or more snow crystals stuck together.
**Aggregation:** Snow crystals clumping into flakes.
**Riming:** Supercooled droplets freezing onto an ice crystal.
**Graupel:** A soft, white pellet made by heavy riming.
**Wet-bulb temperature:** The lowest temperature evaporation can cool the air to.
**Snow-to-liquid ratio:** The depth of snow compared with the depth of water it melts to.
**Snow water equivalent:** The depth of water in snow if it all melted.
**Snowfall:** How much snow fell during a period.
**Snow depth:** How much snow is on the ground at one moment.
**Sleet:** Pellets of ice made when melted snow refreezes before landing (US meaning).
**Freezing rain:** Supercooled rain that freezes on contact.
**Freezing drizzle:** Tiny supercooled droplets that freeze on contact.
**Glaze:** A smooth, clear coat of ice from freezing rain or drizzle.
**Black ice:** Thin, clear ice on a road that looks merely wet.
**Ice storm:** Freezing rain that builds up enough ice to cause damage.
**Ice accretion:** The ice built up on an exposed surface.
**Wintry mix:** Some combination of snow, sleet, freezing rain, and rain.
**Cold-air damming:** Shallow cold air wedged against a mountain range.
**Snow band:** A narrow stripe of very heavy snow inside a storm.
**Nor'easter:** A strong low moving up the East Coast, with northeast wind on the coast.
**Alberta clipper:** A fast, fairly dry low diving southeast from western Canada.
**Lake-effect snow:** Snow made when cold air crosses warmer open water.
**Fetch:** The distance wind travels over open water.
**Thundersnow:** Thunder and lightning during a snowstorm.
**Snow level:** The height where rain changes to snow.
**Upslope snow:** Snow made by moist air flowing uphill.
**Blizzard:** Wind of 56 km/h (35 mph) or more with snow cutting visibility under 400 meters (¼ mile) for at least three hours.
**Ground blizzard:** Blizzard conditions from snow already on the ground.
**Drift:** Snow piled up by the wind.
**Whiteout:** Snow in the air and on the ground blending until no horizon or detail can be seen.
**Snow squall:** A brief, intense burst of snow and wind.
**Cold wave:** A sharp drop to temperatures that are dangerous for the place.
**Wind chill:** How cold it feels on bare skin once the wind is counted.
**Frostbite:** Freezing of the skin and the flesh beneath it.
**Hypothermia:** A dangerous drop in the body's core temperature.
