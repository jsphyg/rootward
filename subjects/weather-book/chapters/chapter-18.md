# Chapter 18
## Weather Instruments: Building and Using a Home Observing Station

In 1654, Ferdinand II, Grand Duke of Tuscany, did something no ruler had done before. He sent matching instruments to observers in different towns and asked them all to read their instruments the same way and write the results down.

The observers were scattered across Italy and beyond: a monastery at Vallombrosa in the hills near Florence, and cities as far off as Innsbruck and Warsaw. A monk named Luigi Antinori collected their reports. The network ran until 1667. It was the first weather-observing network in the world.

Ferdinand's idea was simple, and it is still the whole idea of a weather station. One reading tells you about one place at one moment. Many careful readings, taken the same way, show the ocean of air moving.

This chapter is about joining that tradition from your own backyard. It explains how each instrument works, where to put it, how to check it, and how to keep a record worth keeping. The instruments matter. Where you put them matters more.

---

## 18.1 Eighty Years of Mornings

In 1930, an 18-year-old farmer in Bridgehampton, on the eastern end of Long Island, began taking weather readings for the government. Twice a day, Richard Hendrickson noted the temperature, the wind direction, and the rain. He kept going for more than 80 years, longer than anyone else in the program's history. By the end he had made an estimated 150,000 observations. In 2014, the National Weather Service named its 80-year service award after him. He died in January 2016, at 103.

Hendrickson was a **cooperative observer**, one of the volunteers in a program the US government set up in 1890. More than 8,000 still take part. Each day they record the highest and lowest temperatures and the rain and snow of the past 24 hours. Much of what we know about the climate of the United States rests on their notebooks.

A home station measures the same basic things: temperature, humidity, air pressure, wind, and precipitation. Some add sunshine, soil temperature, or lightning.

It helps to know which numbers are measured and which are worked out. Temperature, pressure, and rain are measured directly. So is relative humidity, by a sensor. But the dew point, the feels-like temperature, the sea-level pressure, and the rain rate are all calculated from other readings. A calculated number can only be as good as the readings behind it. If the thermometer sits in the sun, the dew point, heat index, and everything else built on it will be wrong too.

## 18.2 The Thermometer

In 1724, the instrument maker Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit, a German-born craftsman living in the Dutch Republic, described his thermometers and their fixed points to the Royal Society in London. He had found that mercury, sealed in a thin glass tube, rose and fell evenly and reliably with the warmth around it. In 1742, the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a scale of 100 steps between freezing and boiling water. Oddly, he put 0 at boiling and 100 at freezing. Within two years, others had turned it the right way up.

A liquid thermometer works because liquids expand as they warm. Squeeze that expansion into a hair-thin tube, and a small change in volume becomes a long move up the scale.

Most home stations now use an electronic sensor instead. Its electrical resistance changes with temperature, and a small chip turns that into a number. A good one is accurate to a few tenths of a degree, which is better than most home stations are placed.

One old invention is still worth owning. In 1780, the British scientist James Six made a thermometer that remembers. Its U-shaped tube pushes two tiny steel markers ahead of the liquid, one on the way up and one on the way down. The markers stay where they were pushed, so in the morning you can read the night's lowest temperature and yesterday's highest. You reset them with a magnet. A **maximum-minimum thermometer** is how cooperative observers like Hendrickson recorded each day's range for most of the twentieth century.

Whatever kind you use, remember Chapter 2's warning: a thermometer measures itself. Your job is to keep it at the temperature of the air.

## 18.3 The White Box

In 1864, Thomas Stevenson, a Scottish lighthouse engineer and the father of the writer Robert Louis Stevenson, reported a design for a small wooden box to hold thermometers. Its sides were double slats, angled like window blinds, so that air could pass through but sunlight could not. It was painted white. Versions of the **Stevenson screen** still stand at weather stations around the world.

The box solves the thermometer's biggest problem: the Sun. A thermometer in sunlight absorbs energy directly and reads far warmer than the air. At night, a thermometer under an open sky loses heat to space and can read colder than the air. The screen blocks both. White reflects sunlight. The slats let the air in and keep the light out. A roof keeps off the rain.

Home stations use a smaller version called a **radiation shield**, a stack of white plastic plates shaped like upside-down bowls, with the sensor in the middle. It does the same job, more cheaply.

A shield works best when the wind blows through it. On a calm, sunny afternoon, the air inside can warm a little above the air outside. Some stations add a small fan that pulls air steadily past the sensor. These **aspirated shields** read more accurately in light wind and bright sun, which is exactly when an unaspirated shield is most likely to read too warm.

The World Meteorological Organization asks for the thermometer to sit between 1.25 and 2 meters (about 4 to 6½ feet) above the ground. That is about the height of your face, which is the point. A weather station measures the air that people live in.

## 18.4 Where to Put It

Most temperature errors have nothing to do with the sensor. They come from where it was put.

Walk around your property before you mount anything. Look for open grass or natural ground, away from buildings. Notice what is around you: pavement, walls, roofs, vents, trees, and slopes. Each one changes the air near it.

Brick and stone walls soak up the Sun all day and give the heat back at night. Pavement and roofs bake. An air conditioner blows hot air out of its outdoor unit, and a dryer vent blows warm, damp air. Dense trees shade the sensor in the afternoon and hold warmth in at night. A sensor in a low hollow will catch the cold air that drains downhill on calm nights (Chapter 2, 2.9). That last one is not exactly an error. It is a real temperature, but only for the hollow.

The best spot is over grass, in the open, several meters from buildings and pavement, shaded by the shield rather than a tree. Few yards have one. Pick the least bad spot, and write down what's near it. A compromise you have recorded is far more useful than one you have forgotten.

Humidity sensors usually share the shield with the thermometer, and they need the same care. They have one extra enemy: water. A sprinkler, a nearby pond, or a downspout can make the air beside the sensor much damper than the air of the neighborhood.

Ask one question about every reading. Am I measuring the weather, or the yard?

## 18.5 Measuring the Water in the Air

Around 1480, Leonardo da Vinci sketched a prototype for an instrument to measure moisture in the air: a **hygrometer**. In 1783, the Swiss scientist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure built a better one using a human hair, which grows longer in damp air and shorter in dry air (Chapter 17, 17.9). A pulley turned the hair's tiny changes into the swing of a pointer.

Hair hygrometers are still sold, but they drift and need checking. Most home stations now use a thin film that absorbs water vapor and changes how much electric charge it can hold. They are small and fast, but they age too, especially in dirty air or after being soaked.

The classic check is the **psychrometer**, two matching thermometers side by side. One is ordinary. The other has its bulb wrapped in a wet cloth sock. Air passing over the wet bulb evaporates water from it, and evaporation cools (Chapter 1, 1.6). In dry air, evaporation is fast, and the wet bulb reads much colder than the dry one. In damp air, evaporation is slow, and the two read nearly the same. A table or calculator turns the difference into relative humidity and dew point.

A **sling psychrometer** whirls the pair on a handle to keep the air moving. Swing it in the shade, read the wet bulb first, and swing again until it stops falling.

Chapter 3 explained what relative humidity and dew point mean (3.3 and 3.4). For a station, the practical point is this: relative humidity changes with temperature all day even when the moisture doesn't, so the dew point is the better number to log.

## 18.6 Weighing the Air at Home

On a September day in 1648, Florin Périer carried a mercury barometer up the Puy de Dôme, a volcanic peak in central France. His brother-in-law, Blaise Pascal, had asked him to. Périer measured the height of the mercury at the bottom of the mountain, partway up, and at the top. The higher he climbed, the lower the mercury stood. At the summit it was more than three inches (about 8 centimeters) lower than at the base. There was less air above him pressing down. Pascal had his proof that the ocean of air has weight, and that the weight thins with height.

Chapter 5 told the story of the first barometer and explained what pressure means (5.1). Today's barometers rarely use mercury. In 1844, a French scientist named Lucien Vidi made the **aneroid barometer**: a small, sealed metal can with most of the air pumped out. A spring keeps it from collapsing. As the pressure rises, the can squeezes in a little. As it falls, the can relaxes. Levers make the movement big enough to turn a pointer. The barometer in a home station, or in your phone, is a tiny electronic version of the same idea.

You can keep the barometer indoors. Air pressure is the same inside a house as outside.

Périer's climb explains the one setting you must get right. Your station measures the real pressure where it sits, called **station pressure**. That is lower the higher you live. To compare it with weather maps, which use sea level, the station needs to know its elevation, so it can add back the weight of the air that would be above sea level (Chapter 16, 16.7). Then compare it on a calm day with the nearest airport report (Chapter 12, 12.1) and adjust it to match.

After that, the most useful thing your barometer tells you is not the number. It is the trend over the past three hours (Chapter 5, 5.7).

## 18.7 Catching the Wind

In 1846, Thomas Romney Robinson, an astronomer at the Armagh Observatory in Northern Ireland, mounted four hollow half-spheres on crossed arms, free to spin. The wind pushes harder on the hollow side of a cup than the rounded side, so the cups turn, and the faster the wind, the faster they turn. Count the turns, and you have measured the wind.

The **cup anemometer** is still the most common wind instrument. The newest kind, the **sonic anemometer**, has no moving parts. It sends pulses of sound between small arms. Wind speeds up a pulse traveling with it and slows one traveling against it, and the difference gives both speed and direction. It never sticks with ice or wears out its bearings, but it costs more.

A **wind vane** points into the wind, and wind is always named for where it comes from. A north wind blows from the north.

Wind is the hardest thing to measure well at home. The standard height is 10 meters (33 feet) above open ground, because the wind near the ground is slowed and twisted by everything it touches (Chapter 5, 5.9). A rough rule is to keep the instrument at least ten times as far from an obstacle as the obstacle is tall. So a 6-meter house would need 60 meters of open ground upwind. Few yards have that.

Most home stations put the wind sensor on a mast well above the roofline. That is a fair compromise. A sensor sitting just above the roof is not, because the roof speeds up some winds and blocks others.

Expect your readings to run lower than the airport's and to swing more in direction. You are measuring the wind among the trees and houses, not over a runway.

Two rules for the mast. Mount it where it can fall without hitting anything, and never near a power line. And line the vane up with true north when you install it, not with the edge of the house.

## 18.8 The Rain Gauge

In 1441, during the reign of King Sejong the Great of Korea, iron rain gauges were made for the royal court. The next year, Sejong ordered an identical gauge for every provincial office. Each one was a cylinder about 32 centimeters deep and 15 wide, set on a stone stand. After each storm, an official dipped a ruler into the water, wrote down the depth, and recorded when the rain began and ended. Because all the gauges were alike, rainfall in one province could be fairly compared with rainfall in another. The **cheugugi** is the oldest known standardized rain gauge in the world.

A rain gauge looks like the simplest instrument of all. It is a container that catches rain. But to be accurate, it needs three things that Sejong understood: a known opening, a way to read small amounts, and a standard way of doing it.

A good manual gauge, such as the one used by CoCoRaHS volunteers in North America (18.12), has a funnel 10 centimeters (4 inches) wide that pours into a narrow inner tube. The tube is ten times smaller in area than the funnel, so every millimeter of rain fills it ten millimeters deep. That makes small amounts easy to read.

Most home stations use a **tipping-bucket gauge** instead, an idea first built by Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke in England in 1662. Rain runs down a funnel into one of two tiny buckets balanced like a seesaw. When one fills, it tips and empties, and the other moves under the funnel. Each tip is counted as a set amount, often 0.2 millimeters (or 0.01 inch). Electronics count the tips, so you also learn when the rain fell and how hard.

Tipping buckets miss some rain in downpours, miss snow unless heated, and clog with leaves and insects. Many careful observers keep a manual gauge beside the electronic one and compare them after each storm.

Place the gauge in the open, level, and well away from anything tall. Keep it at least twice as far from a tree, wall, or fence as that obstacle is tall. Wind blowing over the gauge carries some drops past it, so in a windy open spot, a gauge can still catch a little less than what fell.

## 18.9 Snow on the Board

On January 15, 1885, a young farmer in Vermont named Wilson Bentley pointed a camera through a microscope and took the first photograph of a single snowflake. He went on to photograph thousands more. But snow interested scientists for a more practical reason, too: it is water waiting to happen.

In 1906, at the University of Nevada, a professor named James Church made a new instrument for measuring snow on the slopes of Mount Rose. The **Mount Rose snow sampler** was a long metal tube with a cutting edge. Push it down through the snowpack to the ground, pull up the core, and weigh it. The weight tells you how much water the snow holds. It was the first instrument for measuring the water content of snow, and versions of it are still used to forecast the spring runoff that fills rivers and reservoirs across the American West.

At home, snow takes three different measurements. Chapter 10 explained why each matters (10.4). Here is how to take them.

**New snow** is measured on a **snow board**, a flat, white board roughly 40 to 60 centimeters (16 to 24 inches) across, set on top of the old snow in an open spot. Push a ruler down through the new snow to the board in several places, average the readings, then wipe the board clean. Chapter 16 explains why the official rule is to measure and clear it no more than once every six hours (16.5).

**Snow depth** is everything on the ground, old and new. Measure it with a ruler or a marked stake in several open places, away from drifts and bare spots, and average.

**Snow water equivalent** is the water the snow would make if melted. Collect the snow in your manual rain gauge, or cut a core with an upside-down can, bring it in, melt it, and measure the water. Fluffy snow melts to far less water than the same depth of wet, heavy snow.

For freezing rain, measure the ice on a horizontal surface such as a twig or a wire, or on a small board left out for the purpose (Chapter 10, 10.7).

## 18.10 Lightning Bells and Other Sensors

In September 1752, Benjamin Franklin put up an iron rod on the roof of his house in Philadelphia and led a wire from it indoors, where it ended at a small bell. Beside it hung a second bell, wired to the ground. Between them dangled a small metal ball. When a charged storm cloud passed over, the rod filled with electricity, and the ball swung back and forth between the bells, ringing them. His house announced the storm.

Home lightning detectors do something similar with radio. Every lightning flash sends out a crackle of radio energy, which you can hear as static on an AM radio. A small chip listens for the pattern and estimates roughly how far away the strikes are, out to a few tens of kilometers. That is useful as an early alert, but not as a safety rule. If you hear thunder, go inside, whatever the detector says (Chapter 7).

Other sensors measure different parts of the weather machine. A **solar radiation sensor**, or **pyranometer**, measures sunlight arriving at the ground, which shows cloud cover, haze, and smoke at a glance. A **soil thermometer**, buried at a set depth, tells gardeners when the ground is warm enough to plant. UV, leaf-wetness, and soil-moisture sensors serve gardeners and farmers.

None is needed for a good station. Add one only if you'll use it.

## 18.11 Check, Clean, and Doubt

On August 6, 2012, NASA's Curiosity rover landed on Mars carrying a weather station called REMS, built in Spain. It had two wind sensors on small arms. A couple of weeks after landing, one began sending back numbers that made no sense. Engineers tested it from Earth and concluded it was broken, probably hit by a pebble thrown up during the landing. The other sensor kept working.

If the best-engineered weather station ever built can be damaged on its first day, yours can too. Instruments break, drift, clog, and get knocked out of line. A station that is never checked slowly becomes a station that is confidently wrong.

**Check the temperature sensor** against something you trust. Stir crushed ice into water until it is a slushy mix, wait a few minutes, and put the sensor in a sealed plastic bag in the slush. It should read 0°C (32°F), within a few tenths. (Boiling water is a poor check, because its temperature depends on your elevation and the day's pressure.) Or put a good thermometer beside the station's sensor, inside the shield, for a few days.

**Check the rain gauge** by pouring in a measured amount of water slowly and seeing what it reports.

**Check the wind vane** with a compass, the anemometer by making sure it spins freely, and the barometer against the nearest airport on a calm day.

Then inspect the station once a month. Clear the rain funnel of leaves, bugs, and bird droppings. Wipe spider webs and wasp nests from the shield. Check that the gauge is level and the mast straight. Change batteries before they die.

Finally, doubt your own data. Ask whether each reading makes sense. A temperature that jumps 5 degrees in a minute on a calm day, rain with clear skies, or a wind stuck at zero during a storm is a problem with the instrument, not news about the weather. Chapter 16 showed how experts check a record (16.10). The same questions work in your backyard.

## 18.12 Keep the Record, Share It

In late July 1997, a storm stalled over the southwest side of Fort Collins, Colorado. More than 36 centimeters (14 inches) of rain fell there. Only five or six kilometers to the east, less than 5 centimeters (2 inches) fell. Spring Creek burst its banks in the night, and five people died.

Afterward, Nolan Doesken, then the assistant state climatologist, was struck by how little anyone had known about where the heaviest rain was falling. The official gauges were too far apart to see it. He asked people in the area to measure rain in their own yards with simple gauges and report it. About 300 answered. The network that grew from that call, the **Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network**, or **CoCoRaHS**, had more than 20,000 volunteers by 2015, across the United States, Canada, and the Bahamas. Forecasters, hydrologists, and climate scientists use the reports.

That is the last lesson of this chapter. A single reading is a curiosity. A record kept carefully, and shared, becomes science.

Start with a **station log**, written down as soon as the station is set up. Record where each instrument is, its height, what is near it, the date it was installed, and its make and model. Draw a plan of the yard. Every time something changes (a sensor is moved, a tree is cut, a shed goes up, a battery is changed), write it down with the date. Chapter 16 explained why this kind of record, called **metadata**, decides whether a reading can be trusted (16.10).

Keep a daily summary of the high, the low, the precipitation, the peak gust, and the sky. Use one time zone all year, and back up the data.

For your daily observing, use the forecasting routine in Chapter 13 (13.11 and 13.12). The station feeds it: the overnight low in the morning, the pressure trend and wind shift in the afternoon, the dew point at night.

Then share. CoCoRaHS wants manual rain and snow reports. Many electronic stations can send readings to online networks run with national weather services. Reports of hail, flooding, and damage go to the local weather office (Chapter 20).

# Weather Safety
## Instruments Are Replaceable

- **Never go out to check a station during a storm.** No reading is worth being outdoors with lightning, hail, falling branches, or flying debris (Chapters 7 and 8).
- **Never wade into water to reach a rain gauge.** Floodwater hides currents, holes, and debris (Chapter 11).
- **Keep masts well away from power lines.** A falling mast, or a ladder that touches a line, can kill. Mount a mast where it can fall without hitting anything or anyone.
- **Ground a tall mast** and follow the maker's instructions for lightning protection. A mast is a lightning target.
- **Stay off icy roofs and ladders.** Clear snow from instruments you can reach from the ground, or wait for the thaw.

A station can be rebuilt after a storm. You can't.

# Field Exercise
## Survey Your Station Site

Before you mount anything, draw a plan of your property from above. Mark the house, fences, trees, paved areas, slopes, vents, air conditioners, and sprinklers. Note the height of each tall object and the direction it lies from your planned spot. Then choose a place for each instrument using Visual Spread 18.4, and write down the compromises you had to make. Date the drawing. It is the first page of your station log.

# Try It
## Make a Jar Rain Gauge

Find a straight-sided glass jar or plastic container with a flat bottom. Tape a ruler to the outside, with zero at the inside bottom. Set it in the open, at least twice as far from any obstacle as the obstacle is tall, and wedge it so it can't tip. After each rain, read the depth at eye level and empty it. Compare your totals with the nearest official report. A straight-sided container gives a true reading. A tapered one doesn't, because the opening is a different size from the bottom.

---

# Try It
## The Ice-Water Check

Fill a glass with crushed ice and add a little cold water. Stir and wait three minutes. Seal your thermometer or sensor in a small plastic bag and push it into the middle of the slush, not touching the glass. After a few minutes, read it. It should show 0°C (32°F). If it's off by more than half a degree, note the error in your station log, and correct for it, or replace it.

---

# Field Observation
## Your Personal Extremes Table

Once your station has run for a month, start a table of your own records: the hottest day, coldest night, wettest day, biggest snowfall, strongest gust, lowest pressure, and highest dew point. Give each one a date, the instrument, and a note on how sure you are. Each month, add a row for the month's extremes. Each year, compare your numbers with the nearest official station and ask why they differ. Is it your site, your instrument, or a real difference in the weather? After a few years, you'll know your yard's climate better than any app does.

# Chapter Summary

The first weather network, in 1654, rested on one idea: matching instruments, read the same way, in many places. That is still what makes a weather station worth having.

A home station measures temperature, humidity, pressure, wind, and precipitation. Other numbers, such as the dew point and the feels-like temperature, are calculated from those, so they inherit every error.

The thermometer must read the air, not the Sun. That is the job of the white shield, set 1.25 to 2 meters high over grass, away from walls, pavement, and vents. A maximum-minimum thermometer records each day's high and low.

Humidity sensors drift and should be checked against a psychrometer. The barometer can sit indoors, but it must know its elevation. Wind is best measured at 10 meters in the open, so home readings are a compromise. A rain gauge needs a known opening and open space. Snow needs a board, a ruler, and a melted sample.

Check every instrument, clean it monthly, and doubt readings that don't make sense. Keep a station log, keep daily records, and share them.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, a careful backyard observer is one more set of eyes on its tides. Taken together, those eyes see what no single instrument can.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Cooperative observer:** A volunteer who takes daily weather readings for the national weather service.
**Maximum-minimum thermometer:** A thermometer that records the highest and lowest temperatures since it was last reset.
**Stevenson screen:** A white, slatted wooden box that shields thermometers from sunlight and rain.
**Radiation shield:** A small stack of white plates that does the same job for a home sensor.
**Aspirated shield:** A radiation shield with a fan that keeps air moving past the sensor.
**Hygrometer:** An instrument that measures humidity.
**Psychrometer:** A pair of thermometers, one dry and one wet, used to measure humidity.
**Aneroid barometer:** A barometer that uses a sealed metal can that flexes with the pressure.
**Station pressure:** The actual air pressure at the station's elevation.
**Anemometer:** An instrument that measures wind speed.
**Wind vane:** An instrument that points into the wind to show where it is coming from.
**Tipping-bucket gauge:** A rain gauge that counts rain by the tips of two tiny seesaw buckets.
**Snow board:** A flat white board used to measure new snow.
**Snow water equivalent:** The depth of water that snow would make if melted.
**Pyranometer:** An instrument that measures sunlight reaching the ground.
**Station log (metadata):** The written history of a station: where each instrument is, what is near it, and every change.
**CoCoRaHS:** The Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network, a volunteer network that measures precipitation.
