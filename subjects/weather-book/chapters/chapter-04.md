# Chapter 4
## The Cloud Families: Reading the Sky

Clouds are the ocean of air made visible.

Most of the time, the air's currents are hidden from us. We feel the wind on our faces, but we cannot see the great slow rivers of air rising, sinking, and sliding over one another far above. Clouds can. Every cloud marks a place where air has cooled to its dew point, and as Chapter 3 showed, that almost always means the air is rising. A cloud is a signpost that says: something is lifting here.

That makes the sky readable. A thin wisp of ice six kilometers up tells a different story from a gray sheet hanging over the treetops, or a white tower boiling upward on a summer afternoon. The height of a cloud, its shape, and whether it is growing or fading are all clues about what the air is doing, and often about what it will do next.

You don't need instruments to begin. You need a clear view, a little patience, and the willingness to look up before walking into a mailbox.

This chapter introduces the main cloud families, how they get their names, and what each one is telling you. By the end, the sky should look less like scenery and more like a message.

---

## 4.1 How Clouds Got Their Names

For most of history, clouds had no names at all.

People described them as they pleased: mares' tails, castles, sheep. Then, in 1802, a young English pharmacist named Luke Howard gave a lecture in London proposing something new. Clouds, he said, come in a few basic forms, and the forms can be named the way botanists name plants, in Latin.

His system worked so well that we still use it. Nearly every cloud name is built from a handful of roots.

| Root | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cirrus** (cirro-) | a curl of hair, wispy |
| **Cumulus** (cumulo-) | a heap or pile |
| **Stratus** (strato-) | a layer or sheet |
| **Alto-** | middle height |
| **Nimbus** (nimbo-) | rain-bearing |

Put the roots together and the name describes the cloud. **Cirrostratus** is a high, thin sheet. **Altocumulus** is a field of heaps at middle height. **Nimbostratus** is a layer that rains. **Cumulonimbus** is a heap so tall that it rains, and usually thunders.

So when you meet a new cloud name, you can usually take it apart and guess what the cloud looks like. The system is beautifully logical, right up until the sky starts mixing categories, which it does with enthusiasm.

## 4.2 The Four Cloud Families

Clouds sort themselves by height.

That isn't a human convenience. It follows from temperature. As Chapter 1 showed, the air gets colder the higher you go, so the height of a cloud decides what it is made of.

**High clouds** form above about 6 kilometers (20,000 feet), where the air is far below freezing. They are made almost entirely of ice crystals, which makes them thin, white, and fibrous.

**Middle clouds** form between about 2 and 6 kilometers (6,500 to 20,000 feet). They are usually water droplets, often supercooled, with ice mixed in when it is cold enough.

**Low clouds** form below about 2 kilometers (6,500 feet). They are mostly water droplets, and they are the clouds that turn a day gray.

The fourth family is different. **Heaped clouds** begin low but grow upward, sometimes through all three levels at once. They are made by the strong rising air of convection, and their height is a measure of how strongly the air is rising.

Within these families there are ten main cloud types. Learn those ten and you can name nearly anything in the sky.

The heights are only a guide. Near the poles, where the lower atmosphere is shallower, every family sits lower. In the tropics, it sits higher. And a single cloud doesn't always stay in its family. Clouds thicken, lower, spread, and grow, and watching them change is the heart of reading the sky.

## 4.3 High Clouds: Ice in the Upper Air

High clouds are often the first sign that the weather is about to change.

**Cirrus** is the cloud of feathers and brushstrokes: thin white streaks, often with hooked or curling ends. Those streaks are ice crystals falling slowly from a small cloud head, then stretched sideways by the fast winds aloft. The curl shows where the falling ice met slower air below. Old sailors called them mares' tails. A few scattered cirrus on a bright day usually mean nothing. But cirrus that thickens and spreads across the sky from one direction is often the leading edge of a storm system still hundreds of kilometers away.

**Cirrostratus** is a thin, milky veil that can cover the whole sky. The Sun or Moon shines through it clearly enough to cast shadows, and the sky turns from deep blue to a pale, washed-out white. Its calling card is the **halo**, a ring of light around the Sun or Moon. The ice crystals bend the light like tiny prisms. Chapter 15 explains exactly how. When cirrostratus thickens through a day, rain or snow often follows within about a day.

**Cirrocumulus** is a sheet of tiny white grains or ripples, arranged in rows like the scales on a fish. This is the famous **mackerel sky**. The cloudlets look tiny because they are so high and so far away. Cirrocumulus is rare and short-lived, and it shows gentle waves and stirring in the upper air.

All three share a few field marks. They are bright white, even when the lower sky is gray. They cast almost no shadow. And they often drift in a different direction from the clouds below, carried by different winds.

## 4.4 Middle Clouds: The Gray Between

Middle clouds are where a distant storm starts to feel close.

**Altostratus** is a gray or blue-gray sheet, thicker than cirrostratus. The Sun shows through it as a dim, blurry disk, as if seen through frosted glass, and it casts no shadow. There is no halo, because the cloud is too thick. Altostratus often forms as high cloud lowers and thickens ahead of widespread rain or snow. It can shed light precipitation itself, and when it thickens and darkens further, it becomes nimbostratus.

**Altocumulus** is a field of rounded patches, rolls, or clumps, white with gray shading on their undersides. It can look like flocks of sheep, rows of waves, or a cobbled street turned upside down. On its own, it rarely brings rain.

The hard part is telling it from cirrocumulus above or stratocumulus below. Your hand helps. Hold your arm straight out and compare the cloudlets to your fingers. Cirrocumulus pieces look smaller than the width of your little finger. Altocumulus pieces are about one to three fingers wide. Stratocumulus pieces are wider still. The trick works because the farther away a cloud is, the smaller its pieces look. Shading helps too: the lower the cloud, the darker its underside.

One kind of altocumulus is worth watching for. **Altocumulus castellanus** has small turrets rising from its tops, like a line of castle towers. On a warm, humid morning, it is a sign that the middle of the atmosphere is unstable. Thunderstorms often follow by afternoon.

## 4.5 Low Clouds: The Gray-Day Layer

Low clouds are the ones that make a day feel gray.

**Stratus** is a flat, featureless gray sheet, low enough to hide hilltops and the tops of tall buildings. It is the closest thing to fog off the ground, and often that is exactly what it is. A morning fog can lift into stratus as the ground warms. Stratus also forms when moist air drifts over cold ground or water, or when moisture is trapped beneath an inversion, the warm lid described in Chapter 2. It brings mist and drizzle, and very little else. It is the sky's way of being dull without being dramatic.

**Stratocumulus** is a low layer broken into large rolls, lumps, or patches, often with blue sky showing between them. Its pieces have darker undersides and lighter edges. It forms when cumulus clouds spread out beneath an inversion, behind a cold front, or over cool ocean water. It is the most common cloud on Earth, covering huge stretches of the oceans. It usually brings no more than light drizzle or a passing shower.

**Nimbostratus** is the cloud of a day that has fully committed to being wet. It is a thick, dark layer that hides the Sun completely and brings steady rain or snow for hours over a wide area. Its base is often hard to see, blurred by falling rain and by ragged scraps of low cloud drifting beneath it. Nimbostratus doesn't fit neatly into one family. Its base sinks into the low level, but it is usually thousands of meters deep, so official lists place it with the middle clouds. It almost always grows from altostratus that has thickened and lowered.

The quickest way to tell these apart is to ask two questions. Is there texture? Stratus has none; stratocumulus has plenty. And is it raining steadily? Only nimbostratus does that.

## 4.6 Heaped Clouds: From Fair Weather to Thunder

A cumulus cloud is a bubble of rising air you can see.

On a sunny morning, the ground heats and warm bubbles of air begin to rise, as Chapter 1 described. When they reach their dew point, they turn into clouds. That is why **cumulus** clouds have flat bases all at the same height, the invisible floor that Chapter 3 showed how to calculate. Their tops are where the rising bubbles run out of lift.

Small, bright cumulus with crisp edges and plenty of blue sky between them are **fair-weather cumulus**. They tend to appear by late morning and fade near sunset as the ground cools. They mean the air is rising, but not very far.

When the air is unstable, cumulus keeps growing. **Towering cumulus** rises in cauliflower heads and turrets, often taller than it is wide, with a base that darkens as the cloud deepens. It can grow noticeably in a few minutes. Towering cumulus can drop heavy showers on its own, and it is the stage just before a thunderstorm.

Sometimes a smooth, thin cap appears just above a rising tower, like a silk scarf draped over its head. This is a **pileus**. The tower is rising so quickly that it shoves a layer of moist air above it up to its dew point. Within minutes, the tower usually punches through it. A pileus is a sign of a very strong updraft.

If the tower keeps climbing until its top turns to ice, it becomes a **cumulonimbus**, the thunderstorm cloud. It can reach from a base a kilometer or so up to the top of the troposphere, more than 12 kilometers (40,000 feet) high. There, the rising air hits the stable layer above, as if pressing against a lid, and spreads sideways into a flat **anvil**. The strongest storms punch a dome above the anvil, called an **overshooting top**.

A cumulonimbus brings lightning, heavy rain, gusty winds, and sometimes hail. Its full story, from birth to collapse, belongs to Chapter 7. For now, one rule is enough: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Go inside.

## 4.7 Strange Shapes Below a Storm

The most dramatic clouds aren't separate types at all. They are features of a thunderstorm.

Look beneath a storm's anvil, especially near sunset, and you may see rounded pouches hanging down from it. These are **mammatus**. They form where cold, heavy air laden with ice and droplets sinks out of the anvil into drier air below. They look ominous, and they often mean a strong storm is nearby, but they do not mean a tornado is forming. Many appear after the worst of a storm has passed.

Near the ground, the storm's front edge is often marked by a long, low, wedge-shaped **shelf cloud**. It forms as cold air rushing out of the storm lifts the warm, moist air ahead of it. A shelf cloud means strong wind and a sudden drop in temperature are on the way.

Under the strongest storms, a smaller, isolated lowering called a **wall cloud** can hang beneath the rain-free part of the cloud base. It marks where the storm is drawing air in. A wall cloud that rotates is one of the warning signs of a tornado. Ragged, fast-moving scraps of low cloud, called **scud**, are often mistaken for funnels, but they drift and tumble without turning steadily.

Telling these apart takes practice, and the stakes are high. Chapter 7 explains how a storm's air moves to make them. Chapter 8 covers funnels and tornadoes. Chapter 19 sets the look-alikes side by side.

## 4.8 Clouds Drawn by the Wind

Some clouds are less about rising air than about the currents that carry it.

On a breezy day, cumulus sometimes line up in long, parallel rows stretching to the horizon. These are **cloud streets**. The wind near the ground has rolled itself into long, invisible tubes of turning air. Clouds form over the rising side of each roll, and clear gaps form over the sinking side. Cloud streets are common behind cold fronts and when cold air blows across warmer water.

When wind blows across a mountain range, the air can bounce up and down in waves for many kilometers downwind, like ripples in a current behind a rock on the seabed. Where the air rises in each wave, it cools and forms a cloud. Where it sinks, the cloud evaporates. The result can be rows of cloud bands, or smooth, lens-shaped **lenticular clouds**, sometimes stacked like a pile of plates. The strange thing about them is that they stay put. The wind may be howling straight through, but the cloud stays fixed in place, forming at its upwind edge and vanishing at its downwind edge. More than a few have been reported as flying saucers. Pilots read them as a warning of rough air.

Even airplanes make clouds. A **contrail**, short for condensation trail, forms when the hot, moist exhaust from a jet engine mixes with the frigid air at cruising height and freezes into ice. In dry air, a contrail vanishes within seconds. In air already close to full, it lingers for hours and spreads into a sheet of thin cirrus. Long-lasting contrails are a sign of moisture high aloft, and sometimes of thickening cloud to come.

Finally, watch the different layers. On many days, low clouds drift one way while high clouds race another. That difference is called **wind shear**, a change in wind speed or direction with height. It is your best free view of the three-dimensional atmosphere, and it matters a great deal for storms, as Chapters 5 and 7 explain.

## 4.9 Reading the Sky

A single cloud tells you a little. A sequence of clouds tells you a story.

The clearest story is the one that comes before a warm front. Warm air arriving from one direction slides up over cooler air at a long, gentle slope, as Chapter 6 will explain. Because the slope is so gentle, the first clouds appear far ahead of the front, very high up. Then, hour by hour, they get thicker and lower.

It begins with scattered cirrus, sometimes a day or more ahead. The cirrus spreads into a cirrostratus veil, and a halo may appear around the Sun. The veil thickens into altostratus, and the Sun fades to a dim smudge. Then the sky lowers into nimbostratus, and the steady rain or snow begins. Every step down the ladder brings the weather closer.

A cold front tells a faster, rougher story. Cold air plows under the warm air like a wedge, shoving it up steeply. Towering cumulus and thunderstorms can build along a narrow line. The wind shifts, the rain comes hard but briefly, and behind the front, cooler and drier air arrives with lumpy stratocumulus or clearing skies.

Real skies don't always follow these scripts, but they follow them often enough to be useful. And the habit behind them works anywhere. When you look up, ask a few simple questions. How high are the clouds, and what shape are they? Are they growing or fading? Sharp, bubbling edges mean growth. Soft, fraying edges mean the cloud is drying out. And most of all, is the sky changing? Clouds that thicken and lower usually mean the weather is getting worse. Clouds that lift and break usually mean it is getting better.

The trend is worth more than any single cloud. Learning to read it turns the sky from scenery into evidence.

# Field Observation
## Keep a Cloud Log

Once a day for a week, at about the same time, write down the main cloud types, how much of the sky they cover, which way they are moving, and whether they are growing or fading. Six hours later, note the weather.

Don't worry about perfect names. After a week, look back for repeated patterns.

You may find the sky was giving away the plot before your forecast app caught up.

---

# Try It
## Estimate Cloud Cover

Imagine the sky divided into eight equal slices. Count how many are covered by cloud. Weather reports use these words for the answer:

| Slices covered | Report |
|---|---|
| 0 | Clear |
| 1–2 | Few |
| 3–4 | Scattered |
| 5–7 | Broken |
| 8 | Overcast |

Repeat every fifteen minutes for an hour on a breezy or sunny afternoon.

You will see how quickly cloud cover can change. A "scattered" morning can be "broken" by lunch.

---

# Try It
## Watch the Layers Move

Stand still and line up a cloud with a fixed edge, such as a roof, a pole, or a tree branch. Watch which way it drifts and how fast. Then do the same with a cloud at a different height.

If the layers move in different directions, you are watching wind shear. Clouds near the horizon will seem slow even in strong wind, because they are so far away.

# Chapter Summary

Clouds show where the ocean of air is rising and cooling.

Their names come from a few Latin roots: cirrus for wisps, cumulus for heaps, stratus for layers, alto for middle height, and nimbus for rain.

Clouds sort by height. High clouds are made of ice. Middle and low clouds are mostly water droplets. Heaped clouds grow upward through every level.

Ten main types cover nearly everything in the sky: cirrus, cirrostratus, and cirrocumulus up high; altostratus, altocumulus, and nimbostratus in the middle; stratus and stratocumulus down low; and cumulus and cumulonimbus rising through them all.

A cumulus that keeps growing can become a thunderstorm. Anvils, mammatus, shelf clouds, and wall clouds are features of those storms.

Some clouds trace the wind itself: cloud streets, mountain wave clouds, and contrails.

High clouds that thicken and lower often mean rain or snow is coming. Clouds that lift and break mean it is clearing.

The trend matters more than any single cloud.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Cirrus:** A high, wispy cloud made of ice crystals.
**Cirrostratus:** A high, thin veil of ice that can make a halo.
**Cirrocumulus:** Tiny high cloudlets in ripples or rows; a mackerel sky.
**Halo:** A ring of light around the Sun or Moon, made by ice crystals.
**Altostratus:** A gray middle-level sheet that dims the Sun.
**Altocumulus:** Rounded middle-level patches or rolls.
**Altocumulus castellanus:** Altocumulus with small turrets, a sign of possible afternoon storms.
**Stratus:** A flat, low, featureless gray layer.
**Stratocumulus:** A low layer of large rolls or lumps.
**Nimbostratus:** A thick, dark layer that brings steady rain or snow.
**Cumulus:** A heaped cloud made by rising air, with a flat base.
**Towering cumulus:** A cumulus growing tall in cauliflower towers.
**Pileus:** A smooth cap cloud above a fast-rising tower.
**Cumulonimbus:** A thunderstorm cloud.
**Anvil:** The flat, spreading top of a thunderstorm.
**Overshooting top:** A dome rising above an anvil, a sign of a strong storm.
**Mammatus:** Rounded pouches hanging beneath a cloud, often an anvil.
**Shelf cloud:** A low wedge of cloud along a thunderstorm's leading edge.
**Wall cloud:** A lowering beneath a strong storm, where it draws air in.
**Scud:** Ragged, low scraps of cloud.
**Cloud streets:** Parallel rows of cumulus lined up with the wind.
**Lenticular cloud:** A smooth, lens-shaped cloud over or downwind of mountains.
**Contrail:** A trail of ice cloud made by an aircraft.
**Wind shear:** A change in wind speed or direction with height.
**Few, scattered, broken, overcast:** Weather-report terms for how much of the sky is covered.
