# Chapter 7
## Thunderstorms: From Fair-Weather Cloud to Violent Storm

A thunderstorm begins as a bubble of warm air leaving the bottom of the ocean of air.

Most bubbles don't get far. They rise, cool, and fade, or they build a small cumulus cloud with a flat base and a bright white top and stop there. But on some afternoons the air is warm, humid, and unstable, and one bubble keeps going. Its cloud swells into a tower. Within half an hour, the tower can reach the top of the weather layer, more than 12 kilometers (7 miles) above the ground.

Inside that tower, water vapor turns to cloud and gives up its heat. Droplets freeze. Hail grows. Electric charge builds until the air itself breaks down in a flash of lightning. Rain and ice pour out of the cloud, dragging cold air with them, and the cold air hits the ground and spreads out as a gust of wind.

A thunderstorm is the atmosphere evening things out in a hurry. In one tall column, it carries heat and moisture from the warm, wet floor of the ocean of air to the cold air high above, and it does in an hour what a sunny day would take weeks to do. Around the world, about 2,000 are going at any moment.

This chapter follows one storm from birth to collapse, then the storms that refuse to die, and finally the storm's weapons: falling wind, hail, lightning, and thunder.

---

## 7.1 Three Ingredients

Every thunderstorm needs three things: moisture, instability, and lift.

**Moisture** is the fuel. Water vapor supplies the cloud, the rain, and something more important: heat. As Chapter 1 explained, vapor that condenses into cloud releases its latent heat into the air around it. That heat keeps the rising air warmer than its surroundings, so it keeps rising. In North America, most of the moisture for storms east of the Rockies flows north from the Gulf of Mexico in the maritime tropical air of Chapter 6.

**Instability** lets the air keep climbing once it starts. As Chapter 2 showed, a rising bubble that stays warmer than the air around it is lighter, and it rises on its own. The more unstable the air, the faster and higher the bubble goes.

**Lift** gives the first push. Something has to start the air moving up to its cloud base, where the latent heat takes over. A cold front can do it, or a dryline, a sea-breeze front, or the outflow of an earlier storm, all from Chapter 6. So can a mountain slope, a hot patch of ground, or a disturbance in the jet stream overhead.

Take away any one ingredient and the storm doesn't happen.

There is a fourth ingredient, but it doesn't decide whether a storm forms. It decides what kind of storm forms. That is wind shear, the change in the wind with height from Chapter 5, and it returns in section 7.4.

## 7.2 Fuel and the Lid

Chapter 2 described a hidden switch in the air: stable until a cloud forms, then suddenly unstable. Forecasters measure that switch with two numbers.

Twice a day, weather balloons rise through the atmosphere and radio back its temperature and moisture at every height. From that profile, a forecaster can imagine lifting a bubble of surface air and ask, at each height, how much warmer or colder it would be than the air around it.

Where the bubble would be warmer, it gains energy, like a cork released under water. Add up that energy all the way to the top of the storm and you have **CAPE**, short for convective available potential energy. It is a measure of the storm's fuel. The higher the CAPE, the stronger the updraft can become. On a hot, humid afternoon on the Great Plains, it can be five times higher than on an ordinary summer day.

Near the ground, though, the bubble is often colder than the air around it. That happens under a cap, the warm layer aloft from Chapter 6. The energy it takes to push a bubble through that layer is **CIN**, or convective inhibition. It is the lid.

The best storm days often have both. A modest lid stops small, early storms from using up the fuel. Heat and moisture build beneath it all morning. When something finally breaks through, the storms that form are few, isolated, and powerful. Too strong a lid, and nothing forms at all. Much of severe-weather forecasting comes down to one question: will the cap break, and when?

A simpler number, the **lifted index**, compares the bubble with the surrounding air at one height, about 5.5 kilometers (18,000 feet) up. Below zero means unstable.

No single number makes a storm. A day with enormous CAPE and no lift stays quiet.

## 7.3 Birth, Peak, and Collapse

An ordinary thunderstorm lives about half an hour to an hour, and it passes through three stages.

It begins as a growing cumulus. In this **cumulus stage**, the cloud is all **updraft**, a current of rising air. Warm, moist air streams in at the base, cools as it rises, condenses, and releases its heat, which drives it higher still. The tower grows by the minute, with the crisp edges Chapter 4 described. Little or no rain reaches the ground yet.

The **mature stage** begins when rain and hail start to fall. Now the storm contains two currents side by side. The updraft still rises on one side. On the other, the falling rain and ice drag air down with them, and some of the rain evaporates into drier air at the storm's edges and chills it. Cold air is heavy, and it sinks. This is the **downdraft**. The top of the storm reaches the stable air at the top of the troposphere and spreads into an anvil.

This is the storm at its most dangerous, with the heaviest rain, strongest gusts, and most lightning.

Then the storm kills itself. In an ordinary storm, the downdraft falls through the same place as the updraft, and the cold air spreading out at the ground cuts off the supply of warm, moist air. In the **dissipating stage**, the updraft weakens and the downdraft takes over. The rain lightens, the cloud's edges soften and fray, and the lower cloud melts away. Often the anvil is the last part left, drifting downwind for hours after the storm below it is gone.

## 7.4 Storms That Outlive Themselves

Some storms live for hours. Their secret is wind shear.

When the wind blows faster, or from a different direction, higher up than it does near the ground, the storm leans. Its updraft tilts downwind, so the rain falls out beside the updraft instead of straight back down through it. The warm air keeps flowing in on one side while the cold air pours out on the other. The storm no longer chokes on its own downdraft.

With little shear, storms grow straight up and collapse in place. These are **single-cell** storms, the common summer afternoon thunderstorms. When one grows unusually strong in very unstable air, it can drop a sudden burst of damaging wind or hail before it dies. These are called **pulse storms**.

With some shear, storms organize into families. The cold air flowing out of one storm spreads along the ground like a spill of cold water across the sea floor. Its leading edge, the **gust front**, behaves like the small cold front of Chapter 6. It scoops up the warm, moist air ahead of it and lifts it, and a new storm grows there. That one rains, makes its own outflow, and sets off another. The result is a **multicell** cluster: young towers on one side, a mature storm in the middle, and old cells raining themselves out on the far side. Each cell lives less than an hour, but the family can last all afternoon.

The gust front often arrives minutes before the rain, under the shelf cloud of Chapter 4, formed where the warm air is pushed up over the cold.

With strong shear and plenty of fuel, storms can grow into supercells, the subject of 7.6.

## 7.5 Lines, Bows, and Derechos

Storms often join hands.

Along a cold front, or along the gust front of an earlier storm, cells can form side by side until they make a single line hundreds of kilometers long. This is a **squall line**. It arrives as a wall: a shelf cloud, a blast of wind, a short burst of very heavy rain, and then a long stretch of lighter rain behind. Its main danger is **straight-line wind**, wind that blows outward from the storm rather than spinning. The name says nothing about strength. Straight-line wind can be as destructive as a weak or moderate tornado.

Sometimes part of the line surges ahead and bends outward like a drawn bow. On radar, this is a **bow echo**. Behind it, a current of air called the **rear-inflow jet** dives down into the storm and drives the line forward. The worst wind is at the bow's tip, and brief tornadoes can spin up along its ends.

When a bow echo or a line keeps going for hours, it becomes a **derecho**, from the Spanish word for "straight." A derecho is not just a bad storm. It is a swath of wind damage at least about 400 kilometers (250 miles) long. The derecho of August 10, 2020, crossed Iowa and Illinois in a single day, flattening crops across a vast stretch of farmland and causing more than 11 billion dollars of damage.

The largest are **mesoscale convective systems**, clusters of storms big enough to act as one weather system, sometimes the size of a state. Fed by the low-level jet of Chapter 5, they can rumble across the central United States all night. When storms in a line keep passing over the same place, like the train cars of Chapter 6, that rain can become a flash flood. Chapter 11 tells that story.

## 7.6 The Supercell

A **supercell** is a thunderstorm with a spinning updraft.

Only a small fraction of storms become supercells, but they produce most of the largest hail and most of the strongest tornadoes. A supercell can last for hours and travel hundreds of kilometers. It can be nearly 20 kilometers (12 miles) across, a single giant storm rather than a family of small ones.

Its spin starts in the wind, long before the storm exists. When the wind near the ground is light and the wind a kilometer up is strong, the air between them rolls over, like a pencil rolled between your palms. The result is a long, invisible tube of air spinning on its side.

Then a strong updraft rises through the tube. It lifts the middle of the tube and tips it upright, turning sideways spin into upward spin. The rising air also stretches the spinning column taller and thinner, and like a figure skater pulling in their arms, it spins faster.

The result is a **mesocyclone**, a rotating updraft several kilometers wide. The spinning helps the updraft. It lowers the pressure inside the storm and pulls the rising air upward even harder. That is part of why supercells are so strong.

A mesocyclone is not a tornado. It is far wider and higher, and most mesocyclones never produce one. But nearly all strong tornadoes come from them.

Supercells need the most fuel and shear, so they are most common in spring on the Great Plains, where Gulf moisture, desert air, and the jet stream meet.

## 7.7 Inside a Supercell

A supercell has the same parts as an ordinary storm, organized so well that it can look like a machine.

The updraft is so strong that it holds rain and hail aloft. Beneath it, the cloud base stays dark but nearly dry, and this **rain-free base** is often where a storm chaser looks first. Under the strongest part of the updraft, the base can drop lower still, into the **wall cloud** of Chapter 4. It forms there because the storm is drawing in air that has been cooled by rain, which reaches its dew point sooner and turns to cloud closer to the ground.

The storm has two downdrafts. The **forward-flank downdraft** falls through the main area of rain and hail, ahead of the updraft. The **rear-flank downdraft** curls down around the back of the updraft. It can carve a clear slot in the clouds, and it wraps rain and hail around the mesocyclone. On radar, that rain can form a hook-shaped echo, one of the signs that Chapter 12 explains how to read.

In humid air, a **high-precipitation** supercell wraps heavy rain around its center, hiding hail and any tornado behind a curtain of water. In drier air, a **low-precipitation** supercell drops little rain, and its striped updraft can look too beautiful to be dangerous. It can still drop huge hail.

Where the rear-flank downdraft meets the warm, moist air flowing into the storm, the rotation near the ground can tighten into a tornado. Why one supercell makes a tornado and its neighbor does not is still one of the great open questions in meteorology. It seems to depend on just how warm and moist the downdraft is, and how the wind turns with height in the lowest kilometer. Tornadoes, how they form, and how to survive them are the whole subject of Chapter 8.

## 7.8 Wind That Falls from the Sky

On June 24, 1975, an airliner landing at New York's Kennedy Airport flew into a thunderstorm and crashed short of the runway. The meteorologist Ted Fujita studied the wreckage and the weather and concluded that a column of air had slammed down out of the storm and spread across the ground. He called it a **downburst**. Many scientists doubted him. Then, in the late 1970s, special Doppler radars caught dozens of them.

A downburst is a downdraft that hits the ground hard and spreads out in all directions, like water poured from a bucket onto a floor. A small one, less than 4 kilometers (2.5 miles) across, is a **microburst**. Its winds can top 160 km/h (100 mph), and its worst lasts only a few minutes. A larger one, a **macroburst**, can flatten forest for kilometers.

Falling rain drags the air down, and melting hail chills it, but the most powerful push comes from evaporation. When rain falls into dry air, part of it evaporates, and as Chapter 1 explained, evaporation takes heat from the air. The chilled air becomes heavy and falls faster. This is why the virga of Chapter 3 can be dangerous. A storm with little rain reaching the ground, high over a desert, can still produce a violent **dry microburst**. In humid places, **wet microbursts** come wrapped in a burst of heavy rain.

Microbursts are deadly to aircraft. A plane flying into one first meets a headwind that lifts it, then a downdraft that pushes it toward the ground, then a tailwind that robs it of lift. After the crash of Delta Flight 191 at Dallas in 1985, major airports began using special radars and wind sensors to spot them.

Downburst damage tells its own story. Trees fall outward in a starburst, pointing away from where the air hit, while a tornado leaves them in swirls. Fujita learned to read that difference from the air, as Chapter 8 tells.

## 7.9 Hail

Hail is the strangest gift of summer: balls of ice falling on a hot day.

It begins high in the storm, where the air is well below freezing but much of the cloud is still liquid. These **supercooled droplets** stay liquid until they touch something solid. A tiny pellet of ice, called a hail embryo, is swept up by the updraft and collides with them. They freeze onto it, layer by layer.

A hailstone grows as long as the updraft can hold it up. It may ride up and down through the storm, or drift slowly across the updraft, gathering ice the whole way. When it grows too heavy for the rising air, or slips out of the updraft, it falls.

Slice a large hailstone open and you may find rings, like an onion. Clear ice forms where water spread over the stone and froze slowly, letting air bubbles escape. Milky ice forms where droplets froze on contact, trapping tiny bubbles. The rings record the stone's changing journey, but they don't count trips up and down, as was once thought.

Big hail needs a big updraft. A hailstone the size of a softball falls faster than 150 km/h (90 mph), and the storm has to blow upward at least that fast to grow it. That is why giant hail comes mostly from supercells, on days with high CAPE and strong shear.

Large hail often falls on the edges of the rain, sometimes from what looks like a thin part of the storm. The heaviest rain is not always where the hail is. In the United States, a storm counts as severe when its hail is 2.5 centimeters (1 inch) across or larger, the size of a quarter. Chapter 16 tells the story of the record stones.

## 7.10 How a Storm Charges Up

A thunderstorm is a giant electrical generator, and its moving parts are ice.

High in the storm, the updraft carries tiny ice crystals upward past soft pellets of rimed ice called **graupel**, which are heavier and falling. In the presence of supercooled droplets, every collision between a crystal and a graupel pellet moves a little electric charge from one to the other. Over most of the storm, the small crystals come away with positive charge and are carried up toward the anvil. The graupel takes negative charge and sinks into the middle of the cloud.

Billions of collisions a second build up a typical arrangement: positive charge near the top, a large region of negative charge in the middle, at heights where the cloud is around −10 to −20°C (14 to −4°F), and often a small positive pocket near the base. Real storms are messier, but this is the usual picture.

Air is normally a good insulator. But as the charge grows, the electric push between the layers grows too, until the air breaks down and a spark jumps.

Most lightning never leaves the cloud. About three-quarters of all flashes jump between charge regions inside one storm, lighting the whole cloud from within, or leap from one cloud to another. The rest strike the ground, and those are the ones that matter most to us.

## 7.11 The Lightning Bolt

A lightning strike that seems to last an instant is really a conversation between the cloud and the ground.

It starts with a faint, branching channel of charged air called the **stepped leader**, which works its way down from the cloud in jumps of about 50 meters (160 feet), too dim to see. As it nears the ground, the electric push becomes so strong that sparks called **upward streamers** rise to meet it from trees, poles, rooftops, and sometimes people.

When a streamer and the leader touch, the circuit is complete. A huge current rushes through the channel in a **return stroke**, and that is the brilliant flash we see. Most flashes then send more strokes down the same channel, three or four on average, a few hundredths of a second apart. That is why lightning flickers.

The channel is only a few centimeters wide, but for an instant it is several times hotter than the surface of the Sun.

A small share of strikes come from the positive charge at the top of the storm. **Positive lightning** is often more powerful and lasts longer, which makes it better at starting fires. Because it comes from the anvil, it can strike far from the rain. Lightning can travel 15 kilometers (10 miles) or more sideways from a storm and hit the ground under blue sky. This is the **bolt from the blue**, and it is why a storm that seems to be leaving is still dangerous.

Lightning also flashes upward. Above the biggest storms, brief red **sprites** and blue **jets** dance in the thin air dozens of kilometers up. Pilots reported them for decades before anyone caught one on camera, in 1989.

## 7.12 Thunder

Thunder is the sound of air being blown apart.

The lightning channel heats the air around it so fast that the air has no time to move out of the way. It expands explosively and sends out a shock wave, which quickly becomes a sound wave. Close to a strike, thunder is a single sharp crack. Farther away, it becomes a long, rolling rumble. The bolt is several kilometers long, and its different parts are at different distances from you, so their sound arrives one after another. Hills, buildings, and layers of air echo it and stretch it out further.

Light reaches you almost instantly, but sound travels only about one kilometer every 3 seconds (one mile every 5 seconds). To estimate a strike's distance, count the seconds between the flash and the thunder, then divide by 3 for kilometers, or by 5 for miles. A 15-second count means a strike about 5 kilometers (3 miles) away.

That count tells you distance, not safety. Thunder can be heard about 15 kilometers (10 miles) away, roughly the same distance lightning can reach from a storm. So the rule is simple: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

On warm summer nights, you may see the sky flicker with no sound at all. This is sometimes called **heat lightning**, but it is not a special kind. It is ordinary lightning from a storm too far away for its thunder to reach you, sometimes beyond the horizon, lighting up distant clouds.

# Weather Safety
## Lightning

- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** No place outdoors is safe once you can hear thunder.
- **Safe places:** a large enclosed building with plumbing and wiring, or a hard-topped car or truck with the windows closed. The metal body carries the current around you. The rubber tires do not protect you.
- **Not safe:** open fields, hilltops, beaches, water, boats, tents, picnic shelters, porches, dugouts, and under trees.
- **Indoors,** stay off corded phones, away from plumbing, and away from windows and doors. Don't lean on concrete walls. Lightning can travel through wiring, pipes, and the metal inside concrete.
- **Wait 30 minutes** after the last thunder before going back outside. The last flash doesn't announce itself.
- **If someone is struck,** call for help and begin CPR if needed. A lightning victim carries no charge and is safe to touch.

*For damaging wind, find shelter as you would for lightning, away from windows and trees. For tornado safety, see Chapter 8. For flash floods, see Chapter 11.*

# Field Observation
## Watch a Storm Grow

On a warm, humid afternoon, watch one cumulus cloud from indoors or from a doorway. Note the time it first appears, when its top starts to tower, and when it forms an anvil. When the storm comes, write down the time of the first thunder, the first cool gust, and the first rain.

Did the gust arrive before the rain? That was the gust front. Compare your notes with the stages in Visual Spread 7.3. Once you hear thunder, watch through a window.

# Try It
## Track the Lightning

From indoors, count the seconds between flashes and their thunder for several minutes. Divide by 3 for kilometers or by 5 for miles, and write each one down.

If the counts get shorter, the storm is coming toward you. If they get longer, it is moving away. Be indoors before you start counting.

# Chapter Summary

Thunderstorms need moisture, instability, and lift. Wind shear decides what kind of storm forms.

CAPE measures the storm's fuel, and CIN measures the lid that holds it back. The best storm days often have plenty of both, until the lid breaks.

An ordinary storm lives about an hour, through cumulus, mature, and dissipating stages. It dies when its own downdraft cuts off its fuel.

With wind shear, storms lean, separate their updrafts from their downdrafts, and live much longer. Their gust fronts set off new storms, building clusters, lines, bow echoes, and derechos.

A supercell is a storm with a rotating updraft. It gets its spin from wind shear and makes most of the largest hail and strongest tornadoes.

Downbursts are columns of air that crash to the ground and spread out. Evaporation makes them stronger.

Hail grows when supercooled droplets freeze onto ice held aloft by a strong updraft.

Lightning comes from charge separated by colliding ice. Thunder is the sound of the air it heats. If you can hear thunder, you can be struck.

A thunderstorm is the ocean of air turning over in a hurry, its warm floor rushing up to the top in a single tower.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Thunderstorm:** A storm that produces lightning and thunder.
**Wind shear:** A change in wind speed or direction with height.
**CAPE:** A measure of the energy available to rising air: the storm's fuel.
**CIN (convective inhibition):** The energy needed to push air through a stable layer near the ground: the lid.
**Lifted index:** A simple instability number. Below zero means unstable.
**Updraft and downdraft:** The rising and sinking currents in a storm.
**Gust front:** The leading edge of cool air spreading out from a storm.
**Single-cell and pulse storms:** Short-lived storms with one updraft. Pulse storms flare up and collapse fast.
**Multicell:** A family of storm cells at different stages.
**Squall line:** A long line of thunderstorms.
**Straight-line wind:** Damaging wind that blows outward from a storm rather than spinning.
**Bow echo:** A bulging line of storms that brings strong wind.
**Derecho:** A long swath of wind damage from a line of storms, at least 400 kilometers (250 miles) long.
**Mesoscale convective system:** A storm cluster big enough to act as one weather system.
**Supercell:** A thunderstorm with a rotating updraft.
**Mesocyclone:** The rotating updraft of a supercell.
**Rain-free base:** The dark, dry cloud base under a strong updraft.
**Forward-flank and rear-flank downdrafts:** The two downdrafts of a supercell.
**Downburst, microburst, macroburst:** A downdraft that strikes the ground and spreads out. Microbursts are under 4 kilometers (2.5 miles) across.
**Supercooled droplets:** Cloud droplets still liquid below freezing.
**Graupel:** Soft pellets of rimed ice.
**Stepped leader and return stroke:** The faint channel that starts a lightning strike, and the bright flash that completes it.
**Positive lightning:** A strike from the positive charge at the top of a storm.
**Bolt from the blue:** Lightning that strikes far from its storm, under clear sky.
**Heat lightning:** Distant lightning too far away to hear.
