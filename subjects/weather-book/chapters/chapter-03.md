# Chapter 3
## Water Vapor, Humidity, Clouds, and the Birth of Rain

Every cloud begins invisibly.

Look up on a clear summer morning and the sky seems empty. It isn't. The air around you holds tons of water, spread through it as a gas you cannot see. By afternoon, some of that water may be towering overhead as a thunderhead. By evening, it may be falling on your roof.

Chapter 1 introduced water as the hidden fuel of the weather machine: it soaks up heat when it evaporates and gives that heat back when it condenses. This chapter follows the water itself. How much can the air hold? How can you tell how much is there? What turns invisible vapor into a cloud, and why do most clouds drift by without shedding a drop?

The answers come down to one simple event, repeated everywhere in the ocean of air. Air cools until it is full of water vapor, and then the vapor has to go somewhere.

Where it goes is the story of dew on the grass, fog in the valley, clouds in the sky, and rain on the window.

---

## 3.1 Water You Cannot See

Water vapor is invisible. Everything you can see is something else.

The white plume above a boiling kettle is not vapor. Look closely and you will find a clear gap just above the spout. That gap is the vapor. A few centimeters later, it cools and condenses into countless tiny droplets, and those are what you see. Clouds, fog, and your breath on a cold morning work the same way. All of them are liquid droplets or ice crystals floating in the air.

That is why a muggy tropical afternoon can look perfectly clear. The air is loaded with water, but all of it is still gas.

Water vapor enters the air by **evaporation**, as Chapter 1 described, mostly from the oceans. Plants add a great deal more. They draw water up through their roots and breathe it out through tiny pores in their leaves, a process called **transpiration**. A single large tree can release more than a hundred liters (about 30 gallons) on a summer day. Over a forest or a cornfield, plants can put about as much water into the air as a lake of the same size.

Once it is aloft, water doesn't stay long. A typical molecule of vapor spends about nine days in the air before it falls back as rain or snow. Then it may sit in a lake for years, in deep groundwater for centuries, or in a glacier for thousands of years before it rises again.

This endless loop is the **water cycle**. It is not a neat conveyor belt. It is more like the ocean's own currents, with countless paths and side eddies, all moving water from where it is plentiful to where it is not.

## 3.2 How Much Can the Air Hold?

Warm air can hold much more water vapor than cold air before it is full.

When the air holds all the vapor it can at its temperature, it is **saturated**. Add more vapor, or cool it down, and some of the vapor has to condense.

The limit climbs steeply with temperature. It roughly doubles for every 10°C (18°F) of warming. Saturated air at 30°C (86°F) holds about seven times as much water as saturated air at freezing. That is why tropical air can be so heavy with moisture, and why the coldest winter air is always dry.

"Warm air holds more water" is a handy shortcut, but it is only a shortcut. Air is not a sponge, and it doesn't grip the water. What really changes with temperature is the water itself. In warmer air, the water molecules move faster and are more likely to stay as vapor instead of clumping back into droplets. The result is the same, so this book uses the shortcut.

This one fact drives the rest of the chapter. Cool any air enough, and it will reach its limit. Then clouds, fog, and dew begin.

## 3.3 Relative Humidity

The humidity on a weather report doesn't tell you how much water is in the air. It tells you how full the air is.

**Relative humidity** compares the vapor in the air to the most it could hold at its current temperature. Air that is half full has a relative humidity of 50%. Saturated air is at 100%.

The catch is that the size of the jar changes with temperature. Picture a calm summer day. At sunrise, the air is 16°C (61°F) and the relative humidity is 95%. By mid-afternoon, it is 28°C (82°F) and the relative humidity has dropped to about 45%.

No water left the air. Not a drop. The air simply warmed, its jar got bigger, and the same water now fills less than half of it. At sunset the air cools, the jar shrinks, and the humidity climbs again.

So relative humidity usually rises at night and falls during the day, even when the moisture in the air hasn't changed at all. That makes it useful for some things. It tells you how quickly laundry will dry and how likely dew or fog is. But it is a poor way to compare how muggy one day is with another.

For that, we need a better number.

## 3.4 Dew Point

The **dew point** is the temperature the air would have to cool to before it became saturated.

It answers the question relative humidity cannot: how much water is actually in the air? The more vapor there is, the less the air has to cool before it fills up, so the higher the dew point. Unlike relative humidity, the dew point stays put when the air warms or cools. It changes only when water is added or removed, or when a new body of air moves in.

That makes it the best single measure of how humid the air feels. Two summer afternoons can both be 30°C (86°F). With a dew point of 7°C (45°F), the day feels crisp and pleasant. With a dew point of 24°C (75°F), it feels like breathing through a warm towel. Sweat barely evaporates, and the heat index described in Chapter 2 soars.

The gap between the temperature and the dew point is just as useful. When the two are far apart, the air is dry and the sky tends to stay clear. When they draw close together, the air is nearly full. Watch for dew, fog, or low clouds.

Forecasters watch dew points to track air masses, too. A sudden drop in dew point often marks the arrival of drier air behind a cold front, as Chapter 6 will show.

The dew point can never be higher than the temperature. If the air cools to meet it, something has to give.

## 3.5 Dew and Frost

On a clear, calm night, the grass gets wet without a drop of rain.

As Chapter 2 showed, the ground loses heat to the sky on clear nights and chills the air touching it. When that thin layer of air cools to its dew point, vapor condenses on the coldest surfaces it can find: blades of grass, car roofs, spider webs. That is **dew**. It doesn't fall. It forms right where you find it.

If the surface is below freezing, the vapor skips the liquid stage and turns straight to ice. This change, from gas directly to solid, is called **deposition**, and it makes **frost**. Frost forms in delicate feathers and needles because it grows one molecule at a time, straight out of the air.

The same thing happens on a glass of iced lemonade on a summer day. The outside of the glass gets wet. The water didn't leak through. It came from the air, which cooled to its dew point when it touched the cold glass.

Clouds and wind both prevent dew. Clouds act like a blanket and keep the ground from cooling. Wind keeps stirring warmer air down to the surface. That is why the heaviest dew and the thickest frost come on the clearest, stillest nights.

## 3.6 Every Droplet Needs a Seed

Cool air to its dew point, and droplets still need something to form on.

In perfectly clean air, a droplet would have a hard time getting started. A few water molecules that clump together tend to fly apart again. Clean air would need to be several times past full before droplets could form on their own.

Real air is never that clean. It is full of tiny particles of sea salt, dust, smoke, soot, pollen, and pollution, just as Chapter 1 showed in its cube of air. These **condensation nuclei** give the vapor a surface to gather on. Salt is especially good at it, because it attracts water. Over the oceans, every cloud droplet may have started on a speck of salt flung up by a breaking wave.

There are so many of these seeds that clouds almost never have to wait for them. Even the sky needs a little dust to get started.

Ice is pickier. It needs special particles, such as certain kinds of mineral dust or even bacteria, to freeze on. Those are much rarer, and that scarcity will matter when we reach the birth of snow.

## 3.7 Lifting Air to Its Dew Point

Almost every cloud forms in air that is rising.

Chapter 2 showed why. Rising air expands and cools. Lift moist air high enough, and it cools to its dew point. Vapor condenses on its seeds, and a cloud appears. The height where this happens is the **lifting condensation level**, and on a summer day you can see it plainly. It is the flat base of a cumulus cloud. Every rising bubble reaches its dew point at about the same height, so the clouds all begin on the same invisible floor.

You can estimate that height yourself. Subtract the dew point from the temperature, and multiply by 125. The answer is the height of the cloud base in meters. (In Fahrenheit, divide the difference by 4.4 and multiply by 1,000 for the height in feet.) A day of 28°C with a dew point of 16°C gives a cloud base near 1,500 meters (about 5,000 feet).

So what lifts the air? There are four main ways.

The Sun heats the ground, and warm bubbles of air rise on their own. This is convection, from Chapter 1, and it builds the puffy clouds of summer afternoons.

Wind pushes air up a mountainside. Chapter 2 followed a bubble on exactly this journey, and saw it drop its rain on the near slope.

Cold air plows under warm air, or warm air slides up over cold, and the warm air is forced to climb. This happens along fronts, the subject of Chapter 6.

And when winds flow together near the ground, the air piles up and has nowhere to go but up. This is **convergence**. The sea breezes colliding over Florida in Chapter 2 are one example.

Most clouds are a mix of these. But every one of them is a sign that air somewhere is rising.

## 3.8 Why Most Clouds Never Rain

A small summer cloud can hold hundreds of tonnes of water and never drop any of it.

The problem is size. A typical cloud droplet is about 0.02 millimeters across, far thinner than a human hair. A raindrop is about 100 times wider. That means it takes about a million cloud droplets to make a single raindrop.

Droplets that small barely fall at all. They drift down about a centimeter per second, and even a gentle updraft holds them up. Many evaporate at the cloud's edges long before they get anywhere. That is why clouds float, and why a cloud's edges are always changing.

A cloud is not a solid object. It is more like a waterfall. The shape stays, but the water inside it is always moving. Droplets form where air rises and cools, and they vanish where air sinks or mixes with dry air. Watch a small cumulus for ten minutes and you will see it grow, shred, and sometimes disappear.

For rain to fall, droplets have to grow a million times bigger, and fast. Nature has two ways to do it. Which one a cloud uses depends on whether it reaches below freezing.

How thick a cloud is, not what's inside it, decides whether it looks white or dark. Chapter 15 explains why.

## 3.9 Two Roads to Rain

In warm clouds, raindrops grow by bumping into each other.

No two droplets are exactly the same size. The bigger ones fall a little faster, overtake the smaller ones, and swallow them. Each merger makes the drop bigger and faster, so it catches even more. This is **collision and coalescence**. It works best in deep clouds with plenty of moisture, and it makes most of the rain in the tropics.

In colder clouds, rain usually starts as snow.

Cloud droplets don't freeze at 0°C (32°F). With so few ice seeds around, most stay liquid well below freezing. These **supercooled** droplets can survive down to about −40°C (−40°F). So the upper part of many clouds is a mix of a few ice crystals among a vast crowd of supercooled droplets.

In that mix, the ice wins. Air that is just full for liquid water is overfull for ice, so vapor leaves the droplets and builds up on the crystals. The droplets shrink and the crystals grow, until they are heavy enough to fall. This is the **ice-crystal process**, sometimes named for the Swedish scientist Tor Bergeron, who described it.

Falling crystals tangle together into **snowflakes**. Each one grows in a shape set by the temperature and moisture it passes through: flat plates, long needles, or the classic six-armed star. Snowflakes have six sides because of the way water molecules fit together in ice.

If the air below is warm, the snowflakes melt on the way down and arrive as rain. In the middle latitudes, much of the rain, even on a summer day, began its fall as snow.

## 3.10 What Reaches the Ground

Whatever forms in the cloud, the air below decides what lands.

**Rain** is made of drops larger than about half a millimeter. They aren't shaped like teardrops. Small drops are round. Larger ones are flattened by the air rushing past, so they look more like hamburger buns. A drop that grows much bigger than half a centimeter is torn apart into smaller drops.

**Drizzle** is made of much smaller drops, packed close together and falling slowly. It comes from low, shallow clouds, often in stable air, where drops have little room to grow.

Sometimes rain or snow falls from a cloud but never arrives. It evaporates in dry air on the way down, leaving gray streaks hanging beneath the cloud like a curtain that stops short of the floor. This is **virga**. Evaporation takes heat from the air, as Chapter 1 explained, so the air beneath virga cools, grows heavy, and can plunge to the ground as a sudden gust. Chapter 7 shows how violent that can be.

In winter, the layers of air between cloud and ground decide whether snow survives the trip, melts into rain, or turns into sleet or freezing rain. A difference of only a degree or two can change everything. Chapter 10 tells that story.

Hail is something else again. It grows inside thunderstorms, mostly in spring and summer, and belongs to Chapter 7.

## 3.11 Fog: A Cloud That Touches Down

Fog is a cloud at the bottom of the ocean of air.

There is no real difference between the two except where they form. A cloud forms when air rises and cools to its dew point. Fog forms when the air right at the ground reaches its dew point, either by cooling or by gaining moisture. Walk into a cloud on a mountaintop, and you will call it fog.

The most common kind is **radiation fog**. On a clear, calm night, the ground loses heat to the sky and chills the air above it, just as it does when forming dew. If enough air cools to its dew point, the dew becomes a fog. It is thickest near sunrise, in low places, and after a rainy day has left the ground wet. A light breeze can stir it deeper, but a strong wind prevents it. Once the sun climbs, it usually burns off within a few hours.

In valleys, radiation fog collects in the pools of cold air that Chapter 2 described, filling the valley floor like milk in a bowl while the hilltops stay clear.

**Advection fog** forms when warm, moist air flows over a cold surface. It is the fog of coastlines, where sea air drifts over cold ocean currents and rolls ashore. Because wind carries it, advection fog can last all day and travel far inland.

**Steam fog** is the opposite: cold air moving over warm water. Water evaporates into the cold air so fast that it fills up at once, and wisps of fog rise from the surface like steam from a bath. On a frosty morning, lakes and rivers can smoke.

**Upslope fog** forms when moist air is pushed up a slope and cools as it climbs, the same way a mountain cloud forms, only lower.

However it forms, fog always means the same thing. The air at the ground is full.

# Field Observation
## Read the Dew Point

On three different days, write down the temperature, the dew point, the relative humidity, and how the air feels: dry, comfortable, humid, or oppressive. Most weather apps show all three numbers.

Try to catch a dry day, a muggy day, and a foggy or rainy one.

Compare how each day felt with each number. The dew point will usually match your sense of comfort better than the relative humidity does.

# Field Observation
## Watch a Cloud Form

On a warm day with small, scattered cumulus clouds, pick one cloud and sketch it every two minutes for ten minutes.

Notice whether its base stays flat, its top grows, and its edges fray away.

You are watching the air rise, cool, condense, and dry out again. Clouds are terrible at holding still for portraits.

# Try It
## Make Dew

**You will need:** two glasses, room-temperature water, and ice water.

Fill one glass with room-temperature water and the other with ice water. Wait a few minutes and watch the outsides.

Droplets form on the cold glass only. The water came out of the air, which cooled to its dew point when it touched the glass.

---

# Try It
## Make a Cloud in a Jar

**You will need:** a clear glass jar, warm water, a metal lid or small plate, and ice.

Pour a little warm water into the jar and swirl it around. Set the lid on top and pile ice cubes on it. Watch the top of the jar.

The warm water fills the air inside with vapor. The cold lid chills it to its dew point, and a faint cloud forms.

You have made weather in a jar.

# Chapter Summary

Water vapor is invisible. Clouds, fog, and the plume above a kettle are tiny droplets, not vapor.

Warm air can hold far more vapor than cold air before it is saturated.

Relative humidity tells you how full the air is, and it changes whenever the temperature changes. The dew point tells you how much water is really there.

When the ground cools air to its dew point, dew or frost forms on the surface. When it cools a deeper layer, fog forms.

Every droplet needs a tiny particle to form on, and the air is full of them.

Rising air cools to its dew point and forms clouds. Air is lifted by heating, mountains, fronts, and winds that collide.

Most clouds never rain, because their droplets are too small to fall. Rain grows when droplets merge in warm clouds, or when ice crystals grow at the expense of supercooled droplets in cold ones.

Cool any air enough, and it will be full. Then the water has to go somewhere.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Transpiration:** Water vapor given off by plants through their leaves.
**Water cycle:** The endless movement of water between the surface and the air.
**Saturated:** Holding as much water vapor as possible at its temperature.
**Relative humidity:** How full the air is of water vapor, as a percentage.
**Dew point:** The temperature the air must cool to before it is saturated.
**Dew:** Water that condenses on cold surfaces.
**Deposition:** Water vapor turning straight into ice.
**Frost:** Ice crystals that grow on cold surfaces by deposition.
**Condensation nucleus:** A tiny particle that a cloud droplet forms on.
**Lifting condensation level:** The height where rising air reaches its dew point, usually the cloud base.
**Convergence:** Winds flowing together and forcing air upward.
**Collision and coalescence:** Cloud droplets bumping together and merging into raindrops.
**Supercooled:** Liquid water that is colder than freezing.
**Ice-crystal process:** Ice crystals growing by taking vapor from supercooled droplets.
**Virga:** Rain or snow that evaporates before it reaches the ground.
**Fog:** A cloud at ground level.
