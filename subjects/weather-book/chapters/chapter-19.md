# Chapter 19
## The Field Guide: Identifying Clouds, Fronts, Storms, and Sky Signs at a Glance

In 1934, a young art teacher named Roger Tory Peterson published *A Field Guide to the Birds*. The first printing of 2,000 copies sold out within a week.

Bird books already existed. What Peterson changed was how they worked. Instead of describing every feather, he painted similar birds side by side, in the same pose, and showed the few marks that tell them apart: a stripe over the eye, a patch on the wing, the shape of the tail in flight. You didn't need to know everything about a bird to name it. You needed to know where to look.

This chapter does the same for the sky. The earlier chapters explained how each cloud, storm, and front works. Here, the explanations are left behind. Each page is a picture key or a pair of look-alikes, with the marks that tell them apart and a pointer back to the chapter that explains them.

The question on every page is the same one a birdwatcher asks. What am I looking at, and does it matter right now?

## 19.1 How to Use This Guide

In 1896, the International Meteorological Committee published the first *International Cloud Atlas*. It was the work of three meteorologists, Hugo Hildebrandsson of Sweden, Albert Riggenbach of Switzerland, and Léon Teisserenc de Bort of France, and it did something new: it printed pictures of clouds with agreed international names, so that an observer in Uppsala and an observer in Washington would call the same cloud the same thing.

The atlas worked because it gave everyone the same questions to ask. This guide uses four.

**Shape.** Is it heaped, layered, or wispy? Heaped clouds grow upward on rising air. Layered clouds spread out where a whole sheet of air is lifted. Wispy ones are ice high up (Chapter 4, 4.2).

**Height.** Is it high, middle, or low? Use the hand test from Chapter 4: hold your hand at arm's length, and compare the size of the cloud's pieces with your fingers (Visual Spread 4.4).

**Trend.** Is it growing, spreading, thickening, or fading? One look tells you what a cloud is. Ten minutes tells you what it is doing. The trend is usually the more important answer.

**The whole sky.** What else is there? Look all the way around, including behind you and at the horizon. A single dark cloud means little. A line of them, a change in the wind, and a drop in temperature mean a lot.

### Illustration 1: The four questions

*A circle divided into four quarters, each with a small sky scene. Top left, "Shape": three thumbnails, a heap, a sheet, and a wisp. Top right, "Height": a person holding a hand at arm's length against a patch of cloud, with three bands behind them labeled high, middle, and low. Bottom right, "Trend": the same cumulus drawn three times in a row, growing taller, with a clock face over each one reading 2:00, 2:10, and 2:20. Bottom left, "The whole sky": a person turning in a circle under a dome of sky, with a line of dark cloud far behind them. Caption: "Ask all four, in this order, every time."*

## 19.2 The Cloud Key

In the autumn of 1821, the painter John Constable wrote to a friend, "I have done a good deal of skying." He had been painting nothing but clouds, quickly, in oil on paper. On the backs of many sketches he wrote down the weather, the direction of the light, and the time of day. He knew Luke Howard's new cloud names (Chapter 4, 4.1), and he wanted to paint each kind truthfully.

You don't need to paint them. But Constable's habit is the right one: look at a cloud long enough to see what it really is, and write it down.

Chapter 4 explained the ten main cloud types and drew them all on one page (Visual Spread 4.2, The Cloud Atlas). The key below is for identifying them quickly, by asking two questions: what shape, and how high?

### Visual Spread 19.2: The Ten-Cloud Key

*A grid, three rows by three columns, with one tall extra panel down the right side. The rows are labeled from the top: high, middle, low. The columns are labeled: wispy or veil-like, layered sheet, and rows of small heaps. Each square holds a small painting of the cloud, its name, and one field mark. The tall panel on the right shows the two heaped clouds that cross all three rows.*

- ***High, wispy: cirrus.** "Thin white threads and hooks. No shadows."*
- ***High, sheet: cirrostratus.** "A milky veil. Look for a halo around the Sun."*
- ***High, small heaps: cirrocumulus.** "Tiny ripples, smaller than your little fingertip at arm's length."*
- ***Middle, sheet: altostratus.** "Gray sheet. The Sun looks like a coin seen through frosted glass. No halo."*
- ***Middle, small heaps: altocumulus.** "Rolls or patches with shaded sides, about a thumb wide at arm's length."*
- ***Low, sheet: stratus.** "Flat gray ceiling. Drizzle at most."*
- ***Low, small heaps: stratocumulus.** "Lumpy gray rolls, about a fist wide at arm's length, with gaps."*
- ***Low, thick sheet: nimbostratus.** "Dark, formless, and raining or snowing steadily."*
- ***Tall panel, heaped: cumulus.** "Flat base, rounded top, sharp edges."*
- ***Tall panel, heaped and huge: cumulonimbus.** "Towering, with an anvil or a fuzzy, icy top. Thunder."*

*Footer: "Can't decide between two? Pick the one the trend supports, and look again in ten minutes."*

## 19.3 Look-Alikes in the High Sky

On September 11, 2001, after the attacks in the United States, every commercial flight in the country was grounded for three days. For the first time in decades, American skies had almost no contrails. Scientists comparing records from about 4,000 weather stations found that the gap between daytime highs and nighttime lows had widened by about 1.1°C (2°F), the largest jump in 30 years. It looked like proof that contrails had been shading the days and blanketing the nights. Later studies found that a natural change in cloud cover could explain most of it.

The story is a lesson for any field observer. A striking pattern is not the same as a cause. And the high sky is full of look-alikes.

The key below sets the common pairs side by side. Chapter 4 explains contrails (4.8), and Chapter 15 explains halos, coronas, and sundogs (15.8 to 15.10).

### Visual Spread 19.3: Up High, Side by Side

*Four pairs of small paintings, each pair facing across a vertical line, with the field marks circled.*

- ***Contrail or cirrus?** "A contrail starts as a straight line behind a plane, often in a crossing grid. Cirrus has no plane, and its streaks curve and hook. A contrail that spreads into a sheet means the high air is moist. That's the same clue cirrus gives (Chapter 4, 4.8)."*
- ***Halo or corona?** "A halo is a large ring, about a hand-span from the Sun, with a reddish inner edge, made by ice crystals. A corona is a small, tight glow hugging the Sun or Moon, with blue inside and red outside, made by water droplets (Chapter 15, 15.8 and 15.10)."*
- ***Sundog or rainbow?** "A sundog is a bright, colored spot beside the Sun, a hand-span away, at the same height. A rainbow is always on the opposite side of the sky from the Sun (Chapter 15, 15.5 and 15.9)."*
- ***Cirrostratus or altostratus?** "Through cirrostratus the Sun is sharp, makes shadows, and may have a halo. Through altostratus it is a dim, blurry disk with no halo, and shadows fade (Chapter 4, 4.4)."*

*Footer: "Never look straight at the Sun. Block it with your hand, a pole, or a building (Chapter 15)."*

## 19.4 The Tower That Swallowed a Pilot

On July 26, 1959, Lieutenant Colonel William Rankin of the US Marine Corps was flying a jet fighter over the coast of the Carolinas when his engine failed. He was at about 14,300 meters (47,000 feet), right above the top of a thunderstorm. He ejected into air at about −50°C.

He fell into the cloud. Its updrafts carried him back up, again and again. Hail struck him, rain fell so hard he thought he might drown in midair, and lightning flashed around him. His parachute opened inside the storm, and he was tossed about for some 40 minutes before he drifted down into a forest in North Carolina. He survived, frostbitten and bruised.

Rankin's ordeal showed the inside of the last rung of a ladder that every summer day can climb. The ladder below shows each rung and its field mark. Chapter 4 named these clouds (4.6) and Chapter 7 explained why they grow (7.3).

The key thing to watch is how fast a cloud climbs, and whether its top stays sharp. A sharp, bubbling, cauliflower top is still growing. When the top turns soft and fuzzy, it has frozen into ice, and the cloud has probably become a thunderstorm.

### Visual Spread 19.4: The Cumulus Ladder

*Five clouds standing side by side on one flat horizon, each taller than the last, like a row of stairs, with a small clock beside each showing how a single summer afternoon might climb from one to the next.*

- ***Cumulus humilis (fair-weather cumulus).** "Small, flat, wider than tall. Fair weather."*
- ***Cumulus mediocris.** "As tall as wide. Still harmless. Watch the next hour."*
- ***Towering cumulus (cumulus congestus).** "Much taller than wide, with hard, bubbling tops. Showers possible soon."*
- ***A cap of cloud on top (pileus).** "A smooth, silky hood over a rising tower. The tower is pushing up fast."*
- ***Cumulonimbus.** "The top turns fuzzy or spreads into an anvil. Thunder, lightning, heavy rain, maybe hail. Go inside."*
- ***Side panel, altocumulus castellanus.** "Little turrets rising from a mid-level layer in the morning. A sign that the air aloft is unstable and storms may come later."*

*Footer: "Rule of thumb: if the towers are climbing by lunch, watch the sky all afternoon."*

## 19.5 Under the Anvil

On June 20, 2006, Jane Wiggins, in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, photographed something strange. The underside of the cloud overhead looked like a rough sea seen from below: heaving, wave-like, and sharp-edged. She sent the picture to the Cloud Appreciation Society, a group founded by the British writer Gavin Pretor-Pinney. More pictures of the same thing followed. In 2009, Pretor-Pinney proposed a name, and in March 2017, the World Meteorological Organization added **asperitas**, Latin for "roughness," to the *International Cloud Atlas*. It was the first new cloud formation added since 1951.

Asperitas usually fades without a storm. But it belongs on this page because it is one of several dramatic shapes seen around the edges of storms, and most of them are less dangerous than they look.

The spread below shows a thunderstorm from the side, with each feature labeled. Chapter 7 explains how a storm's anvil, downdrafts, and hail form (7.3, 7.8, and 7.9).

### Visual Spread 19.5: A Thunderstorm From the Side

*A wide painting of a mature thunderstorm, seen from about 30 kilometers away across open country. Its tower rises from a dark base to a flat anvil that spreads far to the right, downwind. A dome bulges above the anvil's top. A gray curtain of rain falls under the center. Under the far anvil hang rounded pouches. Off to the left, streaks of rain fall from a high cloud and vanish before reaching the ground.*

- ***Anvil.** "The flat, spreading ice top. It streams downwind, so it points the way the upper winds blow."*
- ***Overshooting top.** "A dome punching above the anvil. A very strong updraft. Expect hail and severe weather."*
- ***Rain shaft.** "The gray curtain under the storm. A bright, white shaft can be hail."*
- ***Mammatus.** "Rounded pouches hanging under the anvil. Usually seen as a storm passes or departs. Spectacular, but not a tornado sign."*
- ***Asperitas.** "Wave-like underside, like a rough sea from below. Usually harmless."*
- ***Virga.** "Rain or snow evaporating before it reaches the ground. Under a storm, it can mean dry air and strong gusts below (Chapter 7, 7.8)."*

*Footer: "Lightning can strike far from the rain shaft, even under the anvil. If you can hear thunder, go inside (Chapter 7)."*

## 19.6 Shelf Cloud or Wall Cloud?

In June 1957, a tornado tore through Fargo, North Dakota. Afterward, Ted Fujita gathered photographs and films taken by people in the area, and pieced together the storm minute by minute, as he would later do for downbursts and tornadoes (Chapters 7 and 8). In those pictures he identified the lowered block of cloud beneath the storm from which the tornado came, and linked it to tornadoes. It became known as the **wall cloud**.

Fujita's method is still the best one for any observer. Watch a feature over time, and pay attention to how it moves.

The wall cloud and the **shelf cloud** are the pair most often confused, and the difference matters. A shelf cloud forms along the leading edge of a storm's cold outflow (Chapter 6, 6.11). It means strong wind is coming, but it is not where tornadoes form. A wall cloud hangs under the storm's updraft, behind the rain, and if it rotates, it can produce a tornado (Chapter 8, 8.4).

Chapter 8 shows a supercell's base with all its features (Visual Spread 8.4). The spread below sets the two side by side.

### Visual Spread 19.6: Shelf Cloud vs. Wall Cloud

*Two paintings side by side, seen from the same distance, with a dividing line between them.*

*Left, shelf cloud: a long, low, wedge-shaped cloud sweeping across the whole width of the scene, smooth on top and ragged underneath, with a dark rain curtain close behind it and dust or leaves blowing ahead of it.*

*Right, wall cloud: a single block of cloud, lowered from a dark, rain-free base, with curved arrows showing it turning. The rain curtain is off to one side, not behind it, and wisps of cloud rise into it.*

- ***Shelf: where it is.** "On the leading edge of the storm, often in front of the rain."*
- ***Wall: where it is.** "Under the storm, usually behind or beside the rain, under the updraft."*
- ***Shelf: how it moves.** "Sweeps toward you. Air under it moves out and away from the storm."*
- ***Wall: how it moves.** "Air rises into it. A dangerous one rotates steadily."*
- ***Shelf: what's coming.** "Gust front: sudden strong wind, a temperature drop, then rain."*
- ***Wall: what's coming.** "Possibly a tornado, if it rotates and the rotation tightens."*
- ***Roll cloud (inset).** "A tube of cloud rolling along by itself, detached from the storm. Gusty, but no tornado."*

*Footer: "Can't tell? Treat it as the dangerous one and get to shelter (Chapter 8)."*

## 19.7 Funnel, Scud, or Rain?

On May 25, 1907, a tornado passed near Wills Point, Texas, and a man named George Alford took photographs of it. They are among the first photographs of a tornado ever made. In the century since, cameras have become so common that thousands of tornadoes have been filmed. So have thousands of things that only looked like tornadoes.

Ragged scraps of low cloud, called **scud**, hang under many storms and drift with the wind. Some of them are shaped like funnels. A curtain of heavy rain or hail can look like a wide, dark tornado. And a real tornado can hide inside rain and be almost invisible (Chapter 8, 8.11).

The spread below sets the three hardest look-alikes side by side. The deciding mark is always motion. A funnel turns around its own center. Scud drifts, rises, or tears apart.

### Visual Spread 19.7: Look-Alikes Under the Storm

*Three pairs of paintings, each pair facing across a line.*

- ***Funnel cloud or scud?** "A funnel hangs from the cloud base and turns around its own center, smooth and steady. Scud is ragged, drifts in one direction, and changes shape quickly. A funnel turning under a rotating wall cloud is serious (Chapter 8, 8.1)."*
- ***Tornado or rain shaft?** "Rain and hail shafts fall straight or slant with the wind, are widest at the bottom, and don't turn. A tornado is attached to the cloud above, rotates, and kicks up a cloud of debris at the ground."*
- ***Debris cloud or dust?** "A swirling cloud of debris at the ground, even with no funnel above it, means a tornado is on the ground. Dust blown ahead of a gust front moves in one direction and has no rotation."*
- ***Inset: green sky.** "A storm with a green tint has a very deep, watery core. It can come with hail or a tornado, but it can't tell you which, and many tornadoes come with no green at all (Chapter 15, 15.4)."*

*Footer: "If a warning is out, don't wait to see the tornado. It may be hidden in rain or darkness (Chapter 8)."*

## 19.8 Storms at a Glance

In 1959, a hailstorm over Wokingham, in southern England, was studied in detail by the British meteorologists Keith Browning and Frank Ludlam. They showed that it was not a short-lived bubble of rising air but a single, long-lasting storm with its own organized flow of air in and out. It was the first storm identified as what we now call a **supercell** (Chapter 7, 7.6).

Since then, forecasters and storm spotters have learned to recognize a handful of storm types from the ground and on radar. Chapter 7 explained each one. The key below shows how to tell them apart at a glance, and what each one tends to bring.

### Visual Spread 19.8: Five Storm Types

*Five panels in a row, each with two views: the storm as seen from the ground, about 30 kilometers away, and the same storm on a simple radar screen, seen from above.*

- ***Single-cell storm.** "Ground: one tower with an anvil, on a calm summer day. Radar: one round blob. Brings a short burst of rain, lightning, maybe small hail. Lasts under an hour (Chapter 7, 7.3)."*
- ***Multicell cluster.** "Ground: towers of different heights, new ones growing on one side. Radar: a cluster of blobs. Brings heavy rain, lightning, gusty wind, some hail (Chapter 7, 7.4)."*
- ***Squall line.** "Ground: a long dark wall with a shelf cloud along its front. Radar: a long line of red and yellow. Brings strong wind first, then heavy rain (Chapter 7, 7.5)."*
- ***Bow echo.** "Ground: like a squall line, with a fiercer, bulging shelf. Radar: a line curved outward like an archer's bow. Brings damaging straight-line wind at the bulge (Chapter 7, 7.5)."*
- ***Supercell.** "Ground: a single, isolated storm with a sculpted, striated, or corkscrew shape, a rain-free base, and perhaps a wall cloud. Radar: a blob with a hook on one side. Brings large hail, damaging wind, and tornadoes (Chapters 7 and 8)."*
- ***Side strip: two kinds of supercell.** "A low-precipitation supercell is thin and sculpted, with little rain, and its tornado is easy to see. A high-precipitation supercell is dark and rain-filled, and its tornado may be hidden in the rain."*

## 19.9 Boundaries You Can See

Every spring, from late September into early November, people travel to the little town of Burketown, near the southern shore of the Gulf of Carpentaria in northern Australia, to see a cloud. Early in the morning, a long tube of cloud called the **Morning Glory** may roll in from the sea, as much as 1,000 kilometers (620 miles) long, often only 100 to 200 meters above the ground, moving at 10 to 20 meters per second. Glider pilots fly ahead of it to ride the rising air along its front, the way surfers ride a wave.

The Morning Glory is a rare, spectacular example of something common. When two different masses of air meet, the boundary between them often shows up in the sky and on the ground. Chapter 6 explained fronts, drylines, and outflow boundaries (6.5, 6.10, and 6.11). Chapter 2 explained the sea breeze (2.5). This key is for spotting them when they arrive.

### Visual Spread 19.9: What a Boundary Looks Like

*Five side views of a boundary sweeping across the landscape from left to right, each with a thermometer, a wind arrow, and a dew-point dial before and after.*

- ***Cold front.** "Before: warm, humid, wind from the south. At the front: a line of towering clouds, a sharp wind shift, gusts, a burst of rain. After: cooler, drier, clearer, wind from the west or northwest, pressure rising (Chapter 6, Visual Spread 6.5)."*
- ***Warm front.** "Hours ahead: cirrus, then a halo, then a lowering gray sheet. Then steady rain or snow. After: warmer and more humid (Chapter 4, Visual Spread 4.9)."*
- ***Dryline.** "Moist air on one side, desert air on the other. The dew point drops sharply as it passes. Storms may erupt along it in the afternoon (Chapter 6, 6.10)."*
- ***Sea-breeze front.** "On a sunny afternoon near a coast: a sudden cool, damp wind off the water, and a line of cumulus marching inland (Chapter 2, 2.5)."*
- ***Outflow boundary.** "Cool gusts from a storm that may be far away. Sometimes a line of dust, or a thin arc of low cloud. New storms can grow along it (Chapter 6, 6.11)."*
- ***Inset: the Morning Glory.** "A roll cloud on a boundary, seen from a glider."*

*Footer: "A boundary shows up first on your instruments: a wind shift, a temperature change, a jump in the dew point (Chapter 18)."*

## 19.10 What's Falling?

In the winter of 2012 to 2013, NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory released a free phone app called **mPING**. It asks anyone, anywhere, a simple question: what is falling where you are right now? Rain, drizzle, snow, sleet, freezing rain, hail, or a mix? The reports flow in by the thousands, and scientists compare them with what the radar thinks is falling.

Radar is good at seeing precipitation. It is not always good at telling what kind it is near the ground, especially in winter, when rain, sleet, and snow can all fall within a few kilometers of each other (Chapter 10, 10.1). People standing outside are often better at that.

The key below helps you answer mPING's question correctly. Chapter 10 explains how each winter type forms, and Chapter 7 explains hail.

### Visual Spread 19.10: The Precipitation Key

*Eight small panels, each showing a gloved hand, a car windshield, or a patch of pavement catching the precipitation, with a close-up of a single particle drawn large beside it.*

- ***Rain.** "Drops you can see splash. Puddles grow."*
- ***Drizzle.** "Fine, close-set droplets that seem to float. They mist your glasses but barely mark a puddle."*
- ***Snow.** "Soft flakes or crystals, slow and drifting."*
- ***Graupel.** "Soft, white, crumbly pellets that squash between your fingers and bounce a little. Often in showers (Chapter 10, 10.2)."*
- ***Sleet (ice pellets).** "Hard, clear little beads that bounce and rattle on a windshield (Chapter 10, 10.5)."*
- ***Freezing rain.** "Looks like rain, but coats everything it touches in clear glaze (Chapter 10, 10.6)."*
- ***Hail.** "Hard balls or lumps of ice, often with rings inside, falling from a thunderstorm in any season (Chapter 7, 7.9)."*
- ***Mixed.** "Report what you see most of, and note the rest."*

*Footer: "Sleet bounces and rattles. Graupel squashes. Freezing rain sticks."*

## 19.11 Is It Fog, Haze, Smoke, or Dust?

On Sunday afternoon, April 14, 1935, a black wall of dust rolled across the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles. People caught outdoors couldn't see their hands in front of their faces. An estimated 300,000 tons of topsoil blew off the plains that day. A reporter named Robert Geiger, writing about the storm, used a phrase that stuck: "the Dust Bowl." The day became known as **Black Sunday**.

Black Sunday was extreme, but the question it raises comes up all the time. When the air turns thick and the distance disappears, what is in it? The answer changes what you should do.

The key below sets the visibility hazards side by side. Chapter 3 explains fog (3.8), Chapter 11 explains smoke and air quality (11.11), and Chapter 10 explains blowing snow (10.11).

### Visual Spread 19.11: The Visibility Key

*Six views of the same country road and the same distant line of hills, each in a different kind of thick air.*

- ***Fog.** "Gray-white, damp, often in valleys or over water, worst near dawn. Visibility under 1 km. Lights on, slow down (Chapter 3, 3.8)."*
- ***Mist.** "Thinner, damp gray air. You can see more than 1 km."*
- ***Haze.** "Dry, pale, bluish or brownish. The hills fade, but the air isn't damp. Fine particles and pollution."*
- ***Smoke.** "Brown, gray, or orange, with a burnt smell. The Sun turns red or orange. Check the air quality (Chapter 11, 11.11)."*
- ***Blowing dust.** "Brown and moving, with a strong wind. Can drop to near zero in seconds. If you're driving, pull off the road, turn off your lights, and keep your foot off the brake (Chapter 11)."*
- ***Blowing snow.** "White, wind-driven snow, even under a blue sky. A whiteout erases the road and the horizon (Chapter 10, 10.11)."*

*Footer: "Is the air damp or dry? Is it moving? Is there a smell? Those three questions sort most cases."*

## 19.12 The One-Minute Sky Check

In the summer of 1946, at the General Electric laboratory in Schenectady, New York, a scientist named Vincent Schaefer dropped a piece of dry ice into a freezer full of supercooled cloud. Instantly, the cloud turned into a sparkle of ice crystals. On November 13 that year, he scattered dry ice from an airplane into a cloud over the Berkshire Mountains, and snow fell from it. It was the beginning of cloud seeding.

Schaefer spent the rest of his life looking at the sky. In 1981, with the photographer John Day, he published *A Field Guide to the Atmosphere*, in the same series Roger Tory Peterson had begun with birds. The circle closed: the man who made snow from a cloud wrote a guide to recognizing them.

This chapter ends with the shortest possible version of that guide, a check you can do in one minute, anywhere. Do it every time you step outside, and you'll learn the sky the way a birdwatcher learns birds: by looking often.

### Visual Spread 19.12: The One-Minute Sky Check

*A clock face divided into four 15-second quarters, each with a small picture of a person doing one step.*

- ***First 15 seconds: the ground.** "What do you feel? Wind direction and strength (Chapter 5, Visual Spread 5.8). Warm or cool, damp or dry. Any smell of rain or smoke (Chapter 17, 17.5)."*
- ***Next 15 seconds: low and overhead.** "What clouds, how much of the sky, and are they growing? (Visual Spreads 19.2 and 19.4.)"*
- ***Next 15 seconds: high and far.** "Cirrus spreading in? A halo? Towers or an anvil on the horizon? Which way is the weather coming from?"*
- ***Last 15 seconds: the trend.** "What has changed since you last looked? Is anything worth checking on radar or in the forecast? (Chapter 13, 13.11.)"*

*Footer: "Write one line in your log. Tomorrow, look again."*

# Weather Safety
## If You Can Name It, You May Be Too Close

- **Identify from shelter when you can.** You can study a shelf cloud or a wall cloud through a window just as well as from a field.
- **Thunder means go inside.** Lightning can strike from the anvil, far from the rain. Stay in for 30 minutes after the last thunder (Chapter 7).
- **Don't wait to be sure.** If you can't tell a wall cloud from a shelf cloud, or a funnel from scud, act as if it's the dangerous one (Chapter 8).
- **A warning outranks your eyes.** Tornadoes hide in rain and darkness. If a warning is issued, take shelter whether or not you can see anything (Chapter 20).
- **Never drive into dust, smoke, or water you can't see through** (Chapter 11).

The best field guide in the world is not worth one lightning strike.

# Field Exercise
## A Cloud Walk

On a day with several kinds of cloud, walk for half an hour with a notebook. Every five minutes, stop and do the one-minute sky check (Visual Spread 19.12). Name each cloud you see using the ten-cloud key, note its height using the hand test, and write whether it's growing, spreading, or fading. At the end, look back over your notes and ask what the sky was doing as a whole. Was it building toward showers, clearing, or getting ready for a front?

---

# Try It
## Watch the Clouds Move

Stand where you can see a fixed object, such as a chimney, a lamppost, or the edge of a roof. Choose one distinct piece of cloud and watch it pass the object. Note which way it moves. Do the same for a cloud at a different height. If low and high clouds move in different directions, the wind is changing with height (Chapter 5, 5.5). If the high clouds come from the west while the low ones come from the south, a front or storm may be on the way (Chapter 6).

# Field Guide Summary

A field guide doesn't explain everything. It shows you where to look. Ask four questions every time: what shape, how high, what trend, and what's in the rest of the sky.

The ten main clouds sort by shape and height. In the high sky, contrails, halos, coronas, and sundogs each have a mark that tells them apart. Heaped clouds climb a ladder from fair weather to thunderstorm, and the speed of the climb is the warning.

Around a storm, the anvil, overshooting top, mammatus, asperitas, and virga each mean something different. A shelf cloud leads a gust front. A wall cloud hangs under the updraft and can produce a tornado. A funnel turns around its own center. Scud drifts. When in doubt, treat it as dangerous.

Storm types, boundaries, precipitation, and visibility hazards all have field marks. Most can be read in a minute, if you look often enough to know what's normal.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, the sky is always writing. A field guide teaches you to read it.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Field mark:** A feature that tells one thing apart from a look-alike.
**International Cloud Atlas:** The world's official guide to cloud names, first published in 1896.
**Contrail:** A line of cloud made by an airplane's exhaust.
**Pileus:** A smooth cap of cloud over a fast-rising tower.
**Overshooting top:** A dome of cloud punching up above a thunderstorm's anvil.
**Mammatus:** Rounded pouches hanging under a storm's anvil.
**Asperitas:** A cloud base with a rough, wave-like underside, like a stormy sea seen from below.
**Shelf cloud:** A low, wedge-shaped cloud along the front edge of a storm's outflow.
**Wall cloud:** A lowered block of cloud under a storm's updraft, which can rotate and produce a tornado.
**Roll cloud:** A tube-shaped cloud rolling along by itself, detached from the storm.
**Scud:** Ragged scraps of low cloud that drift under a storm.
**Debris cloud:** A swirling cloud of dust and debris at the ground, the sign of a tornado on the ground.
**Morning Glory:** A long roll cloud that sweeps in from the Gulf of Carpentaria in northern Australia.
**Graupel:** Soft, white pellets of rimed snow that squash between the fingers.
**mPING:** A free app for reporting what kind of precipitation is falling.
