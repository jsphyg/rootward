# Chapter 12
## Weather Maps, Radar, Satellites, and Forecast Models

In 1922, an English scientist named Lewis Fry Richardson published a strange daydream.

He pictured a vast hall, shaped like a theater, with the map of the world painted on its walls and ceiling. In the seats sat 64,000 people with pencils and paper, each working out what the air over one small patch of the map would do next. In the middle, on a tall pulpit, a conductor shone a light on any section that was running ahead or falling behind.

Richardson's "forecast factory" was never built. But it is a fair picture of how a modern forecast is made. Today the 64,000 people are a supercomputer, and the numbers they work from come from everywhere: from stations on the ground, balloons in the sky, radars, satellites, ships, and airliners.

No single tool sees the whole ocean of air. A weather station sees one spot at the bottom. A balloon sees one thin line up through the depths. Radar sees rain and wind inside storms, but only near the radar. A satellite sees the whole sweep of the clouds, but mostly from the top. The forecast on your phone is what happens when all of those partial views are stitched together and run forward in time.

This chapter follows the forecast from the first measurement to the final number, and shows how to read the maps, radar, and satellite pictures along the way. Chapter 13 shows how to use them in a forecast of your own.

## 12.1 Reports From the Bottom of the Ocean

On the night of October 25, 1859, a hurricane-force storm swept across Britain. Off the coast of Wales, it drove the steam clipper *Royal Charter*, almost home from Australia, onto the rocks. Around 460 people drowned. All around the coast, more than a hundred other ships were wrecked.

Robert FitzRoy, the head of Britain's new weather office, was sure the storm could have been seen coming. The telegraph now let weather reports travel faster than the weather itself. FitzRoy collected them from stations around the coast, drew them on maps, and in February 1861 began sending out storm warnings to the ports. That summer, he started publishing daily forecasts in *The Times*. He even coined the word "forecast."

The idea hasn't changed. Today, many thousands of **surface stations** around the world measure the bottom of the ocean of air: temperature, dew point, pressure, wind, rain, and what the sky is doing. In the United States, the backbone is the airports, whose automatic stations report at least once an hour.

Airport reports go out in a compact code called a **METAR**. It looks like gibberish at first, but it is mostly the same few facts in the same order: where, when, wind, visibility, clouds, temperature and dew point, and pressure. The clouds are reported with the sky-cover words from Chapter 4: few, scattered, broken, and overcast. The lowest layer that is broken or overcast is the **ceiling**, the height pilots care about most, because below it they can't see the ground.

Over the oceans, ships and buoys fill in, and airliners report the temperature and wind as they climb and descend.

Home weather stations add detail, too, if they are set up well. Chapter 18 explains how.

## 12.2 A Balloon Through the Ocean of Air

On January 30, 1930, near Leningrad, the Russian scientist Pavel Molchanov let go of a balloon carrying a small box of instruments and a radio. As it rose, it sent back the temperature and pressure, in a simple code, all the way up. It was the first **radiosonde**, and it changed weather forecasting. For the first time, forecasters could see the whole depth of the atmosphere, not just the bottom.

The same thing happens twice a day now, at about 800 stations around the world, all at nearly the same moment. In the United States, the National Weather Service launches from 92 sites. The balloon starts out about 1.5 meters (5 feet) wide. As it climbs into thinner air, it swells to 6 to 8 meters (20 to 25 feet), and then it bursts, often more than 30 kilometers (20 miles) up, about two hours after launch.

On the way up, the radiosonde measures temperature, humidity, and pressure. Tracking where the wind carries it gives the wind at every height. The result is called a **sounding**: a slice straight up through the ocean of air.

Two days can feel exactly the same at the ground and be completely different overhead. One has a warm lid that will keep storms from forming, the cap of Chapters 6 and 7. The other has none. One has a layer of above-freezing air a kilometer up, the warm nose of Chapter 10, that will turn snow into freezing rain. The other doesn't. The sounding is how forecasters know which day is which.

Forecasters draw soundings on a chart called a **Skew-T**. It looks fierce, but reading it starts with two lines. One shows the temperature. The other shows the dew point. Where the two lines touch, the air is saturated, and there is cloud. Where they spread apart, the air is dry. Where the temperature line leans toward warmer as it climbs, there is an inversion. Chapter 7 showed how forecasters measure a storm's fuel and lid on the same chart.

## 12.3 The Weather Map

In early June 1944, the largest invasion fleet in history was waiting in the ports of southern England. The landings in Normandy were set for June 5. Everything depended on the weather, and the man responsible for it was a Scottish meteorologist, James Stagg.

On June 3, a report came in from a lighthouse at Blacksod Point, on the far west coast of Ireland. The pressure there was falling fast. On Stagg's weather maps, a storm was coming in from the Atlantic, and it would hit the beaches on the 5th. He told General Eisenhower to wait. Then he spotted something else on the maps: a short gap between that storm and the next. Eisenhower gave the order for June 6.

A **surface map** is the tool Stagg used, and it still works the same way. It is a snapshot of the bottom of the ocean of air at one moment, built from every station's report at once. It shows the highs and lows, the isobars of Chapter 5, and the fronts of Chapter 6.

Each station appears on the map as a small cluster of numbers and symbols called a **station plot**. The circle in the middle is filled in to show how much of the sky is covered. The temperature sits at the upper left, and the dew point at the lower left. A symbol between them shows any rain, snow, fog, or thunder. A line sticking out of the circle is a **wind barb**, pointing toward where the wind is coming from. Feathers on its end show the speed.

Pressure, at upper right, is written in shorthand to save space. Only the last three digits are given, with the decimal point left out. So 146 means 1014.6 hPa, and 872 means 987.2 hPa. Put a 10 or a 9 in front, whichever makes the number close to 1,000.

A dew-point map is often the most revealing of all. A sudden jump from dry to humid air marks a front, a dryline, or a sea breeze, even where the temperature barely changes.

## 12.4 Maps of the Air Aloft

In the 1920s, a Japanese scientist named Wasaburo Ooishi sent small balloons up from an observatory northeast of Tokyo and tracked them with a telescope. Over and over, he found that high above Japan, the wind blew from the west at astonishing speed, all year round. He published his results in Esperanto, and almost no one outside Japan read them.

Twenty years later, the Japanese military did. Between late 1944 and the spring of 1945, it launched about 9,000 balloons carrying bombs into that fast wind aloft. In about three days, the wind carried them across the Pacific. About 300 were found in North America. One, in the woods near Bly, Oregon, killed a woman and five children on a church picnic in May 1945.

That fast river of air was the jet stream of Chapter 5. Forecasters follow it, and all the air aloft, on **upper-air maps**.

These maps are drawn at a set pressure instead of a set height. Each one shows how high you must climb to reach that pressure. Warm air is spread out, so in warm places you climb higher. In cold places, it's lower. The lines on the map, called **height contours**, work just like isobars. Where they bend toward the pole, there is a ridge. Where they dip toward the equator, there is a trough.

Each level has its own job. The **850 hPa** map, about 1.5 kilometers (5,000 feet) up, shows warm and cold air moving in near the ground and is the first place to look for the line between rain and snow. The **700 hPa** map, about 3 kilometers (10,000 feet) up, shows moist and dry layers. The **500 hPa** map, about 5.5 kilometers (18,000 feet) up, is the forecaster's favorite. It shows the big waves of ridges and troughs that steer the storms below. The **250 hPa** map, near the height of an airliner, shows the jet stream itself.

## 12.5 Radar: Seeing Inside the Rain

On the evening of April 9, 1953, a radar technician named Don Staggs stayed late at the airport south of Champaign, Illinois, to finish some repairs. His radar was a rare one, built not for war or air traffic but for rain research. As he tested it, a strange shape appeared on the screen: a storm with a curl hanging off one side, like a fishhook. Then reports came in. A tornado had touched down at that spot. It was the first **hook echo** ever recorded, the radar mark of the rain wrapping around a supercell's spin, as Chapter 7 described.

Weather radar works like an echo in a canyon. The radar sends out a short burst of microwaves, then listens. Raindrops, snowflakes, and hailstones scatter a little of it back. The time the echo takes to return tells the radar how far away they are. The strength of the echo tells it how much is there.

That strength is called **reflectivity**, measured in units called **dBZ**. Bigger drops, and more of them, send back stronger echoes. Below about 20 dBZ is light rain or drizzle. Around 40 dBZ is a steady, heavy rain. Above 60 is almost always hail. Apps turn those numbers into colors, and every app uses its own. Read the scale, not the drama.

The United States has about 160 of these radars, a network called **NEXRAD**. Each one sweeps around at several angles, building a three-dimensional picture of the storms around it every few minutes.

Radar has one big limit. Its beam goes out straight, but the Earth curves away below it. So the farther the beam goes, the higher above the ground it is looking. About 100 kilometers (60 miles) out, the lowest beam is already about 1.5 kilometers (5,000 feet) up. At 200 kilometers, it is about 4 kilometers up, above the bottom of most storms. A tornado, shallow snow, or drizzle far from the radar can be invisible to it.

## 12.6 Seeing the Wind

On the afternoon of May 24, 1973, scientists in Norman, Oklahoma, aimed a new kind of radar at a thunderstorm near the small town of Union City. At the same time, a team from the same lab was out near the town, filming it. The radar saw a broad, spinning area high in the storm, several kilometers up. As the minutes went by, the spin tightened and crept down toward the ground. Then the tornado touched down in front of the cameras. When the tapes were analyzed months later, the scientists found the tornado's signature had shown up aloft before it reached the ground.

That radar was a **Doppler radar**. Besides the echo, it hears whether the rain is moving toward it or away from it, the way the pitch of a passing siren rises and then falls. It measures the speed of that motion, too.

Radar apps show this as a **velocity** image. Green usually means moving toward the radar, and red means moving away. There is one catch. The radar measures only motion straight toward or away from it, so you need to know where the radar is to read the picture.

The pattern forecasters look for is bright green and bright red side by side. Air on one side is rushing toward the radar, and air on the other is rushing away. That is spin. A broad pair marks a mesocyclone, the spinning updraft of Chapter 7. A tight, intense pair, packed into neighboring dots on the screen, is a **tornado vortex signature**, the mark that Union City revealed. Chapter 8 explains how forecasters use it to issue a warning.

Velocity also shows a downburst bursting outward, a gust front racing ahead of the rain, and a cold front's wind shift.

Velocity has traps. Very fast wind can wrap around into the wrong color, and a fast-moving storm tints the whole picture, so forecasters often subtract its motion to make the spin stand out.

## 12.7 What Shape Is the Rain?

A falling raindrop is not shaped like a teardrop. Small drops are round. Big ones are squashed flat by the air rushing past them, shaped more like a hamburger bun. Hailstones tumble as they fall, so to a radar, they look round. Bits of a shattered house are any shape at all.

Between 2011 and 2013, every NEXRAD radar in the United States was upgraded to use that difference. The old radars sent out their microwaves in one direction only, waving side to side. The new ones, called **dual-polarization** radars, send them out waving both side to side and up and down. By comparing the two echoes, the radar can tell how wide its targets are compared with how tall.

Three new pictures come from that comparison. **Differential reflectivity** shows shape: high for big, flattened raindrops, near zero for round drops and tumbling hail. **Correlation coefficient** shows whether everything in the beam looks alike. Pure rain or pure snow scores high. A mix of rain and hail, or melting snow, scores lower. **Specific differential phase** measures how much water the beam passes through, and it is one of the best guides to very heavy rain, the kind that floods.

The most famous use is finding tornado debris. When a tornado tears through buildings and trees, it throws pieces of them high into the storm. To the radar, that mess is a jumble of shapes, so the correlation coefficient drops sharply in one small spot, right where the velocity image shows tight spin. That **tornado debris signature** tells forecasters that a tornado is on the ground and doing damage, even at night, with no one there to see it. Its absence proves nothing, though. A weak tornado, or one in open fields, may lift too little debris to see.

Dual-polarization also helps measure rain. The radar turns its echoes into an estimate of how fast the rain is falling and then adds it up into storm totals, checked against rain gauges on the ground. That is how forecasters spot the flood-making rain of Chapter 11 as it falls.

## 12.8 Not Everything on Radar Is Rain

On summer mornings in the eastern United States, radar screens sometimes show a strange sight: rings that appear out of nowhere at dawn and spread outward like ripples from a stone dropped in a pond. They are not storms. They are birds. Purple martins and swallows, hundreds of thousands at a time, roost together overnight. At sunrise, they all leave at once, flying outward in every direction.

In central Texas, a similar thing happens at dusk. Millions of bats pour out of caves to hunt, and the radar sees them as a growing smudge over the hills.

Radar picks up anything in the air that sends back an echo. On a calm night, it fills with insects drifting on the wind. In spring and fall, it fills with migrating birds. Some of this is useful. Bugs and dust swept together where two winds meet make a thin line on the radar, so a front, a sea breeze, or a storm's gust front can appear as a faint line on a dry, clear day, as Chapter 6 described.

Some of it is noise. Near the radar, the beam bounces off hills, buildings, and towers. That is **ground clutter**, and computers filter most of it out. On cool, calm nights, a layer of warm air above cold air can bend the beam down toward the ground, so it picks up clutter far away. This false rain is called **anomalous propagation**. Mountains can block the beam altogether, leaving blind spots behind them.

And sometimes the radar sees rain that never arrives. In dry air, rain falling from a high cloud base can evaporate on the way down, the virga of Chapter 3. The radar shows rain overhead. The ground stays dry.

Dual-polarization helps sort all this out. Birds, bugs, and buildings have shapes nothing like raindrops. When something odd remains, ask whether it moves with the storms or stays still.

## 12.9 Eyes in Orbit

On April 1, 1960, a small, drum-shaped satellite named TIROS-1 sent back the first television picture of Earth's weather from space. It was a fuzzy gray image of clouds over the northeastern United States and Canada. TIROS-1 lasted just 78 days. But forecasters never wanted to be without satellites again.

Two kinds of weather satellites watch the ocean of air today.

A **geostationary satellite** orbits high above the equator, about 35,800 kilometers (22,300 miles) up. At that height, it circles once a day, exactly as fast as the Earth turns, so it seems to hang over the same spot. It can watch the same half of the planet, nonstop. The newest American ones, the GOES satellites, take a picture of the whole Americas every ten minutes and of the United States every five. They can watch a single storm area every minute, fast enough to see thunderstorms bubbling up as they form. From that far away, though, it sees the poles only at a steep slant.

A **polar-orbiting satellite** flies much lower, about 800 kilometers (500 miles) up, on a path that crosses close to both poles. It circles the Earth about fourteen times a day, and as the planet turns beneath it, it gradually sees every part of the globe. It sees much more detail, and it can measure the temperature and moisture through the depth of the atmosphere. But it passes over any one place only about twice a day.

Between them, they cover what the ground can't: the oceans, the deserts, the poles, and everything above the tops of the clouds. Satellites supply most of the observations that go into today's computer forecasts. Over the open sea, they are often the only eyes there are. That is how forecasters track hurricanes from birth, as Chapter 9 described.

## 12.10 Reading the Pictures From Space

On the night of June 24, 1982, British Airways Flight 9 was flying over Indonesia when its engines began to glow with an eerie light. Then, one by one, all four engines stopped. The captain calmly told his passengers they had a small problem. The plane had flown into a cloud of ash from the volcano Galunggung, invisible in the dark. It glided, sinking, for more than ten minutes before the crew could restart the engines, then landed safely in Jakarta.

Today, satellites watch for ash clouds around the clock, because they see far more than visible light.

A **visible** image is the simplest. It is a black-and-white photograph of sunlight reflecting off the Earth. Thick cloud and snow look bright. The sea and forests look dark. It shows fine texture, like the lumpy tops of growing thunderstorms. But it only works by day.

An **infrared** image works day and night. It measures the heat given off by the cloud tops and the ground. Since the air cools with height, high cloud tops are cold and low ones are warmer. Forecasters color in the coldest tops to make the tallest thunderstorms stand out. The colors are just a temperature key.

A **water vapor** image senses moisture high in the atmosphere, even in clear air. It shows the swirling flow of the upper air, the dry slots behind storms, and plumes of tropical moisture streaming north.

Comparing these images reveals even more. Fog and low cloud look almost the same temperature as the ground in infrared, but they glow white on visible images. Other combinations pick out smoke, dust, ash, and the hot spots of new wildfires, as in Chapter 11.

The GOES satellites carry a **lightning mapper**, too. It spots the flashes of lightning across the Americas, both inside the clouds and down to the ground. A storm whose lightning suddenly jumps is often a storm whose updraft is growing stronger, as Chapter 7 explained. Networks of sensors on the ground pinpoint the strikes as well.

## 12.11 The Forecast Factory

Richardson didn't just dream about his forecast factory. During the First World War, while serving as an ambulance driver in France, he tried to work out a forecast by himself, by hand. He used the weather reports from one morning in May 1910 and calculated how the pressure would change over the next six hours at one spot in Germany. It took him six weeks. His answer was a pressure rise of 145 hPa, about as big as the difference between a hurricane and a strong high. The real pressure had barely changed.

His method was right, but his starting numbers were too rough, and no machine was fast enough to use it. In 1950, the first electronic computer, ENIAC, made the first computer forecast, for one day ahead. It took about 24 hours to work out.

A **weather model** is Richardson's factory built inside a computer. It covers the ocean of air with a net of points, like a three-dimensional grid, with dozens of layers stacked one above another. At each point, it keeps track of the temperature, pressure, wind, and moisture. Then it uses the laws of physics to work out what each point will do in the next few minutes, passes the answers along, and repeats, step after step, days into the future.

The gap between the points is called the **grid spacing**. Global models, which cover the whole Earth, have points about 9 to 25 kilometers apart. Models that cover one country can space them about 3 kilometers apart, fine enough to draw individual thunderstorms. Anything smaller, like a single cloud or a narrow valley, has to be estimated with shortcuts.

Every forecast starts from an estimate of the atmosphere right now, called the **analysis**. Building it is called **data assimilation**. The computer blends millions of observations from stations, balloons, airliners, radars, and satellites with its own last forecast to fill the gaps.

Since 2023, a new kind of model has arrived. Instead of calculating physics, it learns from decades of past weather how the atmosphere behaves. It can match traditional models for many forecasts, far faster, but it still starts from the same analysis.

## 12.12 Chaos and the Odds

In the winter of 1961, the American meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a simple weather model on a computer at MIT. He wanted to see one run again, so he typed in the numbers from partway through. To save time, he rounded them, from six decimal places to three. Then he went for a coffee. When he came back, the new forecast had started out just like the old one, then drifted, and after a while looked nothing like it.

The rounding changed the numbers by less than one part in a thousand. Lorenz realized that in the atmosphere, tiny errors don't stay tiny. They grow. This is **chaos**, later nicknamed the butterfly effect. It doesn't mean a butterfly causes a hurricane. It means that no measurement can ever be exact enough to forecast the weather perfectly far ahead.

Forecasters answer chaos by running a model not once but many times. Each run starts from slightly different numbers, all equally believable. This set of runs is an **ensemble**, and each run is a member. If the members mostly agree, the forecast is confident. If they spread apart, with the storm going north in some and south in others, it is uncertain. Even agreement is no guarantee, though. Every member can share the same error.

That is where a forecast's percentages come from. A **probability of precipitation** of 40 percent means a 4 in 10 chance that measurable rain will fall at any one spot in the forecast area. It doesn't mean rain for 40 percent of the day, or over 40 percent of the town.

Each decade, forecasts have gained about one more day of skill. Beyond about ten days, chaos wins, and forecasts can only say whether a week will probably be warmer or wetter than normal. Chapter 14 explains those longer outlooks.

Human forecasters still matter. They spot when the models are going wrong, know their area's tricks, and explain their reasoning in a written forecast discussion. Chapter 13 shows how to use it.

# Weather Safety
## Radar Is Not a Warning

- **In dangerous weather, follow the official warning, not your own reading of the radar.** Forecasters see things a phone app can't: several radars, the storm's history, spotter reports, and what the beam misses close to the ground.
- **Keep at least two ways to get warnings,** such as phone alerts and a weather radio. Don't depend on one app.
- **Check the time on every image.** A radar picture shared online may be an hour old, and the storm has moved.
- **Take shelter first. Look at the radar later.** A dramatic radar loop is something to study from a safe place.

# Field Observation
## Match the Map to the Sky

Find a current surface map and the station plot closest to you. Read the temperature, dew point, sky cover, and wind. Then step outside. Does the sky match the circle? Is the wind coming from where the barb points?

Now find the nearest high, low, and front, and check the satellite and radar. You are seeing the same weather four ways.

# Try It
## Read a Radar Loop

On a day with showers, open a radar loop covering at least an hour. Pause it at the start. Using the direction and speed of the rain so far, mark where you think one storm will be in 30 minutes. Then let the loop run.

Straight-line guesses work well for a while, until storms grow, fade, or split. Watch for where new cells pop up. That is where the next storm is coming from.

# Chapter Summary

A forecast starts with measurement. Stations, ships, buoys, and airliners report the weather at the bottom of the ocean of air. Balloons measure its depths twice a day.

Surface maps turn those reports into a picture of highs, lows, and fronts. Upper-air maps show the ridges, troughs, and jet stream that steer the storms below.

Radar sees inside the rain. Reflectivity shows how much is falling, Doppler velocity reveals spin, and dual-polarization tells rain from hail, birds, and tornado debris. Far from the radar, the beam misses what happens near the ground.

Satellites watch the whole planet, by sunlight, by heat, and by moisture, day and night.

Weather models calculate the atmosphere's future on a grid of points, starting from the best possible picture of the present. Chaos makes tiny errors grow, so forecasters run many versions at once and turn their spread into odds.

At the bottom of the ocean of air, we can't see the whole sea at once. But with enough windows into it, we can see where the currents are heading.

## Weather-Watcher's Vocabulary

**Surface station:** A station that measures the weather near the ground.
**METAR:** A coded weather report from an airport.
**Ceiling:** The height of the lowest cloud layer covering more than half the sky.
**Radiosonde:** A balloon-borne instrument that radios back measurements as it rises.
**Sounding:** A profile of temperature, moisture, and wind straight up through the atmosphere.
**Skew-T:** A chart used to draw soundings.
**Surface map:** A map of the weather at the ground at one moment.
**Station plot:** The small cluster of numbers and symbols showing one station's weather on a map.
**Wind barb:** A map symbol showing wind direction and speed.
**Upper-air map:** A map of the atmosphere at one pressure level aloft.
**Height contour:** A line on an upper-air map joining places where a pressure level is at the same height.
**Reflectivity:** The strength of a radar echo, measured in dBZ.
**Hook echo:** A hook-shaped radar echo made by rain wrapping around a supercell's spin.
**NEXRAD:** The US network of about 160 weather radars.
**Doppler radar:** A radar that measures motion toward or away from it.
**Velocity image:** A radar picture of wind toward and away from the radar.
**Tornado vortex signature:** A tight pair of opposite winds on radar, showing intense spin.
**Dual-polarization radar:** A radar that compares echoes from two directions to see target shape.
**Correlation coefficient:** A radar measure of how alike the targets in the beam are.
**Tornado debris signature:** A radar sign of debris lofted by a tornado.
**Bright band:** A strong radar echo from melting snowflakes.
**Ground clutter:** Radar echoes from buildings, hills, and other fixed objects.
**Anomalous propagation:** False radar echoes from a beam bent toward the ground.
**Geostationary satellite:** A satellite that stays above one spot on the equator.
**Polar-orbiting satellite:** A low satellite circling over the poles, seeing the whole Earth strip by strip.
**Visible image:** A satellite picture made from reflected sunlight.
**Infrared image:** A satellite picture made from heat given off by clouds and ground.
**Water vapor image:** A satellite picture of moisture high in the atmosphere.
**Weather model:** A computer program that calculates the atmosphere's future.
**Grid spacing:** The distance between the points in a weather model.
**Analysis:** The best estimate of the atmosphere right now, where a forecast starts.
**Data assimilation:** Blending observations with a model to build the analysis.
**Chaos:** The way tiny differences in the atmosphere grow into large ones.
**Ensemble:** A set of forecasts started from slightly different conditions.
**Probability of precipitation:** The chance of measurable rain or snow at any one spot in the forecast area.
