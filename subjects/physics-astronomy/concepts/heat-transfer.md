## Three ways heat travels

| Way | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| {{conduction|conduction}} | jiggling particles bump their neighbours | a metal spoon getting hot in soup |
| {{convection|convection}} | warm fluid moves, carrying heat | warm air rising from a radiator |
| {{radiation-heat|radiation}} | electromagnetic waves, mostly infrared | feeling a campfire's warmth on your face |

*Notice: conduction moves energy, not material; convection moves the material itself; radiation needs no material at all.*

In each case, heat flows from hotter to colder, as on the [[temperature|temperature]] page.

## Conduction

Hold one end of a metal rod in a flame. Soon the other end is hot. The fast-jiggling particles at the hot end bump into their neighbours and pass the energy along.

Metals are good **conductors**, because they also have free electrons that zoom through the metal and carry energy quickly. Materials that conduct badly are **insulators**: wood, plastic, glass, and above all, still air. Wool, feathers, foam and double glazing all work by trapping air.

A metal bench feels colder than a wooden one on a cold day, though both are at the same temperature. The metal conducts heat out of your hand faster.

The rate of conduction through a slab grows with its area and the temperature difference across it, and falls with its thickness.^[n_conduction_rate]

## Convection

Heat the bottom of a pan of water. The warm water at the bottom expands, so it becomes less dense, and it rises, the way a cork rises in water. Cooler, denser water sinks to take its place. A loop of moving water forms, called a **convection current**, carrying heat around.

Convection happens only in fluids, liquids and gases, because the material has to flow. It drives sea breezes, weather, ocean currents, and the slow churning of the Earth's mantle. It is why radiators are put low in a room and freezer compartments at the top of old fridges.

## Radiation

Everything gives out electromagnetic radiation, and the hotter it is, the more it gives out, and at shorter wavelengths. A warm body glows in infrared, which we cannot see but can feel. A stove ring heated far enough glows red; the Sun's surface, at 5800 K, glows white-yellow.

Radiation needs no material, so it is the only way heat crosses empty space.

| Surface | Absorbs and gives out radiation |
|---|---|
| dark, dull | well |
| light, shiny | poorly (reflects instead) |

That is why houses in hot countries are often painted white, and why emergency blankets are shiny.

:::example
title: A vacuum flask keeps drinks hot for hours. Explain how each of its features stops one way of losing heat.

1. **Double walls with a vacuum between them.** No particles, so no conduction or convection across the gap.
   why: Both need a material.
   uses: [temperature]

2. **Silvered, shiny walls.** They reflect infrared back toward the drink, cutting radiation.
   why: Shiny surfaces give out and absorb radiation poorly.
   uses: []

3. **A plastic or cork stopper.** It is an insulator, and stops warm air rising out of the top.
   why: It cuts conduction and convection through the opening.
   uses: []

4. **All three ways are blocked as far as possible**, so the drink loses heat only very slowly, mostly through the stopper and where the walls join.
   why: No insulation is perfect.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: Why do pans often have metal bases and plastic or wooden handles?
answer: **The metal conducts heat to the food quickly; the handle is an insulator**, so it stays cool enough to hold.
:::

:::problems
q: How does heat from the Sun reach the Earth?
answer: **Radiation.**

q: Name the way heat moves along a metal spoon in hot soup.
answer: **Conduction.**

q: Why is a radiator usually placed low down on a wall?
answer: **Warm air rises from it**, setting up a convection current that circulates around the room.

q: Why does a metal bench feel colder than a wooden one at the same temperature?
answer: **Metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.**

q: Why does a woolly jumper keep you warm?
answer: **It traps still air**, which is a very poor conductor.

q: Can convection happen in a solid? Why or why not?
answer: **No.** The material has to flow, and solids cannot.

q: Why are many houses in hot, sunny countries painted white?
answer: **White, shiny surfaces reflect radiation**, so they absorb less heat from the Sun.

q: Why does a car parked in the sun get hottest if it is black?
answer: **Dark surfaces absorb radiation well.**

q: Why do birds fluff up their feathers on cold days?
answer: **To trap more air**, an insulating layer.

q: Hot air balloons rise. Which way of heat transfer is this related to, and why?
answer: **Convection.** The hot air is less dense than the air around it, so it rises.

q: Why does double glazing keep a house warm?
answer: **The gap of trapped air (or gas) between the panes conducts heat poorly.**

q: On a clear night, the ground cools faster than on a cloudy night. Why?
answer: **The ground loses heat by radiation.** Clouds absorb and send some of it back; a clear sky lets it escape into space.
:::
