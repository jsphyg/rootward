## A popular mistake

Ask why summer is hot and many people say: the Earth is closer to the Sun. It is not. The Earth's orbit is nearly a circle, and it is closest to the Sun in early **January**, in the middle of northern winter. And when it is summer in New York, it is winter in Sydney, at the same distance from the Sun.

## The tilt

The Earth's axis is tilted by 23.5° compared with its orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space (toward Polaris) all year. So as the Earth goes round the Sun:

| Month | Northern Hemisphere | Southern Hemisphere |
|---|---|---|
| June (solstice) | leans toward the Sun: summer | leans away: winter |
| September (equinox) | neither: autumn | spring |
| December (solstice) | leans away: winter | leans toward: summer |
| March (equinox) | spring | autumn |

*Notice: the two hemispheres always have opposite seasons.*

## Why leaning toward the Sun warms you

Two effects add together:

1. **Higher Sun, stronger sunlight.** When the Sun is high, its rays hit the ground nearly straight on, and the energy is concentrated. When it is low, the same beam is spread over a bigger patch of ground, like a torch shone at a slant.
2. **Longer days.** In summer the Sun is up for more hours, so there is more time to heat up and less night to cool down.

## How high is the noon Sun?

At the equinoxes, the noon Sun's height above the horizon is $90° - \text{your latitude}$. At the {{solstice|solstices}}, add or subtract the 23.5° tilt:

$$\text{noon height} = 90° - \text{latitude} \pm 23.5°$$

Some special latitudes come from the tilt: the **tropics** at 23.5° (where the Sun can be straight overhead) and the **polar circles** at 66.5° (where the Sun can stay up all day in summer, or never rise in winter).

:::example
title: Find the noon Sun's height at latitude 40° N on the June and December solstices. How much more strongly does the June sunlight heat each square metre?

1. June: $90 - 40 + 23.5 = 73.5°$.
   why: The Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, so add the tilt.
   uses: [sky-motions]

2. December: $90 - 40 - 23.5 = 26.5°$.
   why: It leans away, so subtract.
   uses: []

3. The energy on each square metre goes as the sine of the Sun's height: $\dfrac{\sin 73.5°}{\sin 26.5°} = \dfrac{0.959}{0.446} \approx 2.15$.
   why: A slanted beam spreads over a bigger area; the sine gives the fraction that hits straight on.
   uses: []

4. **73.5° in June, 26.5° in December. June sunlight is about twice as strong per square metre**, and the days are much longer too.
   why: Both effects make summer warmer.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: Find the noon Sun's height at latitude 40° N at an equinox.
answer: $90 - 40 =$ **50°**.
:::

:::problems
q: What causes the seasons?
answer: **The 23.5° tilt of the Earth's axis.**

q: When is the Earth closest to the Sun?
answer: **Early January.**

q: It is summer in Canada. What season is it in Australia?
answer: **Winter.**

q: Find the noon Sun's height at 51.5° N (London) on the June solstice.
answer: $90 - 51.5 + 23.5 =$ **62°**.

q: Find the noon Sun's height at the equator at an equinox.
answer: **90°**: straight overhead.

q: Give two reasons summer is warmer than winter.
answer: **The Sun is higher, so its light is more concentrated; and the days are longer.**

q: Where is the Arctic Circle, and why is it there?
answer: **At 66.5° N** ($90° - 23.5°$). North of it, the Sun stays up all day at the June solstice.

q: If the Earth had no tilt, would there be seasons?
answer: **Almost none.** Every place would get the same sunlight all year (with only tiny changes from the orbit's slight oval).
:::
