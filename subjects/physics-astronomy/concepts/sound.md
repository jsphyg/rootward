## Squeezes in the air

A loudspeaker cone pushes forward, squeezing the air in front of it. Then it pulls back, leaving the air a little stretched out. Pushing and pulling hundreds of times a second, it sends a train of squeezes and stretches outward. Each air molecule just jiggles back and forth, passing the push along to its neighbours.

That is {{sound|sound}}: a [[waves|longitudinal wave]] of pressure. It reaches your ear, pushes your eardrum in and out, and you hear it.

Because sound is passed from molecule to molecule, it needs a material. In the vacuum of space there is nothing to carry it, so space is silent.

## How fast sound goes

| Material | Speed of sound, about |
|---|---|
| air, 20°C | 343 m/s |
| water | 1500 m/s |
| steel | 5000 m/s |

*Notice: sound is faster in liquids and solids, where the particles are more tightly linked.*

Sound in air is about a million times slower than light. That is why you see a distant lightning flash before you hear the thunder, and see a far-off football kicked before you hear it.^[n_sound_temp]

## Pitch and loudness

| What you hear | What it is |
|---|---|
| **pitch**, high or low | the frequency |
| **loudness** | the amplitude: how hard the air is squeezed |

A high note, like a whistle, has a high frequency. A low note, like a tuba, has a low frequency. Doubling the frequency raises a note by one octave. Musicians tune to 440 Hz, the A above middle C.

People hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, and the top limit drops with age. Higher frequencies are called **ultrasound**: bats and dolphins use it, and so do hospital scanners.

Loudness is often given in **decibels** (dB). Every 10 dB more means 10 times the sound intensity: 80 dB is 100 times as intense as 60 dB.

## Echoes

Sound bounces off hard surfaces. An echo travels out and back, so for a reflecting surface at distance d:

$$d = \frac{v \times t}{2}$$

Ships, bats and medical scanners all find distances this way.

:::example
title: You shout toward a cliff and hear the echo 2 s later. How far away is the cliff?

1. The sound travels $343 \times 2 = 686$ m in 2 s.
   why: Distance = speed × time.
   uses: [waves]

2. That is the trip **there and back**.
   why: The echo has to reach the cliff and return.
   uses: []

3. One way: $\dfrac{686}{2} = 343$ m.
   why: Halve the total.
   uses: []

4. **About 343 m.**
   why: Forgetting to halve is the most common mistake in echo problems.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: Find the wavelengths of the lowest (20 Hz) and highest (20,000 Hz) sounds people hear, in air.
answer: $\dfrac{343}{20} \approx$ **17 m**. $\dfrac{343}{20{,}000} \approx$ **1.7 cm**.
:::

:::problems
q: Thunder arrives 3 s after the flash. How far away was the lightning?
answer: **About 1 km.**

q: Thunder arrives 5 s after the flash. How far away was it?
answer: $343 \times 5 \approx$ **1.7 km**.

q: You hear an echo from a wall 0.5 s after clapping. How far is the wall?
answer: $\dfrac{343 \times 0.5}{2} \approx$ **86 m**.

q: A ship sends a sound pulse down. The echo from the seabed returns after 0.4 s. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in seawater. How deep is the sea?
answer: $\dfrac{1500 \times 0.4}{2} =$ **300 m**.

q: A bat squeaks at 50,000 Hz. Find the wavelength in air.
answer: $\dfrac{343}{50{,}000} \approx$ **6.9 mm**. Short waves let bats detect small insects.

q: Someone hits a steel rail 1 km away. How long does the sound take through the rail, and through the air?
answer: Steel: $\dfrac{1000}{5000} =$ **0.2 s**. Air: $\dfrac{1000}{343} \approx$ **2.9 s**.

q: Why can't astronauts hear an explosion in space?
answer: **There is no air**, or anything else, to carry the sound wave.

q: A note's frequency is doubled. What happens to its pitch?
answer: **It rises by one octave.**

q: Two notes have the same pitch, but one is louder. What is different about the waves?
answer: **The amplitude.** The frequency is the same.

q: A noise goes from 60 dB to 90 dB. How many times more intense is it?
answer: 30 dB is three steps of 10 dB: $10 \times 10 \times 10 =$ **1000 times**.

q: Can people hear a 30,000 Hz sound?
answer: **No.** It is ultrasound, above about 20,000 Hz.

q: The note A is 440 Hz. What is the A one octave higher?
answer: **880 Hz.**
:::
