## Two kinds of eclipse

| | Solar eclipse | Lunar eclipse |
|---|---|---|
| line-up | Sun, Moon, Earth | Sun, Earth, Moon |
| phase | new moon | full moon |
| what happens | the Moon blocks the Sun | the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon |
| who can see it | only people in the Moon's small shadow | everyone on the night side of the Earth |
| how long | totality lasts at most about 7 minutes | totality can last over an hour |

## Solar eclipses

At new moon, the Moon is between the Earth and Sun. If it lines up exactly, its shadow falls on the Earth. In the darkest part of the shadow, the Sun is completely covered: a **total** eclipse. The sky goes dark, stars come out, and the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona, glows around the black Moon. Nearby, people see a **partial** eclipse, with a bite out of the Sun.

Total eclipses work because of the coincidence on the [[angular-size|angular size]] page: the Moon and Sun look almost the same size, about 0.5°. But the Moon's orbit is slightly oval. When the Moon is furthest away, it looks a little smaller than the Sun and cannot cover it, leaving a bright ring: an **annular** eclipse.

Never look at the Sun directly, even when mostly covered, without proper eclipse filters.

## Lunar eclipses

At full moon, the Moon is on the far side of the Earth. If it passes through the Earth's shadow, it darkens. It rarely goes fully black: sunlight bending through the Earth's atmosphere reaches it, reddened in the same way as at sunset. A totally eclipsed Moon glows a deep coppery red.

## Why not every month?

If the Moon's orbit lay flat in the same plane as the Earth's orbit, there would be a solar eclipse every new moon and a lunar eclipse every full moon. But it is tilted by about 5°. Most months, the new or full Moon passes a little above or below the line to the Sun, and the shadows miss. Eclipses happen only when a new or full moon falls near one of the two points where the orbits cross. That gives a few eclipses each year, at least two solar ones, though each total solar eclipse is seen only along a narrow track.

title: The Moon (3474 km across) is 363,300 km away at its closest and 405,500 km at its furthest. The Sun looks 0.53° across. Can each give a total eclipse?

1. Closest: $\dfrac{3474}{363{,}300} \approx 0.00956$ rad $\approx 0.55°$.
   why: The small-angle formula.
   uses: [angular-size]

2. Furthest: $\dfrac{3474}{405{,}500} \approx 0.00857$ rad $\approx 0.49°$.
   why: Same formula, bigger distance.
   uses: []

3. Compare with the Sun's 0.53°: 0.55° is bigger, 0.49° is smaller.
   why: To cover the Sun, the Moon must look at least as big.
   uses: [moon-phases]

4. **At its closest, the Moon can cover the Sun (total eclipse). At its furthest, it cannot, leaving a ring (annular eclipse).**
   why: The difference is only a few hundredths of a degree, but it decides the type of eclipse.

:::practice
q: At which phase of the Moon can a solar eclipse happen?
answer: **New moon.**
:::

:::problems
q: At which phase can a lunar eclipse happen?
answer: **Full moon.**

q: Why don't we get a solar eclipse every new moon?
answer: **The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5°**, so the Moon usually passes above or below the Sun.

q: Why do far more people see a given lunar eclipse than a given total solar eclipse?
answer: **A lunar eclipse is visible from the whole night side of the Earth; the Moon's dark shadow covers only a narrow track.**

q: Why does the Moon turn red in a total lunar eclipse?
answer: **Sunlight bends through the Earth's atmosphere**, which lets mostly red light through, as at sunset, and that faint red light reaches the Moon.

q: What is an annular eclipse?
answer: **A solar eclipse when the Moon is far enough away to look smaller than the Sun**, leaving a bright ring.

q: Find the Moon's angular size at 363,300 km.
answer: **About 0.55°.**

q: What can you see during a total solar eclipse that you normally cannot?
answer: **The Sun's corona**, its faint outer atmosphere, and stars in the daytime.

q: Why do lunar eclipses last much longer than total solar eclipses?
answer: **The Earth's shadow is much bigger than the Moon**, so the Moon takes a long time to cross it; the Moon's shadow on Earth is small and moves fast.
:::
