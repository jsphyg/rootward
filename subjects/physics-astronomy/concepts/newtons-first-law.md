## What everyday life suggests

Push a book across a table and let go. It stops. Stop pedalling a bike and it slows down. It seems that things need a force to keep moving.

That idea is wrong, and it took until the 1600s to see why.^[n_galileo_newton] Look at what happens as the surface gets smoother:

| Surface | What a slid puck does |
|---|---|
| carpet | stops after a few centimetres |
| wooden floor | slides a metre or two |
| ice | slides across the whole rink |
| ice with no friction at all | would slide forever at the same speed |

*Notice: the less friction, the longer it keeps going. The slowing is caused by friction, a force. Take away the force and nothing slows it.*

## The law

**Newton's first law:** if the net force on an object is zero, its velocity does not change. A still object stays still. A moving object keeps moving in a straight line at a steady speed.

The key word is **net**. A car cruising at a steady 60 km/h on a straight road has forces on it: the engine drives it forward, air and friction hold it back. They balance, so the net force is zero, and the velocity stays the same.

So a constant velocity and being at rest are the same situation as far as forces go. Both have zero net force. Both are called **equilibrium**.

## Inertia

The tendency to keep doing the same thing is called {{inertia|inertia}}. You feel it all the time:

- When a bus brakes, your body keeps moving forward, and you lurch toward the front.
- When a car turns sharply, your body keeps going straight, and you feel pushed to the outside.
- In the tablecloth trick, a fast pull leaves the dishes behind, because they stay where they are.

**Mass measures inertia.** A full shopping trolley is much harder to start and to stop than an empty one. The more {{mass|mass}} an object has, the more it resists a change in motion.

## What the law does not say

The first law does not say "no forces act". It says the forces add to zero. A skydiver falling at a steady speed has weight pulling down and air resistance pushing up, exactly balanced.

It also does not cover turning. A car going around a bend at a steady speed is changing direction, so its velocity is changing, and there **must** be a net force on it.

:::example
title: A lamp hangs still from a cable. Its weight is 30 N. What is the tension in the cable?

1. The lamp is at rest, so its velocity is not changing.
   why: The first law links unchanging velocity with zero net force.
   uses: []

2. So the net force is zero: the forces on the lamp are balanced.
   why: Zero net force is exactly the condition for staying at rest.
   uses: []

3. Free-body diagram: weight 30 N down, tension T up. For them to cancel, $T - 30 = 0$.
   why: Up is positive; adding the forces with signs must give zero.
   uses: [force]

4. **The tension is 30 N**, pointing up.
   why: If it were less, the lamp would start falling. If more, it would start rising.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A lift moves upward at a steady 2 m/s. Its weight is 8000 N. How hard does the cable pull?
answer: Steady velocity means zero net force, the same as being still. **The cable pulls with 8000 N.** It does not need extra force to keep moving up.
:::

:::problems
q: A book rests on a table. What is the net force on it?
answer: **Zero.** It is at rest and stays at rest.

q: A car drives at a steady 60 km/h on a straight, flat road. What is the net force on it?
answer: **Zero.** Its velocity is constant. The engine's push balances friction and air resistance.

q: Why do passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly?
answer: **Inertia.** Their bodies keep moving forward at the old speed while the bus slows.

q: A space probe far from any planet or star turns its engines off. What happens to its motion?
answer: **It keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed**, because no net force acts.

q: A ball rolling across grass slows down. Is there a net force on it?
answer: **Yes.** Its velocity is changing, so something must be acting: friction from the grass.

q: Which has more inertia: a 1 kg ball or a 10 kg ball?
answer: **The 10 kg ball.** More mass means more inertia.

q: A skydiver falls at a steady speed. Their weight is 700 N. How big is the air resistance?
answer: Steady velocity, so the forces balance: **700 N upward.**

q: A lamp hangs still. Its weight is 30 N. Find the cable tension.
answer: **30 N.**

q: A car goes around a bend at a steady 40 km/h. Is the net force on it zero?
answer: **No.** Its direction is changing, so its velocity is changing, and that needs a net force.

q: In the tablecloth trick, a cloth is pulled quickly from under some plates, and the plates stay put. Explain why.
answer: **Inertia: the plates tend to stay at rest.** The pull is so brief that friction cannot get them moving much.

q: A crate is pushed across a floor at a steady speed with 120 N. How big is the friction force?
answer: Steady velocity means balanced forces: **120 N, opposite to the push.**
:::
