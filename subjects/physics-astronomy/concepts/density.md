## Heavy for its size

Which is heavier, a kilogram of feathers or a kilogram of lead? Neither: both are a kilogram. But the lead fits in your palm, and the feathers fill a sack. Lead packs much more mass into each bit of space. That is {{density|density}}.

| Material | Density (kg/m³) | Mass of 1 litre |
|---|---|---|
| air | 1.2 | 1.2 g |
| wood (pine) | 500 | 0.5 kg |
| ice | 920 | 0.92 kg |
| water | 1000 | 1 kg |
| aluminium | 2700 | 2.7 kg |
| iron | 7900 | 7.9 kg |
| lead | 11,300 | 11.3 kg |
| gold | 19,300 | 19.3 kg |

*Notice: a litre of gold weighs almost 20 times as much as a litre of water.*

## The formula

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

ρ is the Greek letter rho. The SI unit is kg/m³. In the lab, g/cm³ is often handier: water is 1 g/cm³, which is 1000 kg/m³. To convert, multiply g/cm³ by 1000.

Rearranging (see [[units|units]] for keeping them straight):

$$m = \rho V \qquad V = \frac{m}{\rho}$$

**Why densities differ.** It depends on how heavy the atoms are and how tightly they pack. Gold atoms are heavy and close together. Gas molecules are far apart, so gases are about a thousand times less dense than liquids.

**Density belongs to the material.** A gold ring and a gold bar have the same density. Saw a block in half: half the mass, half the volume, same density.

## Measuring density

Weigh the object for its mass. For a box shape, multiply length × width × height for the volume. For an odd shape, lower it into water and see how much the water level rises: the rise is the object's volume.^[n_archimedes_crown]

:::example
title: A gold-coloured ring has a mass of 9.65 g. Dropped into a measuring cylinder, it raises the water level by 0.5 cm³. Is it pure gold?

1. The volume is the rise in water level: $V = 0.5$ cm³.
   why: The ring pushes aside its own volume of water.
   uses: [units]

2. $\rho = \dfrac{9.65}{0.5} = 19.3$ g/cm³.
   why: Mass divided by volume.
   uses: []

3. In kg/m³: $19.3 \times 1000 = 19{,}300$ kg/m³.
   why: Multiply g/cm³ by 1000.
   uses: []

4. **It matches gold's density, so it is very likely pure gold.** A brass fake, about 8.5 g/cm³, would have twice the volume for the same mass.
   why: Comparing with the table.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A block of wood is 0.5 m × 0.2 m × 0.1 m and has a mass of 5 kg. Find its density.
answer: $V = 0.01$ m³. $\rho = \dfrac{5}{0.01} =$ **500 kg/m³**.
:::

:::problems
q: A 2 kg block has a volume of 0.001 m³. Find its density.
answer: **2000 kg/m³.**

q: Find the mass of 2 m³ of water.
answer: $1000 \times 2 =$ **2000 kg**.

q: Find the mass of 0.5 m³ of iron.
answer: $7900 \times 0.5 =$ **3950 kg**.

q: What volume does 54 kg of aluminium take up?
answer: $\dfrac{54}{2700} =$ **0.02 m³**.

q: A rock of mass 300 g raises the water in a cylinder by 120 cm³. Find its density in g/cm³ and kg/m³.
answer: $\dfrac{300}{120} =$ **2.5 g/cm³ = 2500 kg/m³**.

q: Convert 0.92 g/cm³ to kg/m³.
answer: **920 kg/m³** (ice).

q: A classroom is 10 m × 8 m × 3 m. Find the mass of the air in it.
answer: $V = 240$ m³. $m = 1.2 \times 240 =$ **288 kg**, more than most people expect.

q: A block is cut in half. What happens to its density?
answer: **Nothing.** Half the mass and half the volume.

q: A 1 cm³ cube of lead and a 1 cm³ cube of wood. Find each mass.
answer: Lead: **11.3 g**. Wood: **0.5 g**.

q: Gas is squeezed into half its volume. What happens to its density?
answer: **It doubles.** Same mass, half the volume.

q: A 1 kg bag of sugar has a density of about 1600 kg/m³. What is its volume, in litres?
answer: $\dfrac{1}{1600} = 0.000625$ m³ $=$ **0.625 litres**.

q: Why does ice float on water?
answer: **Ice is less dense than water** (920 against 1000 kg/m³). The buoyancy page explains why that makes it float.
:::
