## Stellar nurseries

The space between the stars is not empty. It holds thin gas, mostly hydrogen, and specks of dust. In places this gathers into **giant molecular clouds**, thousands of times the mass of the Sun, so cold (10 to 20 K) that hydrogen atoms pair up into molecules. The dust makes them dark: they blot out the stars behind them.

These clouds are where stars are born. The Orion Nebula, visible to the eye as a fuzzy patch in Orion's sword, is one lit up by young stars that have just formed inside it.

## Gravity against pressure

Every clump of gas feels two things, just as in [[the-sun|the Sun]]: gravity pulling inward and gas pressure pushing out. In a hot, thin cloud, pressure wins and the clump spreads out. In a cold, dense clump, gravity wins and it starts to fall in on itself.

A nudge often starts it: a nearby exploding star, or two clouds colliding, squeezes the gas enough to tip the balance.

## From collapse to ignition

| Stage | What happens | Time for a Sun-like star |
|---|---|---|
| collapse | the clump falls inward, spinning faster and flattening into a disc | about 100,000 years |
| protostar | a hot ball at the centre glows, heated only by its own shrinking | a few tens of millions of years |
| ignition | the core reaches about 10 million K and hydrogen fusion starts | |
| main sequence | fusion's pressure stops the collapse; the star is stable | billions of years |

As gas falls in, it loses gravitational [[potential-energy|potential energy]], which becomes heat. That is why a {{protostar|protostar}} shines before it has any fusion. The leftover disc around it is where planets can form, as in the [[solar-system-formation|birth of the solar system]].

On the [[hr-diagram|H–R diagram]], a protostar starts cool and large, at the right, and moves toward the main sequence as it shrinks and heats. **Where it lands depends on its mass**: heavy stars land at the hot, bright top left, light ones at the cool, dim bottom right. Heavy stars also form much faster.

## Too small to shine

The smaller the clump, the less its gravity squeezes the core. Below about **0.08 solar masses** (about 80 Jupiters), the core never reaches the temperature for hydrogen fusion. The result is a {{brown-dwarf|brown dwarf}}: a failed star, glowing dimly as it slowly cools.

At the other end, stars much over about 100 solar masses are rare: they shine so fiercely that their light blows away gas before more can fall on.

## Born in crowds

A single molecular cloud makes hundreds or thousands of stars at once, as a **star cluster**. Most clusters drift apart over hundreds of millions of years. The Sun was almost certainly born in one, its siblings now scattered around the galaxy.

:::practice
q: A collapsing cloud core shrinks to a tenth of its radius. By [[angular-momentum|conservation of angular momentum]], how much faster does it spin?
answer: Spin goes as $\frac{1}{R^2}$, so **100 times faster**.
:::

:::problems
q: Why must a cloud be cold and dense before it collapses?
answer: **Its gas pressure must be low enough for gravity to win.**

q: What heats a protostar before fusion starts?
answer: **Its own shrinking**: falling gas turns gravitational energy into heat.

q: At what core temperature does hydrogen fusion begin?
answer: **About 10 million K.**

q: What stops a new star from collapsing further?
answer: **The pressure from fusion's heat** balances gravity.

q: A cloud core shrinks to a hundredth of its radius. How much faster does it spin?
answer: **10,000 times.**

q: Why do collapsing clouds form discs?
answer: **They spin faster as they shrink**, and the spin flattens them, like pizza dough thrown in the air.

q: A gas ball has 0.05 solar masses. What becomes of it?
answer: **A brown dwarf**: under 0.08 solar masses, the core never gets hot enough for hydrogen fusion.

q: How many Jupiter masses is 0.08 solar masses? (The Sun is 1047 Jupiters.)
answer: $0.08 \times 1047 \approx$ **84 Jupiters**.

q: Where on the H–R diagram does a new 10 solar mass star join the main sequence?
answer: **At the top left**: hot, blue and very luminous.

q: Shrinking could power the Sun for about 30 million years. Why can't that be its main power source?
answer: **The Sun is 4.6 billion years old**, over 100 times longer.

q: Why is the Orion Nebula glowing?
answer: **Young, hot stars just formed inside it** light up the gas around them.
:::
