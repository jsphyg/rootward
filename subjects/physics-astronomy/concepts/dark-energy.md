## Expected: slowing down

After the [[big-bang|Big Bang]], everything is flying apart, but every bit of matter pulls on every other. So astronomers expected the expansion to be **slowing down**, like a ball thrown upward. The big question in the 1990s was only: how fast is it slowing? Enough to stop and fall back one day, or not?

## Found: speeding up

To find out, two teams, led by Saul Perlmutter, and by Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess, measured exploding white dwarfs, the [[stellar-remnants|supernovae]] that all reach about the same peak luminosity. These are [[brightness-distance|standard candles]] bright enough to see billions of light-years away. Comparing each one's distance with its redshift shows how the expansion rate has changed over time.

In 1998, both teams found the same surprise. The distant supernovae were about 25% **fainter** than expected. Fainter means further away. The universe had stretched more since their light left than a slowing universe could manage. The expansion is **accelerating**. The three leaders won the Nobel Prize in 2011.

## What could push?

Something is working against gravity on the largest scales. It is called {{dark-energy|dark energy}}.

The simplest idea is one Einstein had first, in 1917, called the **cosmological constant**: an energy that belongs to empty space itself. Each cubic metre of space has the same amount, always. Strange as it sounds, in general relativity such energy pushes space apart.

This explains the timing:

| As the universe expands | Matter (ordinary and dark) | Dark energy |
|---|---|---|
| amount per cubic metre | falls as the volume grows | stays the same |
| importance | dominated the past | dominates now and the future |

Early on, matter was packed tightly and its gravity slowed the expansion. As matter thinned out, the steady dark energy took over, a few billion years ago, and the expansion began to speed up.

## What the universe is made of

| Ingredient | Share of the total |
|---|---|
| dark energy | about 68% |
| [[dark-matter|dark matter]] | about 27% |
| ordinary matter: every star, planet, gas cloud and person | about 5% |

*Notice: everything ever seen through a telescope is in that last 5%. The other 95% is known only through its effects.*

## The far future

If dark energy stays constant, the acceleration goes on forever. Galaxies beyond our own group will recede faster and faster, their light stretched beyond detection. In about a hundred billion years, astronomers in the merged Milky Way and Andromeda would see only their own galaxy, with no sign of the expanding universe. Stars will eventually burn out, and the universe will grow cold and dark.

But no one knows what dark energy really is, or whether it stays constant. It is perhaps the biggest open question in physics.

:::example
title: A supernova is 0.25 magnitudes fainter than expected. How much further away is it than expected?

1. 0.25 magnitudes fainter means dimmer by a factor of $10^{0.25 \times 0.4} = 10^{0.1} \approx 1.26$.
   why: One magnitude is a factor of $10^{0.4} \approx 2.512$ in brightness.
   uses: [magnitudes]

2. Brightness goes as $\dfrac{1}{d^2}$, so the distance is larger by $\sqrt{1.26}$.
   why: The inverse-square law.
   uses: [brightness-distance]

3. $\sqrt{1.26} \approx 1.12$.
   why: Take the square root.
   uses: []

4. **About 12% further away than expected**: the sort of gap that revealed the acceleration.
   why: A factor of 1.12.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A supernova looks 0.5 magnitudes fainter than expected. How much further away is it?
answer: Dimmer by $10^{0.2} \approx 1.58$, so further by $\sqrt{1.58} \approx$ **1.26, about 26%**.
:::

:::problems
q: Before 1998, what did astronomers expect the expansion to be doing?
answer: **Slowing down**, because of gravity.

q: Why were exploding white dwarfs used to measure the expansion?
answer: **They all reach nearly the same luminosity**, so they are standard candles, and are bright enough to see far away.

q: A supernova looks a quarter as bright as expected. How much further away is it?
answer: **Twice as far.**

q: What are the three ingredients of the universe and their shares?
answer: **Dark energy about 68%, dark matter about 27%, ordinary matter about 5%.**

q: The universe doubles in size. What happens to the amount of matter per cubic metre, and to dark energy per cubic metre?
answer: **Matter falls to an eighth**, as the volume grows 8 times; **dark energy stays the same**.

q: Today matter is 31% and dark energy 69% of the total. When the universe was half its size, which was bigger?
answer: **Matter**: 8 times denser, $0.31 \times 8 = 2.48$ against 0.69.

q: Why did dark energy only take over in the last few billion years?
answer: **Matter thinned out as space grew**, while dark energy per cubic metre stayed the same.

q: What is the cosmological constant?
answer: **An energy belonging to empty space itself**, the same in every cubic metre, which pushes the expansion faster.

q: If the acceleration continues, what will future astronomers in our galaxy see?
answer: **Only their own galaxy**: all others will have receded out of sight.

q: How much of the universe has ever been seen directly?
answer: **About 5%**: ordinary matter. The rest is dark matter and dark energy.
:::
