## Charge on the move

On the [[electric-charge|electric charge]] page, charge sat still. When charge flows, that flow is an electric {{current|current}}. In a metal wire, the free electrons drift along it. In salty water or a battery's paste, charged atoms (ions) move.

## Measuring current

Current is how much charge passes a point each second:

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$

The unit is the {{ampere|ampere}}, or amp, A: one coulomb per second. So 1 A means about $6 \times 10^{18}$ electrons passing every second.

| Thing | Current, about |
|---|---|
| a phone, charging | 1 to 3 A |
| a lamp on 230 V mains | 0.25 A |
| a kettle | 10 A |
| a car's starter motor | 100 to 200 A |
| a lightning bolt | 30,000 A, for a split second |

Current is measured with an **ammeter**, placed in the path of the current so that all of it flows through.

## A complete loop

Current only flows if there is a complete path, a {{circuit|circuit}}, from one side of the battery round to the other. Break the loop anywhere, with a switch or a cut wire, and the current stops everywhere at once.

In a single loop, the current is the **same at every point**: charge does not pile up or get used up. What the lamp uses is energy, not charge.

## Fast push, slow drift

Flip a switch and the light comes on instantly, but the electrons themselves drift along the wire at only about a millimetre per second or less. How?

The wire is already full of free electrons. When the switch closes, the push travels through all of them almost at the speed of light, like a long tube already full of marbles: push one in at one end, and one pops out at the other end at once.^[n_drift]

## Which way?

Long before electrons were discovered, scientists decided current flows from the positive terminal to the negative one. We now know that in wires, the electrons actually move the other way, from − to +. The old **conventional current** direction is still used on circuit diagrams. Either way, the physics works out the same.

:::example
title: A lamp carries 0.5 A for 1 minute. How much charge passes through it, and how many electrons is that?

1. Time in seconds: $t = 60$ s.
   why: Amps are coulombs per second.
   uses: []

2. Charge: $Q = It = 0.5 \times 60 = 30$ C.
   why: Rearranging $I = \dfrac{Q}{t}$.
   uses: [electric-charge]

3. Electrons: $\dfrac{30}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 1.875 \times 10^{20}$.
   why: Each electron carries $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
   uses: []

4. **30 C, about $1.9 \times 10^{20}$ electrons**, in just one minute.
   why: A large number, because each electron's charge is so tiny.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A current of 2 A flows for 5 s. How much charge passes?
answer: $Q = 2 \times 5 =$ **10 C**.
:::

:::problems
q: 6 C passes in 3 s. Find the current.
answer: **2 A.**

q: 120 C passes in one minute. Find the current.
answer: $\dfrac{120}{60} =$ **2 A**.

q: A 3 A current flows for 10 minutes. Find the charge.
answer: $3 \times 600 =$ **1800 C**.

q: How long does it take 50 C to pass with a current of 0.2 A?
answer: $\dfrac{50}{0.2} =$ **250 s**.

q: A 0.5 A current flows for 1 minute. How many electrons pass?
answer: **About $1.9 \times 10^{20}$.**

q: How many electrons pass a point each second in a 1 A current?
answer: $\dfrac{1}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} =$ **$6.25 \times 10^{18}$**.

q: A phone battery is rated 3000 mAh: it can give 3000 mA for one hour. How much charge is that?
answer: $3 \times 3600 =$ **10,800 C**.

q: In a single loop with a battery and a lamp, the current is 0.3 A just before the lamp. What is it just after?
answer: **0.3 A.** Current is the same all round a single loop.

q: Does a lamp "use up" current?
answer: **No.** It uses energy; the same charge flows in and out.

q: Why does a lamp light at once when you flip the switch, if electrons drift so slowly?
answer: **The wire is already full of electrons**, and the push travels through all of them almost instantly.

q: Which way do electrons move in a wire, compared with conventional current?
answer: **The opposite way**: electrons go from − to +.

q: A lightning bolt carries 30,000 A for 0.001 s. How much charge does it carry?
answer: $30{,}000 \times 0.001 =$ **30 C**.
:::
