## The balloon trick

Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. Your hair stands up and reaches toward it. Pull off a woolly jumper in the dark and you may see tiny sparks. All of this is **static electricity**: electric charge that has built up in one place.

| Try | What happens |
|---|---|
| two balloons, both rubbed on hair | they push each other apart |
| a rubbed balloon and the hair it was rubbed on | they pull together |
| a rubbed balloon and small bits of paper | the paper jumps up to it |

*Notice: sometimes there is a push, sometimes a pull. There must be two kinds of charge.*

## Two kinds of charge

{{charge|Electric charge}} comes in two kinds, called positive and negative.

- **Like charges repel**: positive pushes positive away, negative pushes negative away.
- **Opposite charges attract**: positive and negative pull together.

These are real [[force|forces]], acting across a gap without touching, like gravity. But unlike gravity, which only pulls, electric forces can push or pull.

## Where charge comes from

Every atom has a tiny nucleus of positive protons (and neutral neutrons), surrounded by negative electrons. Normally there are equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel and the atom is neutral.

Rubbing can knock electrons off one material onto another. The balloon steals electrons from your hair, so the balloon ends up negative and your hair positive. Protons stay locked in the nuclei; it is always electrons that move.

## Charge comes in packets

Charge is measured in {{coulomb|coulombs}}, C. Every proton carries exactly $+e$ and every electron exactly $-e$, where

$$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$$

Every charge in nature is a whole number of these packets. A coulomb is a huge amount of charge: over six million million million electrons. A balloon's charge is usually under a millionth of a coulomb.

**Charge is conserved.** It cannot be made or destroyed, only moved. When the balloon gains $-1$ µC, your hair loses exactly the same amount of negative charge, and ends up at $+1$ µC.

## Conductors and insulators

In metals, some electrons are free to wander through the whole piece. These are {{conductor|conductors}}: charge flows through them easily. In plastic, rubber, glass and dry air, electrons are held tightly. These are **insulators**: charge stays where it is put. That is why a rubbed balloon (rubber) keeps its charge, but a rubbed metal spoon would lose it through your hand.

**Why the wall attracts the balloon.** The negative balloon pushes the wall's electrons slightly away, leaving the near surface a little positive. The nearby positive charge is pulled more strongly than the further-away negative charge is pushed, so there is a net attraction.

:::example
title: A plastic rod is rubbed and ends up with a charge of −1 µC. How many extra electrons has it gained?

1. Convert: $-1$ µC $= -1 \times 10^{-6}$ C.
   why: Micro means a millionth.
   uses: []

2. Each electron is $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C, so the number is $\dfrac{1 \times 10^{-6}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$.
   why: Total charge divided by the charge of one electron.
   uses: [force]

3. $\dfrac{1}{1.6} \times 10^{-6 + 19} = 0.625 \times 10^{13} = 6.25 \times 10^{12}$.
   why: Dividing powers of ten: subtract.
   uses: []

4. **About $6.25 \times 10^{12}$ extra electrons**, over six million million, yet the rod's charge is tiny by everyday standards.
   why: Shows how small e is.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A sphere has lost $5 \times 10^{10}$ electrons. What is its charge?
answer: Losing electrons leaves it positive: $5 \times 10^{10} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} =$ **$+8 \times 10^{-9}$ C**.
:::

:::problems
q: A balloon gains $1 \times 10^{12}$ electrons. Find its charge.
answer: **$-1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ C.**

q: How many electrons make up −1 C?
answer: $\dfrac{1}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} =$ **$6.25 \times 10^{18}$**.

q: A rod has a charge of +3.2 nC ($3.2 \times 10^{-9}$ C). How many electrons has it lost?
answer: $\dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-9}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} =$ **$2 \times 10^{10}$**.

q: Two rubbed balloons are hung side by side. What do they do, and why?
answer: **They push apart.** Both have the same kind of charge.

q: A cloth rubs a rod, and the rod becomes −2 µC. What is the cloth's charge?
answer: **+2 µC.** Charge is conserved.

q: Can an object have a charge of $0.5e$?
answer: **No.** Charge comes in whole numbers of e.

q: When an object becomes positively charged, has it gained protons or lost electrons?
answer: **Lost electrons.** Protons stay in the nuclei.

q: Why does a charged balloon keep its charge, but a charged metal spoon held in your hand does not?
answer: **The balloon is an insulator.** The spoon is a conductor, so the charge flows away through you.

q: Name two conductors and two insulators.
answer: Conductors: **copper, aluminium** (any metal). Insulators: **plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood.**

q: A neutral atom has 6 protons. How many electrons does it have?
answer: **6.**

q: That atom gains one electron. What is its charge?
answer: **$-e$, or $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.**

q: Why are you more likely to get a static shock on a dry winter day than on a humid summer day?
answer: **Damp air conducts a little**, letting charge leak away before it builds up. Dry air is a better insulator.
:::
