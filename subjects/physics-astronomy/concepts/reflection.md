## Bouncing light

Throw a ball at a wall without spin and it bounces off at the same angle it came in. Light does the same off a mirror.

Draw a line at right angles to the mirror where the ray hits. This line is called the {{normal|normal}}. Measure the angles from it:

$$\text{angle of incidence} = \text{angle of reflection}$$

| Ray arrives at (from the normal) | Ray leaves at (from the normal) |
|---|---|
| 0° (straight on) | 0° (straight back) |
| 30° | 30° |
| 60° | 60° |

*Notice: the incoming ray, the outgoing ray and the normal all lie in one flat plane, with the normal in the middle.*

## Smooth and rough surfaces

A mirror is very smooth, so all the rays from one direction bounce off together in one direction. This is **specular** reflection, and it makes clear images.

Paper, walls and skin are rough on a tiny scale. Each tiny patch obeys the law, but the patches face every which way, so light scatters in all directions. This is **diffuse** reflection. It is how we see most objects: they scatter light into our eyes from every angle.

## Plane mirrors

Look into a flat mirror and you see yourself "behind" the glass. Rays from you bounce off the mirror into your eyes, and your brain traces them back in straight lines to a point behind the mirror. The image is:

- as far behind the mirror as you are in front,
- the same size as you,
- **virtual**: no light actually comes from behind the mirror, so you cannot catch the image on a screen,
- flipped front to back, which is why your left hand appears as the image's right hand.

## Curved mirrors

A **concave** mirror curves inward, like the inside of a spoon. Rays coming in parallel to its axis all reflect through one point, the {{focal-point|focal point}}. For a gently curved mirror, the focal length is half the radius of the curve:

$$f = \frac{R}{2}$$

Concave mirrors gather light. Large telescopes use them, because a big mirror can collect far more light than a lens of the same size, and can be supported from behind. Torches and car headlamps use them the other way round: a bulb at the focal point sends out a parallel beam.

A **convex** mirror bulges outward, like the back of a spoon. It spreads rays out and gives a small, wide view. Car wing mirrors and shop security mirrors use them.

:::example
title: You stand 1.5 m in front of a plane mirror. Where is your image, and how far is it from you? You walk toward the mirror at 1 m/s. How fast does your image approach you?

1. The image is as far behind the mirror as you are in front: 1.5 m behind.
   why: A plane mirror's image is the same distance behind as the object is in front.
   uses: [em-waves]

2. Distance from you to your image: $1.5 + 1.5 = 3$ m.
   why: You are 1.5 m in front, the image 1.5 m behind.
   uses: []

3. Each second you get 1 m closer to the mirror, and your image also gets 1 m closer to the mirror from the other side.
   why: The image always stays the same distance behind as you are in front.
   uses: []

4. **The image is 1.5 m behind the mirror, 3 m from you, and it approaches you at 2 m/s.**
   why: The gap closes from both sides at once.
   uses: []
:::

:::practice
q: A ray hits a mirror at 40° to the normal. At what angle does it reflect, and what angle does it make with the mirror's surface?
answer: **40° to the normal**, which is **50° to the surface**.
:::

:::problems
q: A ray hits a mirror at 30° to the surface. Find the angle of reflection from the normal.
answer: **60°.**

q: A ray hits a mirror straight on, along the normal. Which way does it reflect?
answer: **Straight back** along the same line.

q: You stand 2 m in front of a plane mirror. How far are you from your image?
answer: **4 m.**

q: Is a plane mirror's image real or virtual? Why?
answer: **Virtual.** No light actually comes from behind the mirror.

q: A concave mirror has a radius of curvature of 40 cm. Find its focal length.
answer: **20 cm.**

q: A torch uses a concave mirror with a focal length of 2 cm. Where should the bulb go for a parallel beam?
answer: **At the focal point, 2 cm from the mirror.**

q: Why do big telescopes use mirrors rather than lenses?
answer: **A mirror can be made very large and supported from behind**, and it gathers far more light.

q: Why can you see a sheet of paper from any angle, but not use it as a mirror?
answer: **Its surface is rough, so it scatters light in all directions** (diffuse reflection) instead of keeping the rays together.

q: Why does the word AMBULANCE appear back to front on the front of ambulances?
answer: **A driver ahead sees it in the rear-view mirror**, which flips it the right way round.

q: Why are convex mirrors used in shops and on cars?
answer: **They show a wide view**, though the images are smaller.

q: Two plane mirrors are set at right angles. A ray hits one, then the other. How does the final ray compare with the first?
answer: **It goes back parallel to the way it came**, in the opposite direction. Corner reflectors left on the Moon work this way.

q: How tall must a plane mirror be for you to see your whole body in it?
answer: **Half your height.** Rays from your feet reflect from a point halfway between your eyes and feet.
:::
